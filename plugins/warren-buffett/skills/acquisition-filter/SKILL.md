# The Berkshire Acquisition Filter

Derived from *Berkshire Hathaway Shareholder Letters, 1977-2024* by Warren E. Buffett (1989 acquisition criteria and 2014 fiftieth-anniversary letter).

## Core Principle

A transaction should work because the target is understandable, durable, well managed, and sensibly priced, not because leverage, synergy language, or deal momentum makes it appear so.

## Framework

### Step 1: Write the owner case

Explain how the target earns cash, why customers stay, what capital it needs, and why its economics should endure.

### Step 2: Assess management and autonomy

Determine whether the current managers are able, trustworthy, motivated to remain, and compatible with the intended level of control.

### Step 3: Value without synergies

Estimate conservative stand-alone owner earnings and value before adding integration or cross-selling claims.

### Step 4: Audit transaction incentives

List how executives, bankers, consultants, and sellers benefit if the transaction closes and who is paid if it fails.

### Step 5: Test financing and opportunity cost

Compare cash, debt, and equity consideration, including dilution, fragility, and the best alternative use of each resource.

### Step 6: Set the walk-away line

Define maximum price, unacceptable terms, diligence failures, and the person empowered to stop the process.

## Anti-patterns

- Starting with the desired closing date and back-solving the economics.
- Paying for synergies before they are achieved.
- Using cheap debt or inflated equity to excuse a weak business.
- Letting advisors whose fee depends on closing frame the final decision.

## Output shape

Return a pass or proceed scorecard covering business, moat, management, stand-alone value, incentives, financing, opportunity cost, walk-away conditions, and final verdict.
