# Hell Yeah or No

Derived from *Hell Yeah or No* by Derek Sivers, the title essay, originally published August 26, 2009, expanded into the 2020 book.

## Core Principle

Most overcommitment does not come from bad opportunities, it comes from good enough ones said yes to out of fear: fear of missing out, fear of disappointing someone, fear of closing a door. Sivers' filter removes the middle ground entirely. If the honest, gut level answer to an opportunity is not hell yeah, the answer is no. There is no polite maybe category. A calendar full of medium yeses is what a life without direction actually looks like from the inside.

## Framework

### Step 1: Name the decision plainly

Write down exactly what you are being asked to say yes or no to, stripped of the surrounding justification for why you should probably do it.

### Step 2: Ask the gut question first, before the reasons

Before listing pros and cons, ask: is this a hell yeah. Notice the honest, immediate answer before you start building a case for why you should do it anyway.

### Step 3: Separate a real hell yeah from relief or flattery

A hell yeah feels like genuine excitement about the thing itself, not relief that you avoided an awkward no, and not flattery at being asked. Check which one you are actually feeling.

### Step 4: If it is not a hell yeah, decline plainly

Say no without over explaining. A long justification for a no is usually a sign you are still negotiating with yourself about whether to change it to a yes.

### Step 5: Protect the yeses you have already said

Every hell yeah you already committed to needs its portion of your time protected. A new maybe that gets promoted to yes usually steals time from an existing hell yeah rather than from nowhere.

## Anti-patterns

- Saying yes because declining would be awkward, rather than because you actually want to.
- Treating a flattering request as automatically a hell yeah.
- Writing a long justification for a no, which is usually second guessing in disguise.
- Letting a new maybe crowd out time already promised to an existing hell yeah.
- Applying the filter only to big decisions and letting small obligations pile up unfiltered.

## Output shape

A short answer to the specific decision in front of you: what is actually being asked, the honest gut reaction before reasons were added, whether that reaction is a real hell yeah or something else wearing its costume, and a plain yes or no with no extended justification required.
