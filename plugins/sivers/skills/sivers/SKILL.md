# The Sivers Filter

Derived from Derek Sivers, across *Anything You Want*, *Hell Yeah or No*, *How to Live*, and *Useful Not True*, a recurring pattern across all five of his short books.

## Core Principle

Sivers built a company by accident, sold it for 22 million dollars, gave the money away, and spent the years since writing very short books, each under 135 pages, each built around one compressed test. Across five books the same move repeats: strip a decision down to a blunt, almost uncomfortable question, and trust the short answer over the long justification. He does not trust his own certainty either. He is explicit that everybody's ideas seem obvious to them, which is exactly why a person is a bad judge of their own work, their own excitement, and their own excuses.

## Framework

### Step 1: State the decision in one sentence

Before applying any test, write the actual decision in one plain sentence. If it takes a paragraph to state, it has not been thought through enough to filter yet.

### Step 2: Run the hell yeah test

Ask honestly: is this a hell yeah, or is it a maybe. If it is not a hell yeah, the answer is no. Do not let a maybe get rounded up to a yes out of guilt, fear of missing out, or fear of disappointing someone.

### Step 3: Check whether it is actually being executed, not just held

An idea that is being protected, rated, or discussed instead of built is worth nothing yet. Ask what the smallest real step is that would count as execution starting today, not someday.

### Step 4: Check whether you are the right judge of it

Because your own ideas feel obvious to you, do not trust your own flat certainty that something is or is not good. If you are dismissing something as too obvious to matter, or too good to fail, get an outside test before deciding either way.

### Step 5: Ask whether it is useful, not whether it is true

If the decision hinges on a belief, do not only ask whether the belief is objectively correct. Ask whether holding it right now is useful, whether it helps you act, and let that carry real weight alongside the factual question.

### Step 6: Let two answers both be true if they are

If the honest answer is genuinely split, for example wanting independence and wanting commitment at once, do not force a single rule. State both fully, and choose which one governs this specific decision rather than pretending one cancels the other everywhere.

## Anti-patterns

- Rounding a maybe up to a yes because saying no feels rude or risky.
- Rating or defending an idea instead of executing the smallest real version of it.
- Trusting your own certainty about your own work without any outside test.
- Treating every belief as a factual claim that must be proven, instead of asking whether it is useful right now.
- Forcing a single rule onto a genuinely contradictory situation instead of letting both sides be true.

## Output shape

A short verdict on the decision in front of you: the one sentence statement of it, the hell yeah or no answer, whether it is currently being executed or only held, an outside check on whether you can actually judge it fairly, and if a belief is involved, whether holding it is useful right now regardless of whether it is provably true.
