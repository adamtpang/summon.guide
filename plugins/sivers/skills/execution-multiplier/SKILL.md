# Ideas Are Just a Multiplier of Execution

Derived from *Anything You Want* by Derek Sivers, the CD Baby origin story, built to sell his own CD before anyone else asked.

## Core Principle

Sivers built a webpage to sell his own CD, and it grew into the largest independent music retailer online because he kept solving the next problem directly in front of him rather than executing a plan someone else handed him. His claim: an idea is only a multiplier of execution, not a substitute for it. A brilliant idea with weak execution produces less than a mediocre idea with strong execution, and a brilliant idea with no execution at all produces nothing.

## Framework

### Step 1: Find the problem you already have, not one you invented

The strongest starting point is a real problem you are personally experiencing right now, the way Sivers needed to sell his own CD before CD Baby existed. Check whether you are solving something real or manufacturing a problem to justify an idea you already like.

### Step 2: Rate the execution, not the idea

Stop asking whether the idea is good enough. Ask what the current level of execution actually is: has anything shipped, has anyone used it, does it work today.

### Step 3: Take the smallest next real step

Identify the next concrete action that would move execution forward this week, not the final version of the plan. A webpage that sells one CD is a real step. A business plan for selling all music is not.

### Step 4: Let the next problem, not the original plan, decide what comes next

Once the first version exists, notice what breaks or what people actually ask for next, and build that, rather than sticking rigidly to an original plan that predates any real feedback.

### Step 5: Multiply, do not start over

If execution is already underway, look for what would multiply the existing effort, more reach, more reliability, more speed, rather than switching to a new idea because the current one feels less exciting than it did at the start.

## Anti-patterns

- Spending time rating, defending, or perfecting an idea that has not been executed at all yet.
- Inventing a problem to solve instead of building from a real one you already have.
- Treating the original plan as fixed instead of following what the next real problem reveals.
- Abandoning a partially executed idea for a fresher, unexecuted one because starting feels more exciting than continuing.

## Output shape

A short assessment of the idea in front of you: what real problem it is actually solving, the current honest level of execution, the smallest next concrete step, and whether the right move is to keep building the current thing or that the idea itself was never actually the bottleneck.
