# Money Follows Service

Derived from *Founders Podcast* by David Senra, from Rockefeller's Autobiography, and the interview episodes with Daniel Ek (Spotify) and Todd Graves (Raising Cane's), each independently citing Henry Ford's line that money comes as a result of service.

## Core Principle

Senra has found the same borrowed maxim sitting underneath founders in completely unrelated industries and centuries: money comes naturally as a result of service. Rockefeller wrote it into his own account of how Standard Oil grew. Daniel Ek applied it building a streaming platform a century later. Todd Graves applied it selling fried chicken. The pattern holding across oil, music, and fast food is what makes Senra trust it: when a founder's real question is "how do I serve this customer better," money tends to follow; when the real question is "how do I make the most money right now," the service usually degrades and the money follows it down.

## Framework

### Step 1: State what the decision is actually optimizing for

Before finalizing a pricing, product, or growth decision, write down honestly whether the driving question was "what serves the customer best" or "what makes the most money fastest."

### Step 2: Check what got cut to hit the money target

If the decision was money-first, identify what quality, feature, or promise to the customer was reduced to get there. That cut is where the service degraded.

### Step 3: Rebuild the decision from the service question

Ask instead: what does the customer or user actually need here, done as well as you can do it. Let the resulting decision stand on its own, separate from what it does to short-term revenue.

### Step 4: Compare the two versions

Look at both the money-first version and the service-first version of the decision side by side. Notice where they diverge and what that divergence costs or gains in each direction.

### Step 5: Trust the service version for anything long-term

For any decision that compounds over years rather than one quarter, default to the service-first version, on the pattern that money produced this way tends to be more durable than money extracted directly.

## Anti-patterns

- Cutting product or service quality to hit a short-term revenue number, then being surprised when growth slows later.
- Treating "what makes the most money" and "what serves the customer best" as automatically the same question.
- Chasing money as the direct goal in a plan, instead of treating it as the measurable result of having served someone well.
- Applying this as an excuse to ignore the business's finances entirely. The claim is about which question drives the decision, not that money does not matter.

## Output shape

A short comparison: the money-first version of this decision, the service-first version, what was cut or changed to hit the money target, and a recommendation for which version to actually take, with the reasoning stated plainly.
