# Read the Primary Source First

Derived from *Founders Podcast* by David Senra, a reading pattern across the catalog: Steve Jobs in His Own Words, 38 Letters John D. Rockefeller Wrote to His Son, 400 Pages of Warren Buffett and Charlie Munger in Their Own Words, Lessons from Jeff Bezos's Shareholder Letters, and Napoleon: His Written and Spoken Words.

## Core Principle

Senra reads the founder's own words before he trusts a biography written about them, and where a founder left letters, an autobiography, shareholder letters, or a collected body of writing, he treats that as the real source and everything else as commentary. His reasoning: people writing near the end of a life, or writing to their own children, have little reason to perform for an audience. A secondhand account has already been filtered through someone else's interpretation before it reaches you.

## Framework

### Step 1: Name what you actually have

Before forming an opinion, ask plainly: is this the person's own words, or is it someone else's account of the person? If you cannot answer, you do not yet know what you are working with.

### Step 2: Find the closest thing to the primary source

Look for letters, an autobiography, a collected volume of talks or writing, an interview transcript, anything in the person's own voice. If a founder has a shareholder letter archive or a published memoir, that outranks a magazine profile of them.

### Step 3: Read it before the summaries

Go to the primary material first, even briefly, before reading what other people concluded about it. Let your own read form first, then compare it against the secondhand accounts.

### Step 4: Flag secondhand claims as secondhand

When you cannot get to a primary source, say so explicitly rather than presenting a summary as settled fact. Hold conclusions from secondhand accounts more loosely.

## Anti-patterns

- Forming a firm opinion about someone's methods or character from a single profile piece or a viral summary of their book.
- Treating a biography written about a person as equivalent in reliability to that person's own letters or writing.
- Citing a quote you found in a summary without checking it against the person's actual work.
- Skipping the primary source because the secondhand version is shorter or easier to find.

## Output shape

A short note on what you are actually relying on: primary source or secondhand account, and if secondhand, what the closest available primary material is and whether it is worth tracking down before you act on the conclusion.
