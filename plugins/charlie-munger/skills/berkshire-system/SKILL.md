# The Berkshire System

Derived from *Poor Charlie's Almanack* by Charles T. Munger, edited by Peter D. Kaufman (2014 fiftieth-anniversary letter, Munger's first-person essay).

## Core Principle

Centralize capital allocation and leader selection, decentralize operations to trustworthy managers, keep headquarters small, use little debt, and let a reputation for permanence attract more owners and operators who fit the system.

## Framework

### Step 1: Define the center

Reserve headquarters for capital allocation, chief-executive selection, essential control, and culture. Challenge every additional function.

### Step 2: Choose trustworthy operators

Select able, owner-oriented managers who need little supervision and want to remain for a long time.

### Step 3: Grant real autonomy

Give operating leaders clear outcomes, honest information channels, and freedom over methods. Intervene when integrity, capital, or survival is at risk.

### Step 4: Allocate capital centrally

Make retained cash compete across all units and external alternatives without letting local prestige determine funding.

### Step 5: Preserve permanence

Avoid unnecessary sales, leverage, and equity issuance. Make commitments credible enough to attract sellers and managers who value stewardship.

### Step 6: Protect the virtuous circle

Track whether trust is reducing bureaucracy and attracting better people, or whether weak selection is turning autonomy into neglect.

### Step 7: Plan succession

Specify who can allocate capital, select the next leader, preserve culture, and act when a trusted manager no longer fits.

## Anti-patterns

- Calling neglect autonomy.
- Building headquarters functions that manufacture activity and reports.
- Rewarding acquisition volume instead of long-term value creation.
- Using debt or equity issuance that undermines permanence and trust.

## Output shape

Return: Center mandate; Unit autonomy; Manager test; Capital flow; Bureaucracy cuts; Permanence safeguards; Succession design; Virtuous-circle metric.
