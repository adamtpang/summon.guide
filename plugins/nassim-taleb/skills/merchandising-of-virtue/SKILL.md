# The Merchandising of Virtue Test

Derived from *Skin in the Game* by Nassim Nicholas Taleb (Chapter 13, The Merchandising of Virtue (The Virtue Merchants, Unpopular Virtue, Take Risk)).

## Core Principle

Real virtue costs the person holding it something real, in money, comfort, reputation, or risk, and it does not require an audience to be worth doing. A stance that is popular to hold, costs nothing, and is loudly advertised, a hotel sign that mainly saves it laundry costs, a donation timed for maximum visibility, an opinion only voiced once it is safe, should be treated as marketing wearing virtue's clothes. The clearest, hardest to fake form of virtue is taking an unpopular, personally costly position, or better yet, taking real risk by building something rather than advocating from a position with no downside.

## Framework

### Step 1: Ask who benefits from the advertisement

For any publicly stated virtuous position, check whether advertising it also serves the advertiser's own interest (cost savings, reputation, fundraising, career advancement). If so, treat the public display with more skepticism than the underlying cause.

### Step 2: Check whether it would survive being done privately

Ask whether the person or organization would still do the thing if no one could ever find out. Give in secret is a real filter: virtue that requires an audience to be worth doing is not virtue, it is status buying.

### Step 3: Look for whether it costs the holder anything

Does holding this position risk the person's income, relationships, or standing with people whose approval they want? A position that is safe, popular, and free to hold tells you little about the holder's actual character.

### Step 4: Prize the unpopular stance over the popular one

When two people hold similar sounding values, give more weight to the one who took the position when it was unpopular or costly, and less to the one who only voiced it once it became safe or fashionable.

### Step 5: When asked how to do good, point to risk, not advocacy

For anyone, including yourself, asking how to actually help, the answer is not another conference, campaign, or hashtag; it is to take real, bounded risk by building something, a business, a product, a service, that generates real value, and to give generously once it succeeds.

## Anti-patterns

- Crediting an organization's ethical stance because of how loudly and visibly it advertises it, without checking whether the stance also serves its own bottom line.
- Publicly announcing your own good deeds in a way that would embarrass you if you had to give in total secrecy instead.
- Treating a popular, cost free opinion as evidence of someone's character.
- Substituting advocacy, campaigns, and meetings about a cause for taking real personal or financial risk on it.

## Output shape

A short verdict on the virtuous claim or stance in question: who benefits from its advertisement, whether it would survive being done privately, what it actually costs the holder, and how much weight to give it as a signal of real character.
