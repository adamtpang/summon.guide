# The Ludic Fallacy Check

Derived from *The Black Swan* by Nassim Nicholas Taleb (Chapter 9: The Ludic Fallacy, or The Uncertainty of the Nerd).

## Core Principle

In a casino you know the rules, the odds are computable, and the uncertainty is sterilized and bounded, a form of randomness Taleb calls ludic, from the Latin for game. Real life uncertainty is not like this: the rules are not posted anywhere, the range of outcomes is not bounded, and the biggest risks come from outside the model entirely, the way a casino's four largest losses all came from events its gambling theory never considered (a tiger, a disgruntled contractor, a hidden stack of IRS forms, a kidnapping) rather than from any gambler's lucky streak. Mistaking real life for a game, applying its clean probabilities to open-ended situations, is the specific error that makes trained, credentialed people more vulnerable to Black Swans than street-smart operators who know they do not know the rules.

## Framework

### Step 1: Ask who wrote the rules of this game

For any model or probability being cited, identify whether the rules and the space of possible outcomes are genuinely fixed and known (a coin, a die, an exam) or assumed and incomplete (a market, a career, a geopolitical event). If nobody can point to where the rules are actually written down and guaranteed, you are not in a ludic domain.

### Step 2: Look for the casino's tiger

Explicitly list the biggest historical losses or surprises in this domain and check whether they came from inside the stated model or from something the model never considered. If the largest past hits came from outside the model, expect the next one to as well.

### Step 3: Distrust exam-shaped confidence

Notice when confidence in a forecast comes from someone's training in a bounded, game-like discipline (statistics exams, backtests, textbook cases) rather than from lived exposure to the actual messy domain. Credentialed fluency in the model is not the same as knowledge of the territory.

### Step 4: Ask what the model excludes by construction

Every model has a boundary past which it stops describing anything. Name explicitly what kinds of events fall outside this one's boundary (regime changes, fraud, acts unrelated to the modeled variable) rather than assuming the boundary does not exist.

### Step 5: Prefer the operator who admits the odds are unknown

When choosing between two people making a real-world risk call, one who states a precise probability with confidence and one who says the odds cannot be computed but describes the worst case, weight the second one's judgment more heavily on anything outside a genuinely bounded game.

## Anti-patterns

- Applying a bell curve, a fixed probability, or a game's clean odds to a real situation whose rules and outcome space are not actually known or bounded.
- Treating academic or exam-based fluency in probability as equivalent to real judgment about an open-ended domain.
- Building elaborate defenses against the risks the model already names while ignoring that the largest past losses came from outside the model entirely.
- Being reassured by a precise-sounding number instead of asking whether the situation is even the kind of thing that has a real, computable probability.

## Output shape

A short verdict on whether the situation is a genuinely bounded, rule-known game or an open-ended real-world domain, a list of past surprises that came from outside the cited model, and a recommendation to trust the number or discard it.
