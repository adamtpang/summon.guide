# Set Your Falsification Point Before You Act

Derived from *Fooled by Randomness* by Nassim Nicholas Taleb (Chapter Seven, The Problem of Induction, Popper's Answer, Pascal's Wager).

## Core Principle

No amount of confirming evidence proves a theory right, since the next observation could always be the exception; but a single disconfirming event can prove it wrong. This asymmetry means data should be used to test and reject a testable claim, never to affirm it, and any decision built on a belief should carry a predetermined exit point defined before the bet is placed, not improvised later in the heat of losses. Use statistics and judgment to size aggressive bets, but never let them talk you out of a predetermined exit.

## Framework

### Step 1: State the thesis as testable

Phrase the belief so that some observable outcome could prove it false. If no outcome could ever disprove it, it is not a thesis, it is a story.

### Step 2: Name the falsification point in advance

Before committing, write down the specific event, price, or result that would mean you were wrong.

### Step 3: Separate the bet from the risk control

Use your best judgment and available data to decide the direction of the bet, but let the predetermined exit point, not your evolving opinion, decide when to stop.

### Step 4: Update on disconfirmation, not on discomfort

When the falsification point is hit, exit regardless of how convinced you still feel. Feeling convinced is not new evidence.

## Anti-patterns

- Committing to a thesis with no predefined condition that would prove it wrong
- Treating a string of confirming data points as proof rather than as merely not yet disproven
- Moving the goalposts after a loss instead of honoring the exit point set in advance
- Using historical data to justify the size of a risk instead of just the existence of an opportunity

## Output shape

A one page pre-commitment: the testable thesis, the specific falsification point, and the predetermined action to take when it is hit.
