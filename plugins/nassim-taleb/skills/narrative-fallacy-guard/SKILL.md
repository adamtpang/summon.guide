# Guard Against the Narrative Fallacy

Derived from *The Black Swan* by Nassim Nicholas Taleb (Chapter 6: The Narrative Fallacy).

## Core Principle

The mind cannot hold raw, unconnected facts for long, so it compresses any sequence of events into a story by inserting a cause, and the more the story compresses the facts the easier it is to remember, repeat, and believe, regardless of whether the inserted cause is real. This is why the sentence the king died and then the queen died of grief feels truer than the king died and the queen died, even though it contains an unproven claim, and it is why after the fact, everything looks explainable while the same events looked like scattered noise before they happened. The fallacy is dangerous specifically around rare events, because it retroactively erases the surprise, teaching you the wrong lesson from a Black Swan.

## Framework

### Step 1: Separate the sequence from the explanation

Write down the bare sequence of what happened with no causal words (then, because, so, as a result). Then write the causal story as a separate paragraph. If the bare sequence feels thin and forgettable while the story feels rich and inevitable, that gap is the fallacy at work, not evidence the story is correct.

### Step 2: Ask what the story throws away

A good story reduces the dimensionality of events by discarding almost all of them. List three details from the actual sequence that the current explanation omits, and check whether any of them would complicate or contradict it.

### Step 3: Test the story against a coin flip

Ask whether the explanation would have felt just as convincing if the opposite outcome had occurred (the same personality trait or decision explained as the cause of the reverse result). If a story can explain any outcome equally well, it explains none of them.

### Step 4: Distrust the vivid, romanced, or theatrical version

When two explanations are available, the dull, low-drama one and the vivid, character-driven one, treat the pull toward the vivid one as a symptom, not a signal of quality. Vividness predicts memorability, not accuracy.

### Step 5: Write the postmortem before you know how it will read

Where possible, record your live, in-the-moment uncertainty (a decision log, a dated prediction) before the outcome is known, specifically so a later narrative cannot silently replace what you actually believed at the time.

## Anti-patterns

- Accepting a causal explanation mainly because it is well told, memorable, or emotionally satisfying rather than because it survived a real test.
- Writing a postmortem that reads as inevitable, erasing how uncertain the moment actually felt while it was happening.
- Favoring the vivid, personality-driven explanation for a success over the dull, structural, or lucky one.
- Letting a compelling story about the past replace an honest inventory of what was actually knowable at the time.

## Output shape

Two short paragraphs side by side: the bare sequence of facts with no causal language, and the causal story being told about them, followed by a list of the details the story omits and whether any would change the conclusion.
