# The Skin of Others Audit

Derived from *Skin in the Game* by Nassim Nicholas Taleb (Chapter 4, The Skin of Others in Your Game (A Mortgage and Two Cats, Finding Hidden Vulnerabilities)).

## Core Principle

A person's stated ethics are only as reliable as their independence from the people who can be hurt through them. Someone with a mortgage, young children, a single employer, or a fragile reputation is not free in the way someone with savings, no dependents, or a diversified reputation is free; pressure applied to the people and things they cannot afford to lose will bend them long before their stated principles do. The same audit works in the mirror: before you decide how far you will actually go for a position, list out honestly what and who can be used against you.

## Framework

### Step 1: List the dependents

For the person, or yourself, list who else is exposed to the consequences of the stance: children, an ill parent, a spouse's job, employees who depend on them. Each dependent is a lever someone else can pull.

### Step 2: List the concentrated exposures

Identify single points of failure: one employer, one client, one landlord, one platform, one reputation with one specific community. A person with only one source of income or standing has far less room to hold an unpopular position than one with several.

### Step 3: Ask what pressuring them would look like

Imagine an opponent who wants to silence or discredit this person, or you. What is the cheapest, most effective pressure point? If it is obvious and soft, expect the stance to weaken under real pressure, no matter how firm it sounds today.

### Step 4: Weight public statements accordingly

Give more credibility to positions held by people whose exposure profile can absorb the pressure (savings, multiple income sources, few or independent dependents), and treat confidently stated positions from highly exposed people as provisional until tested.

### Step 5: If it is your own stance, close the gaps you can

Before taking a costly public position, do what you realistically can to reduce your own exposure (savings, diversified income, fewer single points of failure) so the position is not quietly hostage to something you never named.

## Anti-patterns

- Assuming a person will hold a costly position simply because they state it with conviction.
- Ignoring that a person's family, job, or single client relationship is a lever an opponent can pull to change their behavior.
- Taking a public stand yourself without first naming, honestly, what you have to lose and who could be hurt through you.
- Treating financial independence as a reason to stop asking who else depends on you.

## Output shape

A short exposure map for the person, or yourself, in question: dependents, concentrated exposures, the likely pressure point an opponent would use, and a revised confidence level in whether the stated position will hold under real pressure.
