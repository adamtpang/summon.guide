# Mediocristan or Extremistan

Derived from *The Black Swan* by Nassim Nicholas Taleb (Chapter 3: The Speculator and the Prostitute).

## Core Principle

Mediocristan is the province of physical, additive quantities like height or weight, where no single observation can move the total by much, so a large sample really does teach you what you need to know. Extremistan is the province of scalable, informational quantities like wealth, book sales, or company size, where a single observation, one Bill Gates, one bestseller, one crash, can dwarf everything else in the sample, so the average is unstable and history alone cannot bound the risk. Confusing which province a quantity belongs to, applying Mediocristan-style confidence to an Extremistan quantity, is the single most common way people manufacture false certainty right before a Black Swan hits them.

## Framework

### Step 1: Ask the doubling question

Take the largest single value you have ever observed in this domain (the biggest customer, the worst crash, the bestselling title) and ask whether a value ten or a hundred times larger is physically or structurally possible. If yes, you are in Extremistan.

### Step 2: Ask what a single new data point can do

In your current sample, could adding one more observation change the total or the average by more than a rounding error? In Mediocristan it cannot. In Extremistan it routinely can.

### Step 3: Check whether the quantity is physical or informational

Physical quantities (height, calories, car accidents) are capped by biology or physics and tend toward Mediocristan. Informational or social quantities (wealth, fame, citations, casualties from modern weapons) are capped by nothing and tend toward Extremistan.

### Step 4: Size the sample to the province

In Mediocristan, a modest sample tells you almost everything; keep collecting data with confidence. In Extremistan, no realistic sample is ever large enough to bound the risk, so stop asking for more data and instead redesign your exposure so the next extreme observation cannot ruin you.

### Step 5: Never let one column borrow the other's confidence

The specific error to watch for is applying a Mediocristan tool, a bell curve, a standard deviation, a tight confidence interval, to an Extremistan quantity. If the quantity is scalable, throw out the tool before you throw out the data.

## Anti-patterns

- Using a bell curve or standard deviation on a quantity that is scalable, like company size, casualties, or market moves.
- Treating a long, calm historical track record in an Extremistan business as proof the risk has been measured, when it has only been unobserved so far.
- Assuming more data will eventually tame an Extremistan quantity, instead of accepting that the tail will keep surprising you at any sample size.
- Applying Extremistan-level paranoia to a genuinely Mediocristan quantity and over-hedging something that was never going to move much.

## Output shape

A one-line classification (Mediocristan or Extremistan) for the quantity in question, the doubling-question test that produced it, and the concrete change this implies for how much you trust the sample and which risk tools you are allowed to use.
