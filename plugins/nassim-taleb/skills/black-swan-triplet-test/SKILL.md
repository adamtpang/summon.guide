# The Black Swan Triplet Test

Derived from *The Black Swan* by Nassim Nicholas Taleb (Prologue: On the Plumage of Birds).

## Core Principle

A Black Swan is not just a big surprise. It is an event that is, first, an outlier that nothing in the past convincingly pointed to, second, carries an extreme impact, and third, gets explained after the fact in a way that makes it feel like it was predictable and even inevitable, even though it was not. The third attribute is the trap: our own explaining machinery manufactures the illusion that we understood the event in advance, which is exactly what stops us from preparing for the next one. Naming the triplet correctly matters because the response to a true Black Swan (build robustness to the unknown) is different from the response to an ordinary bad outcome (fix the specific known cause).

## Framework

### Step 1: Check rarity against the actual reference class

Ask what population of past events this one should be compared against, not the narrow local history everyone is using. An event that looks unprecedented in one company's five year history may be common across an industry's fifty year history. Only call it rare if it is rare in the right, wider reference class.

### Step 2: Check impact, not just surprise

A rare event with small consequences is a curiosity, not a Black Swan. Ask what actually changed, in money, casualties, market share, or structure, as a direct result of the event. If the impact is modest, treat it as noise, not as a signal to overhaul your models.

### Step 3: Separate the explanation from the prediction

Write down, honestly, whether anyone stated this specific scenario in writing, with a rough timeframe, before it happened. A plausible-sounding explanation invented afterward does not count as prior predictability, no matter how obvious it now feels.

### Step 4: Locate whose Black Swan it is

A Black Swan is relative to a given observer's model, not an absolute property of the event. The event that blindsided the turkey was expected by the butcher the whole time. Ask who in this situation had the wider model, and what they knew that your model excluded.

### Step 5: Route the response accordingly

If it passes all three tests, the correct response is to strengthen general robustness (redundancy, optionality, capped downside) rather than patch the one specific mechanism that failed, because the next Black Swan will not repeat this one's mechanism.

## Anti-patterns

- Calling every unpleasant surprise a Black Swan to avoid owning an ordinary, foreseeable failure.
- Claiming in hindsight that the event was obviously coming, which quietly justifies not building any real defenses against the next one.
- Fixing only the specific mechanism that caused this event, as if the next Black Swan will use the same door.
- Judging rarity from too narrow a reference class, so genuinely common events keep getting mislabeled as unique.

## Output shape

A short verdict: does this event pass all three tests (yes or no on each), whose model it was a Black Swan relative to, and whether the right response is a specific fix or a general increase in robustness.
