# Don't Tell Me What You Think, Tell Me What's in Your Portfolio

Derived from *Skin in the Game* by Nassim Nicholas Taleb (Chapter 1, Why Each One Should Eat His Own Turtles: Equality in Uncertainty (A Customer Is Born Every Day, Talking One's Book)).

## Core Principle

Advice that costs the giver nothing is a sales pitch, not counsel. Taleb's rule for separating the two is simple: find out what the advice giver actually holds. A person who tells you a stock is good while holding none of it, a journalist who forecasts a war he will never fight in, a consultant who recommends a strategy he will not be fired for, an intellectual who explains why some policy is good for you from a job that carries no downside if he is wrong, all of these are the turtle fisherman offering you the fish they will not eat themselves. The fix is not to distrust everyone; it is to make the exposure explicit before you weigh the words.

## Framework

### Step 1: Separate advice from a sale

Ask directly: is this person selling me something (a product, a vote, a course of action that benefits them) while dressing it up as advice for my own good? The tell is that they never mention a personal cost to themselves if it goes wrong for you.

### Step 2: Ask for the portfolio, not the opinion

Before weighing a forecast or recommendation, ask what the person holds: do they own the stock, work for the firm, get paid if you say yes, keep their job if it fails? If they will not answer, treat the opinion as a pitch.

### Step 3: Check the size of the exposure, not just its presence

A token position is closer to no skin at all than to real skin. Look for exposure that would actually hurt them: a meaningful fraction of their net worth, their job, or their reputation with people whose opinion matters to them.

### Step 4: Apply the same test to yourself before you speak

Before publishing a strong opinion, forecast, or piece of advice, ask whether you would still say it if you had to act on it yourself, publicly, with something real at stake. If not, either put something at stake or soften the claim to what you actually believe with your own money and time.

### Step 5: Weight the survivors, don't average the room

When two opinions conflict, do not average them. Give more weight to the one held by someone who loses something real if they are wrong, and discount the one that costs its holder nothing either way.

## Anti-patterns

- Taking a recommendation at face value because the person sounds confident, without asking what they hold.
- Treating a paid promoter's enthusiasm as equivalent to a customer's unpaid, self funded endorsement.
- Giving public advice or forecasts you would never act on with your own money, time, or reputation.
- Averaging the opinion of someone with real exposure and someone with none, as if they carry equal weight.

## Output shape

A short exposure check for the specific opinion or advice in front of you: who is talking, what (if anything) they hold or risk, whether their stated position matches their actual exposure, and a revised weight to give their words. Applied to your own claim, it ends in a plain yes or no on whether you would put real skin behind it.
