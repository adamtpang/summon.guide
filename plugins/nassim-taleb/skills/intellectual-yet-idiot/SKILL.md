# The Intellectual Yet Idiot Check

Derived from *Skin in the Game* by Nassim Nicholas Taleb (Chapter 6, The Intellectual Yet Idiot).

## Core Principle

Credentials measure the ability to pass exams written by people with the same credentials; they do not measure contact with reality. The Intellectual Yet Idiot gets first order effects right and misses everything past that, because he has never had to eat the consequences of being wrong. He mistakes fluency in a field's vocabulary for competence in the field itself. The fix is not anti intellectualism; it is a specific, checkable test: has this advice giver ever paid a real personal price for being wrong about this exact kind of thing, and would they pay one again if they are wrong this time.

## Framework

### Step 1: Ask what happens to them if they are wrong

For the specific claim in front of you, ask concretely: if this turns out to be wrong, does the person lose income, a job, savings, or reputation with people who matter to them? Or do they simply move on to the next paper, panel, or client with no visible cost?

### Step 2: Separate vocabulary fluency from track record

Notice when someone is using the right words for a field (probability, complexity, nutrition, macroeconomics) without a track record of decisions made under those words that carried real consequences. Fluency in jargon is not evidence of competence.

### Step 3: Check for first order thinking

Ask whether the advice accounts for second and third order effects, the things that happen after the immediate, visible consequence, or whether it stops at the first, most obvious effect. The Intellectual Yet Idiot routinely stops at the first step.

### Step 4: Weigh tested, ancestral wisdom against fresh theory

When a piece of hard won practical wisdom, from people who lived with the consequences, conflicts with a recent theoretical claim, do not automatically prefer the theory. Ask which one has actually been tested against reality, and for how long.

### Step 5: Prefer the source with something to lose

Between two sources of guidance on the same question, prefer the one whose life would visibly change if they turned out to be wrong, even if their prose is rougher or their credentials are thinner.

## Anti-patterns

- Deferring to a recommendation because of a prestigious degree, title, or publication, without asking what the person risks if it fails.
- Accepting a policy or plan because it sounds scientific, without checking whether it has ever been tested against real consequences.
- Dismissing practical, inherited wisdom as unsophisticated just because it lacks academic packaging.
- Following advice from someone who will not still be around, professionally or personally, if the advice turns out to be wrong.

## Output shape

A short verdict on the advice giver in front of you: what they risk if wrong, whether their fluency is backed by a track record, whether the advice stops at first order effects, and a decision on how much weight to give their recommendation.
