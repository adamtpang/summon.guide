# Count the Monkeys Before You Trust a Track Record

Derived from *Fooled by Randomness* by Nassim Nicholas Taleb (Part Two, Monkeys on Typewriters, Chapters Eight and Nine (Too Many Millionaires Next Door, It Is Easier to Buy and Sell Than Fry an Egg)).

## Core Principle

Given enough independent tries, some fraction will produce a streak of success by chance alone, and that streak will look identical to genuine skill from the outside. The streak tells you almost nothing until you know the size of the population it was drawn from, and how many equally plausible attempts failed and vanished from view. This applies to money managers, to a trading rule you fit to historical data until it finally works, and to any success literature that only interviews people who are already rich, famous, or still in business.

## Framework

### Step 1: Ask how many started

Before trusting a track record, ask or estimate how many people, funds, or rule variations were tried over the same period, not just how many are still standing today.

### Step 2: Ask who's missing

Actively look for the failures and dropouts that did not make it into the sample. If you cannot find them, assume the sample has already been filtered down to the survivors.

### Step 3: Check for a second bias

Ask whether the whole population, not just this individual, benefited from a rising tide (a bull market, a lucky era, a fad) that will not necessarily repeat.

### Step 4: Discount by cohort size

The larger the number of original attempts, the more you should discount an individual streak, no matter how impressive it looks in isolation.

### Step 5: Prefer the tested claim

Trust a claim only to the extent it can be tested against data you were not shown in advance, rather than the cherry picked evidence handed to you on a book jacket or a pitch deck.

## Anti-patterns

- Being impressed by a track record without asking how many others were tried and failed
- Reading a success study that only interviews the winners
- Fitting a trading rule to historical data until it looks great, then trusting it going forward without asking how many other rules you tried first
- Accepting an unsolicited pitch from someone who found you, rather than someone you sought out and vetted

## Output shape

A short due diligence note: estimated cohort size, estimated survivor rate, an era dependence check, and a revised trust score for the track record.
