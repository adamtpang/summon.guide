# Build Problem-Solving Capacity, Not A Sustainable Steady State

Derived from *The Beginning of Infinity* by David Deutsch (Chapter 17, Unsustainable (Easter Island's static culture, the falsified Ehrlich resource-collapse predictions)).

## Core Principle

Deutsch argues against sustainability in its usual sense: locking in a fixed, closed, resource balanced steady state. That framing is doomed by construction, because problems are inevitable, and an unforeseen problem will eventually arrive that a static plan has no way to correct, as he shows through Easter Island's static culture and the falsified resource collapse predictions of the 1970s. The only durable policy, in his view, is to judge institutions, plans, and ways of life by how good they are at correcting mistakes and creating new solutions when the current plan breaks, not by how little change they claim to need.

## Framework

### Step 1: Name the plan's promise of stability

Write down exactly what the plan claims will not need to change.

### Step 2: List the load-bearing assumptions

Identify the specific assumptions the plan depends on staying true for its stability claim to hold.

### Step 3: Ask what happens when one breaks

For each assumption, ask what the plan does if it turns out false. Does the plan have a way to generate a new solution, or does it just fail.

### Step 4: Redesign around correction, not stasis

Rebuild the plan around preserving and growing the capacity to solve the next unforeseen problem, rather than around eliminating the need to solve one.

## Anti-patterns

- Selling a plan as sustainable or self sufficient as if that were itself a strength rather than a hidden single point of failure
- Locking in a fixed resource budget or steady state instead of building in the capacity to find more of what you need or replace it
- Treating an unforeseen problem as proof the plan failed instead of an expected part of any long horizon plan

## Output shape

The plan's hidden fixed assumption, the failure mode when that assumption breaks, and the one change that turns the plan into a mistake-correcting system instead of a static one.
