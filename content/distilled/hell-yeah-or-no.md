# Hell Yeah or No

This governs the moment before a commitment: an invitation, a project, a job, a plan you have been describing for years without starting. The claim is that most people are too busy with medium-good obligations to take the rare great one when it shows up, so the filter is binary. Underneath the filter sits the harder question the book keeps returning to, which is whether the thing you say you want is something you actually want or something you inherited.

## Do

- **Say no to anything short of a real "hell yeah."** Half-hearted commitments fill a life and then leave nothing available when a genuine opportunity arrives. Refusing almost everything is what makes the rare yes worth anything. [Source: "Saying No, Part 1"]

- **Name the one thing you are optimizing for.** Money, fame, freedom, learning, or giving. Chasing several at once produces constant internal conflict, and his example is Hollywood, where the richest people are often the least famous, having traded the spotlight for the money the famous ones gave up. [Source: "Updating Identity, Part 1"]

- **Run the thought experiment where money and attention are already maxed out.** If you could not possibly want more of either, what would you stop doing? What is left is the part of your life that is not a response to somebody else's scoreboard. [Source: "Updating Identity, Part 1"]

- **Ask what you hate not doing, not what you love.** "What do you love" rarely returns anything actionable. The inverse question, what leaves you depressed or off-track when you skip it, is sharper and produces an answer you can act on. [Source: "What's Worth Doing?, Part 1"]

- **Change the "ands" in your preconditions to "ors."** His exercise habit was gated on a chain of conditions all being true at once (nice day and work done and not just eaten and feeling energetic), which almost never happened. Any one of them being true happens constantly. [Source: "Making Things Happen, Part 2"]

- **Generate a third and fourth option before comparing the first two.** A friend stuck between quitting a frustrating job and staying got unstuck only after brainstorming hybrids and absurd variants (build the company at night, pitch it to the boss as a new division) until he could see what he actually wanted. [Source: "Making Things Happen, Part 2"]

- **Keep a stable job and do the art without needing it to sell.** Each half takes the pressure off the other, so neither the income nor the work has to satisfy every need at once. He prescribes it to artists and burned-out professionals alike. [Source: "What's Worth Doing?, Part 1"]

- **Find people whose problem you can solve one at a time before you start the business.** No name, no website, no system. A new campus left its lawn unpaved for a year and then paved only where students had already worn paths. You are at your dumbest at the start of a plan and your smartest at the end, so decide late. [Source: "What's Worth Doing?, Part 2"]

- **Judge a goal only by whether it changes what you do today.** A "someday" goal that produces no action right now is, by his definition, a bad goal. A great one is close and specific enough that you drop what you are doing and start. [Source: "Saying Yes"]

- **Move toward whatever scares you.** He applies it to the small version (talking to someone intimidating) and the large one (starting a business, quitting a job), treating fear as a form of excitement. Maslow's version, which he quotes, is choosing growth over safety a dozen times a day. [Source: "Saying Yes"]

- **Switch strategies when the current one starts working.** Early on, say yes to everything to maximize the chance of a lucky break. Once something takes off, drop everything else and focus on it. Once the focus pays off and opportunities flood in, switch to the hell yeah filter. Then repeat at the next plateau. [Source: "Making Things Happen, Part 2"]

## Don't

- **Don't fix an unbalanced life by adding.** The default move is more effort, more commitments, more stuff, when the effective one is removing something already there. His observation: the least successful people he knows say yes to almost everything, the most successful say no to almost everything. [Source: "Fixing Faulty Thinking, Part 1"]

- **Don't keep a title you have stopped earning.** He retired his own "entrepreneur" label once enough years had passed since founding anything, on the grounds that titles like leader or good friend expire and have to be re-earned. Holding one lets you feel settled about who you are without doing the work that would justify it. [Source: "Updating Identity, Part 1"]

- **Don't take one successful person's advice as a prescription.** Their advice reflects their own lucky numbers, or whatever opinion happens to be scarce in their surroundings, not your situation. Treat advice like echolocation and collect many reflections instead of trusting one. [Source: "Making Things Happen, Part 2"]

- **Don't mistake taking things in for inspiration.** Inspiration means breathing in, and it only arrives once an idea gets applied to your own work. Endless articles, books and talks with no output are, in his word, anti-inspiring. [Source: "Saying Yes"]

- **Don't answer a real question on the spot.** He describes needing days to form an opinion and asks interviewers for questions a week in advance, preferring "I don't know" now and a considered answer later over a reflexive first reaction performed as spontaneity. [Source: "Saying No, Part 1"]

- **Don't act while your state of mind is bad.** His routine is to ask what is literally wrong this second, which is usually nothing physical, then observe now and delay any action for days, raise your standards, refocus on the long-term goal, and do the dishes and the bills regardless of mood. [Source: "Saying No, Part 2"]

- **Don't enter a commitment without imagining how it ends.** In the $100 bill auction, the second-highest bidder still pays and gets nothing, which is what keeps people escalating past any sane value. Sometimes the winning move is refusing to play. [Source: "Saying No, Part 2"]

- **Don't accept the standard pace as your actual speed limit.** At seventeen a studio owner taught him a full semester of Berklee music theory in one three-hour lesson. He compressed six semesters into a handful of lessons and finished college in two and a half years. Systems are paced for the average person. [Source: "Making Things Happen, Part 1"]

- **Don't assume maximum effort is buying you much.** His all-out fifteen mile bike sprint took forty-three minutes. At roughly half effort the same route took forty-five, and he finished relaxed instead of wrecked. Most of the habitual strain was producing stress, not results. [Source: "Making Things Happen, Part 1"]

- **Don't wait until you feel confident.** Selling his company left him humbled and creatively stuck. What got him moving was reducing the job to contributing something useful, since unused learning is wasted and the public reaction was never really about him. [Source: "What's Worth Doing?, Part 2"]

- **Don't discard an idea because of its source.** He pulls author photos off books before reading them, and avoided meeting James Brown, his biggest musical influence, on purpose. Rejecting a good idea over its flawed origin is usually just an excuse to stop engaging with it. [Source: "Fixing Faulty Thinking, Part 1"]

- **Don't defend an early opinion as your true self.** He hated Tom Waits for years after one bad first impression, then loved him after hearing a different song by chance, and the same reversal happened with Indonesia and with weightlifting. An early opinion is usually inexperience wearing the costume of taste. [Source: "Updating Identity, Part 2"]

## Hold

- **Assume you are below average.** Large majorities rate themselves above-average drivers, teachers and patients. Deliberately assuming the opposite keeps him asking questions instead of dismissing other people as less capable. Held, you keep learning in rooms you would otherwise talk in. [Source: "Changing Perspective, Part 1"]

- **Take the fault, not the forgiveness.** When someone lies to him or disappears, he assumes he created the conditions that made it likely. He argues this beats forgiving, because forgiveness still casts you as the victim while fault puts you back in control of what happens next. [Source: "Changing Perspective, Part 1"]

- **"We'll see" is usually the accurate verdict.** The farmer's horse runs off, returns with twenty wild ones, the son breaks both legs taming them, and the broken legs later keep him out of a fatal war. Every neighbor calls each turn good news or bad news, and the farmer declines to. [Source: "Changing Perspective, Part 2"]

- **Your character is being built by small daily choices, not fixed at birth.** Returning to New York fifteen years after working in music there, he found people had ended up roughly where their character predicted rather than where their starting position did. The corollary he draws is that how you do anything is how you do everything. [Source: "Updating Identity, Part 2"]

- **The public version of you is a cardboard cutout with your name on it.** A thousand hostile comments on a post about switching programming languages were reactions to that cutout, not to him. He now applies the same discount to praise. [Source: "Updating Identity, Part 2"]

- **Most of the meaning you find was supplied by you.** Chinese characters built from unrelated phonetic parts, and Talking Heads lyrics assembled from phrases pulled out of a bowl, both get read as deliberate. If meaning is attached rather than discovered, a bad story attached to an event can be detached the same way. [Source: "Fixing Faulty Thinking, Part 2"]

## The one line

Refuse everything that is not a hell yeah, then check that the few things left are ones you actually want rather than ones you agreed to want a long time ago.
