# Tribe of Mentors

The design of this book is the argument: Ferriss sent the same eleven questions to over a hundred people and printed the answers side by side, so contradictions are visible rather than edited out. Ferriss asks; the answers belong to the people who gave them. What survives across almost all of them is that refusal, recovery and resilience are built as systems rather than exercised as willpower, and where two of them answer the same question in opposite directions, that is written down below rather than averaged into advice nobody actually gave.

## Do

- **Turn refusal into a rule so you never have to decide in real time.** Samin Nosrat keeps a running notebook of her own goals and checks each incoming opportunity against the list rather than judging it fresh, since vagueness about her own direction was what used to make her say yes out of fear of missing out. Kyle Maynard borrowed a rule from a CEO's hiring process that banned the number 7 on a 1 to 10 scale, then noticed that the invitations he felt obliged to accept were almost always the ambiguous 7s, so he now forces every 7 up or down before answering. Michael Gervais keeps an explicit catalog of automatic nos, unstructured requests to pick his brain among them, and treats the specificity of the list as the discipline. [Source: "What They've Gotten Better at Saying No To"]

- **Decline with a concrete constraint, not a vague brush-off.** All three declines Ferriss printed inside the book name a specific reason. Wendy MacNaughton had recently maxed out after five uninterrupted years of output and was cancelling existing contracts to protect unstructured time. Danny Meyer names the plain shortage of hours against his own company and his long-delayed writing. Neal Stephenson was running an experiment in adding nothing new to his to-do list at all, having noticed that completing items kept spawning sub-tasks, so every new request was being declined as blanket policy rather than case by case. [Source: "How to Say No: The Declined Invitations"]

- **Change the body before you try to change the mind.** Asked what they do when overwhelmed, the interviewees converge on a physical reset almost unanimously. Josh Waitzkin surfs if there are waves and otherwise takes a short intense kettlebell session, a cold plunge or controlled breathing, arguing that not understanding how strongly the mind follows the body is what keeps most people stuck. Neil Gaiman runs a diagnostic first: has he slept, eaten, or taken a walk. Samin Nosrat gets into the ocean. Yuval Noah Harari observes his own breath for a few seconds. [Source: "What They Do When Overwhelmed or Unfocused"]

- **Decide how the thing ends before you start it.** Samin Nosrat ran a restaurant that lost money for all five years of its existence and was devastated when it finally closed. What she took from it was to envision the ideal ending of a project at the outset, which she then applied deliberately, closing a subsequent thriving food market on her own terms rather than letting a success run past the point she wanted it to. [Source: "Favorite Failures"]

- **Raise your rate against the advice of the people telling you not to.** Kyle Maynard was told early in his speaking career that raising his fee would price him out of a competitive market. He raised it anyway, repeatedly, and found that both demand and negotiating friction moved in his favor rather than against him. He lists it as advice he wishes he had ignored sooner. [Source: "Bad Advice People Hear in Their Field"]

- **Buy the conditions the work needs rather than waiting for pressure to supply them.** Susan Cain taught negotiation skills on the side for years to build a modest financial cushion, so that creative time could be spent on focus and joy instead of survival, and then spent seven unhurried years on Quiet, including a restart after an editor called an early draft bad. She names the time itself, not the outcome, as the investment she was certain about. [Source: "Advice for a College Student, and What to Ignore"] [Source: "The Best Investments They Ever Made"]

- **Discount any claim that would sound the same whether or not it were true.** Vitalik Buterin's five year change was learning to reason counterfactually about what people say when their interests diverge from his: ask what this person would say in both worlds, and treat an answer that does not vary with the truth as containing no information at all. [Source: "The Five-Year Belief or Habit That Changed Everything"]

- **Take the shot yourself so the failure is yours.** Terry Crews missed the final shot of a high school championship game and was publicly blamed for it, then reframed it within days: he had taken the shot rather than passing it to somebody else, and decided from then on that any failure would at least be on his own terms. Ann Miura-Ko's version is two losing years on a debate team, parents advising her to quit, a summer of over-preparation instead, an undefeated year and eventually a national championship, and the conclusion that she is a better judge of her own capability than anyone advising her to stop. [Source: "Favorite Failures"]

- **Treat the years already spent as gone.** Peter Attia names the sunk cost argument, that you have spent years learning something and therefore cannot leave, as the advice to ignore, on the plain reasoning that the spent years are unrecoverable while the years ahead are fully open. He applied it across his own moves from engineering to medicine to finance. [Source: "Advice for a College Student, and What to Ignore"]

- **Reserve time you cannot justify.** Gary Vaynerchuk says no to nearly everything now but deliberately protects roughly 20 percent of his time for things with no obvious return, arguing the serendipity from that reserved fifth has repeatedly paid for the whole allocation. John Arnold gets at the same asymmetry from the other side, noting that organizations which agonize over small direct expenses show no comparable discomfort about wasting staff hours in unproductive meetings. [Source: "What They've Gotten Better at Saying No To"]

- **Ask a stranger for the thing you need.** Arianna Huffington's second book was rejected by 37 publishers and she was out of money, so she walked into a random London bank and asked for a personal loan. She got it, it kept her afloat long enough to be published, and she still sends the banker a holiday card. [Source: "Favorite Failures"]

- **Start collecting the list before you have anything to sell.** Marie Forleo names a three dollar yellow legal pad she carried everywhere while bartending and teaching hip-hop classes in her twenties, using it to take email addresses from anyone curious about her side business, and credits that pad and the list it built as the literal foundation of a company that has since generated more than $75 million. Ray Dalio's answer to the same question is a pocket notepad, valued entirely for the habit of immediate written capture rather than for any feature of the notepad. [Source: "The Sub-$100 Purchases That Mattered"]

- **Ask what someone actually hands to other people, not what they call a favorite.** Ferriss deliberately asked for the most-gifted book rather than the best one, on the reasoning that a favorite is high stakes and hard to commit to in print, while a gifted book is one the person has already tested on real people and watched land. The answers bear the design out: Jocko Willink names a single book, David Hackworth's combat memoir, which he has reread continuously since partway through a twenty year career and from which he says he draws his leadership principles more than from any military doctrine. [Source: "The Books They Gift Most"]

- **Act before the confidence arrives.** Debbie Millman took a reframe from a conversation with Dani Shapiro: confidence is overrated and frequently a mask for insecurity, while courage means acting in spite of fear, and confidence itself only ever grows out of the repeated practice that the courage makes possible. [Source: "The Five-Year Belief or Habit That Changed Everything"]

## Don't

- **Don't build the platform instead of the work.** Steven Pressfield names the publishing industry's obsession with building a following and establishing a platform as the central bad advice in his field, likening the result to a river a mile wide and an inch deep, and treats it as a substitute for the sustained work of writing something worth reading. [Source: "Bad Advice People Hear in Their Field"]

- **Don't be present on every platform at once.** Marie Forleo's objection to the startup impulse to be everywhere is partly about quality, since spreading thin produces mediocre content in every channel, and partly a plainly unglamorous operational point: each additional platform is also a new customer service channel that somebody has to monitor. [Source: "Bad Advice People Hear in Their Field"]

- **Don't buy something because it has been doing well.** Ray Dalio names that heuristic as backward. A market that has already performed well has by that fact become more expensive rather than more attractive, so recent performance should be triggering caution rather than enthusiasm. [Source: "Bad Advice People Hear in Their Field"]

- **Don't repeat that anyone can do anything they put their mind to.** Michael Gervais treats the platitude as revealing about whoever says it, arguing it exposes a naive model of how human potential and real constraint actually work. [Source: "Bad Advice People Hear in Their Field"]

- **Don't optimize for how you look and feel this quarter.** Peter Attia's charge against his own field is that longevity medicine over-indexes on short term appearance and symptoms and under-indexes on the harder work of delaying the onset of chronic disease, which is where the actual effect on lifespan sits. Gary Vaynerchuk's habit is the same bet in personal form: a daily strength and mobility regimen he keeps up while explicitly reporting zero immediate energy benefit, because the payoff he wants is in his seventies and eighties. [Source: "Bad Advice People Hear in Their Field"] [Source: "The Five-Year Belief or Habit That Changed Everything"]

- **Don't answer while angry.** Debbie Millman imposes a forced delay, sometimes an hour and sometimes overnight, before responding to anything written in anger or frustration, and keeps a fortune cookie message taped to her laptop as the reminder. [Source: "What They Do When Overwhelmed or Unfocused"]

- **Don't call it being busy.** Millman's billboard is that busy is a decision, on the argument that saying you are too busy is only ever shorthand for saying the thing is not a priority, since people make time for what actually matters to them rather than finding it. [Source: "What They'd Put on a Billboard"]

- **Don't aim at success as the target.** The book's final interlude quotes Viktor Frankl to exactly this effect, that success, like happiness, only ever arrives as a byproduct of pursuing something meaningful and cannot be pursued directly. Ferriss places it after Harari's profile as the closing note on the entire project. [Source: "Quotes I'm Pondering: Tim's Own Commonplace Book"]

- **Don't insist on doing the whole thing alone.** Yuval Noah Harari's Hebrew Sapiens was a bestseller and was then rejected by every publisher he approached for an English translation; self-publishing it himself sold a couple of hundred copies. He credits professional help, an agent, an editor and a public relations firm, with the eventual international result, and names the limits of doing everything himself as the lesson. [Source: "Favorite Failures"]

## Where they disagree

- **Is burnout the price of success, or the proof you are doing it wrong?** Arianna Huffington's billboard says burnout is not the price, a position she reached after collapsing from exhaustion in 2007, and she has since stopped dividing her day into work time and non-work time, treating walks and unplugging as part of the work rather than as its opposite. Samin Nosrat guards eight to nine hours of sleep mercilessly, insisting on early dinner reservations and leaving parties early. Directly against them, Jocko Willink and Gary Vaynerchuk each independently name working relentlessly harder than peers as non-negotiable early-career advice, with Willink adding that it holds even at a first job you dislike. Debbie Millman goes further and rejects the concept of work-life balance outright, arguing that searching for balance is itself a signal you are in the wrong career, and frames job hunting as a competitive sport requiring career athleticism. Steven Pressfield's rule, learned from a friend who trained with Jack LaLanne, is that a day off work is fine but on that day you do not eat. The corpus does not settle it, and the reason is a difference in vantage: Huffington and Nosrat are answering after a collapse and after five money-losing years respectively, while Willink and Vaynerchuk are describing the stretch before anything had been built. Nothing here tells you what either pair would say from the other's position. [Source: "What They'd Put on a Billboard"] [Source: "The Five-Year Belief or Habit That Changed Everything"] [Source: "Advice for a College Student, and What to Ignore"] [Source: "Bad Advice People Hear in Their Field"] [Source: "What They Do When Overwhelmed or Unfocused"]

- **Is saying no a skill you develop, or a job you hand to somebody else?** Josh Waitzkin declines more than 99 percent of professional opportunities offered to him, working from the position that nothing beats investing in his own learning and that being 99 percent committed to something produces worse results than being fully committed to fewer things. Steven Pressfield adopted the mental model of a celebrity security firm he once toured, where virtually nothing reaches the client, and now imagines himself as his own gatekeeper. Against both, Yuval Noah Harari admits he is genuinely bad at refusing and has outsourced the function completely: his husband handles most declines, and the couple employs an assistant whose job includes spending hours a day saying no on his behalf. Rick Rubin gives the rarest answer in the chapter, that he does not think he is good at this at all, which Ferriss deliberately kept in. What this actually settles is that in no case is the answer willpower. It is a rule, a delegate, or an unresolved problem. [Source: "What They've Gotten Better at Saying No To"]

- **Should you outwork the competition, or is competing itself the error?** Terry Crews names working hard to beat the competition as actively counterproductive, arguing that competition-focused thinking is the opposite of creative thinking and that real security comes from being irreplaceable in your own particular way rather than outperforming rivals at a shared game. Debbie Millman argues the reverse in answering the neighboring question, telling students to keep asking whether they are working harder and refining their skills more than the specific people going for the same roles. Willink and Vaynerchuk line up behind Millman. What separates them is the object. Millman is describing a job opening, a slot that somebody else will occupy if you do not, where relative effort genuinely decides it. Crews is describing a body of work that only he would have made, where there is no slot and therefore no race. The corpus never says this outright, but the two are not describing the same situation. [Source: "Bad Advice People Hear in Their Field"] [Source: "Advice for a College Student, and What to Ignore"]

- **Should a young person take advice from older people at all?** Harari's answer to what to ignore is unusually pointed given where it is printed: he argues most of what schools teach now will be irrelevant by 2040, and that trusting adults' accumulated wisdom too heavily is a mistake, because hard-won lessons about a slower-changing world may simply no longer apply. Set against that, inside the same book, Peter Attia tells students to seek mentors without shame, Ann Miura-Ko has kept a mentor's instruction to commit a fixed percentage of income to charity from the very start of a career and treats it as lifelong, and Kyle Maynard runs on a line he took from his friend Richard Machowicz and carried through wrestling losses, mountains and doubters. The distinction that holds up is between content and conduct. Harari's objection is to transferable domain knowledge, which dates; what Attia, Miura-Ko and Maynard actually carry from their elders is how to behave, which is also the category Harari's own recommendation, resilience and the capacity to keep reinventing yourself, falls into. [Source: "Advice for a College Student, and What to Ignore"] [Source: "Favorite Failures"]

- **Does the work need hardship, or does hardship just need surviving?** Susan Cain warns directly against the popular belief that creative work requires financial desperation or crisis to be any good, and built a cushion specifically so it would not. Against her, Steven Pressfield tells young people to leave the optimization mindset entirely, drive a truck, join the Marines, seek out adversity on purpose. Gary Vaynerchuk reframes his entire record as an immigrant D and F student with nothing to his name as the exact pressure that forced him to build outside a system that had written him off. Kyle Maynard names suffering itself as the thing he loves most, listing bullying, broken bones, freezing mountainsides and near-bankruptcy among his favorite experiences in retrospect. The two sides are not answering the same question: Cain is describing the conditions for producing work, the others the conditions for forming a person. And the corpus cannot settle whether the hardship was required or merely survived and later narrated as necessary, since only the people it did not stop are in the book. [Source: "Advice for a College Student, and What to Ignore"] [Source: "Favorite Failures"] [Source: "Unusual Habits and Absurd Obsessions"]

## Hold

- **Roughly half of this will not be for you, by the author's own estimate.** Ferriss frames the book as a buffet rather than a syllabus, and states the target plainly: like about half, love a quarter, and have some small fraction change your life. He instructs readers to skip whatever does not grab them rather than push through out of duty. Held, a piece of advice that does not fit is simply not yours. Not held, every unused answer becomes evidence of a personal failure to apply it. [Source: "Why This Book Exists: The Eleven Questions"]

- **Advice is a report from one specific journey, not a mechanism.** Rick Rubin distrusts nearly all advice tied to chasing commercial safety, testing with focus groups or copying a stable path, on the grounds that genuinely new work is off the map by definition and that received wisdom from somebody else's particular journey rarely transfers cleanly. His own account supports it: his early albums succeeded so consistently that his first commercial failure was traumatic, and it took several more of each before he accepted that a project's commercial fate is often disconnected from its actual quality. Held, this book is a hundred hypotheses. Not held, it reads as a hundred proofs. [Source: "Bad Advice People Hear in Their Field"] [Source: "Favorite Failures"]

- **Everyone in here is compulsive about something small and slightly embarrassing.** Harari stands on his toes in elevators. Vitalik Buterin only watches in-flight films in languages he is not yet fluent in and will only eat dark chocolate between 80 and 90 percent cacao. Ann Miura-Ko is devoted to specific pen and notebook models traced to a childhood in her family's stationery store. Peter Attia has invented a pastime involving eggs. Ferriss's reading is that normal people are simply people whose particular oddities you have not learned yet. Held, your own strangeness stops being a disqualification. Not held, the accomplished look like a different species. [Source: "Unusual Habits and Absurd Obsessions"]

## The one line

Build the rule that says no for you, move the body before you try to fix the mind, and read every answer here as one person's report from one life rather than as proof of what works.
