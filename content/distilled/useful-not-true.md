# Useful Not True

This governs the beliefs you run on: the story you tell about what just happened, what someone meant, what you are capable of, what a situation is. Sivers sets the bar for "true" at absolutely and objectively true for everyone everywhere, notes that almost nothing anyone says clears it, and argues that since you are choosing an interpretation either way, you should choose the one that produces the action you need. Worth naming plainly: this is a real break from a truth-first approach, and the book's only brakes on it are its own definition of useful, which is what ultimately helps, and its rule to be slow and cautious on anything that could hurt you or someone else.

## Do

- **Strip the claim back to what was observably said or done.** In his parable a bridge guard only lets travelers cross once they state the bare fact. When the king tastes the soup and grimaces, "he thinks it's too salty" is refused, and only "He said the words: 'It's too salty.'" gets through. Even the plausible read is an addition. [Source: "The Bridge Guard: Rules, Obligations, and Culture Aren't True"]

- **Interrogate your own strong statements like a clerk filing an incident report.** A friend's "my mother abandoned me," reduced to observable events, meant she worked two jobs and he was raised by his grandmother nearby. Record the events, and file the rest as feeling. [Source: "What 'True' Means Here"]

- **Attach a number or a bet to any confident prediction.** For "if that guy gets elected it will be a disaster," ask "wanna bet," or ask how confident you are from 0 to 100. It converts a certainty into an estimate you can work with. [Source: "Your Brain Invents Reasons, Memories Lie, Even Science Isn't True"]

- **Aim off-target to correct a curve you already know you have.** His bowling ball always hooks left, so he aims right. Applied to his own head: he tends to blame others, so he assumes everything is his fault; he underestimates project time, so he doubles his guess; he assumes he is right and talks more than he listens, so he assumes he knows nothing. [Source: "Bowling: Aim Away From the Target"]

- **Turn a disempowering statement into a question, then sit and answer it.** "I'm too old" becomes "how can I use my age to my advantage." "I can't afford it" becomes "how can I afford it." The step people skip is answering rather than collecting more questions. [Source: "The Gap Between What Happens and How You Respond"]

- **Push past the second and third answer.** When "what's great about this?" returns "nothing," keep going, generating extreme and even ridiculous options, on the theory that being convinced there is a way out is what keeps you searching until you find one. [Source: "The Gap Between What Happens and How You Respond"]

- **Check a candidate perspective against the traits before trusting it.** His list includes direct, energizing, self-reliant so it does not depend on anyone's approval, balancing whatever has been neglected, healthy rather than merely easy, long-term, compensating for a known bias, tested in reality first, and lucid and lasting, meaning it still holds up a week later from a calm state. [Source: "Traits of a Perspective Worth Keeping"]

- **Switch from exploring to leading once you have picked.** The expedition captain tries every inlet during the explorer phase, then sets one destination and stops entertaining suggestions to change it mid-voyage. You can go back to exploring after you arrive. [Source: "From Explorer to Self-Leader"]

- **Take the first step immediately on anything that is not destructive.** He booked a $400 flight to London on a whim and only afterward realized he had committed to moving abroad for six months, which four years later had produced a child, three citizenships and a life he calls deeply happy. Slow and careful is reserved for what could hurt you or others. [Source: "Take the First Step Immediately"]

- **Write the new perspective into a private journal, then say it out loud to friends.** Stack up the reasons it helps, compress it into something a stranger would get in ten seconds, list the actions it implies, and pre-plan for the moment the old mindset comes back. Then re-tune it weekly, the way a freshly strung guitar needs constant retuning rather than one setup. [Source: "Take the First Step Immediately"]

- **Act the part for the minutes you need it.** Your outside does not have to match your inside: act brave for one minute while terrified and you were, in that minute, brave. He was not always in the mood to be a good father, so he collected his strength and acted like the one his son needed in short bursts, for years. [Source: "You Are What You Pretend to Be"]

## Don't

- **Don't treat your own confidence as evidence.** Split-brain patients invented confident false reasons for actions they had no knowledge of. Students surveyed on the day of the Challenger disaster and again three years later were almost all completely certain, and almost all contradicted their own original answers. [Source: "Your Brain Invents Reasons, Memories Lie, Even Science Isn't True"]

- **Don't trust your first read of a situation.** His model is the optical illusion where two equal lines look unequal because of the arrows on the ends: knowing better does not stop the first impression, so the instinctive verdict needs a deliberate second pass. He names getting past that automatic reaction as the hardest step in the whole method. [Source: "Beliefs Are Make-Believe"] [Source: "The Gap Between What Happens and How You Respond"]

- **Don't defend a belief with heat.** Ordinary beliefs get updated the moment you are shown proof. The ones defended with intense emotion are usually serving an identity rather than an accuracy, since if it were provable there would be nothing to get upset about. [Source: "Beliefs Are Make-Believe"]

- **Don't assume your own vantage point is the neutral one.** An American telling Scots he loves their accent gets told they do not have one, he does. A traveler asking how to get to the other side of the river is told he is already on it. Everyone else's beliefs get filed as mythology. [Source: "You Are the Strange One (And You Can't Trust Your Mind)"]

- **Don't leave inherited beliefs hanging unexamined.** His mother kept a painting she disliked out of sentiment, and reframing it revealed it had been hung upside down the entire time. A parent's fear or temper, or a lesson taken from a bad breakup, is worth taking off the wall and inspecting. [Source: "You Are the Strange One (And You Can't Trust Your Mind)"]

- **Don't throw out the contents to punish the box.** His cousin abandoned a fitness program that was working for her because she disliked the coach's political posts, and readers commonly discard a useful psychology book over an unrelated scandal. [Source: "Religion Is Action, Not Belief"]

- **Don't hold out for the perfect tool.** One carpenter works through the repair while his companion theorizes about the all-in-one tool that would replace every one of them. Refusing an imperfect tool is usually a sign of not doing the work. [Source: "Religion Is Action, Not Belief"]

- **Don't take in more input while the program is still running.** More books, more podcasts, more courses without output is a computer restarted mid-calculation. The people who describe themselves as running in circles are usually still in input mode. [Source: "From Explorer to Self-Leader"]

- **Don't argue that your choice is the right one.** Objections will come from people who would have chosen differently, from experts, from what everyone else did. The answer is that the choice is not for them, not for your past or future self, only for you and only for now, so it does not need to be defended as true. [Source: "Take the First Step Immediately"]

- **Don't seek the truth without asking what you plan to do with it.** People rarely want it for its own sake. Usually they want ammunition for an argument or the comfort of feeling informed before a decision, because emotion decides and facts rationalize afterward. [Source: "Life Is a Blank You Fill In"]

- **Don't accept a duty framed as a higher law.** When someone calls something your obligation, they want something and are avoiding the vulnerability of saying "I want." His test for whose problem it actually is: ask who benefits most from solving it. [Source: "The Bridge Guard: Rules, Obligations, and Culture Aren't True"]

## Hold

- **Not true does not mean false.** His bar for true is what any observer anywhere would agree on, always. Everything below that line is not disproven, it is just one available answer among others, which is what leaves room to reconsider. Refuse this and the whole method reads as a license to lie to yourself, which is not what it claims. [Source: "What 'True' Means Here"]

- **The most accurate model is not always the most useful one.** A scientist friend corrects him that science never claimed to be true in his sense, only progressively less wrong. Newton was superseded by Einstein and then by quantum mechanics, and Newton is still what you use to land on the moon. [Source: "Your Brain Invents Reasons, Memories Lie, Even Science Isn't True"]

- **Beliefs make emotions, emotions make actions.** This is the load-bearing claim under everything else. The same run feels different depending only on the story he tells himself while running it, a tiger behind him or a pot of gold ahead. It is why his question is never whether a belief is accurate but which one produces the action he needs. [Source: "Bowling: Aim Away From the Target"]

- **Meaning is supplied by the person, never built into the thing.** A workshop teacher let a dinner table argue for hours over what belongs in "life is ______," then pointed at the ceiling and asked what it meant, got shelter and structure, and told the room those were their meanings, not the ceiling's. [Source: "Life Is a Blank You Fill In"]

- **No option is best in itself, it becomes best once you commit to it.** You can do anything but not everything, and indecision is itself a choice that gets you nothing. The bestness comes from choosing, aligning behind it and acting, not from a property the option already had. [Source: "From Explorer to Self-Leader"]

- **What matters about a belief is the practice it produces.** He contrasts a man who believes God is on his side and treats his own temper as forgivable with a man full of doubt who keeps praying, giving and acting kindly. Belief without matching action misses the point of holding it. [Source: "Religion Is Action, Not Belief"]

- **The grief is part of the deal.** The book closes on the real death of his son's pet mouse, and argues that dodging grief by dodging love is like listening only to major chords. He treats heaven as possibly the first reframing humans ever invented. [Source: "You Are What You Pretend to Be"]

## The one line

You are picking an interpretation whether you notice or not, so strip the event down to what actually happened, then choose the reading that makes you do the thing you need to do next.
