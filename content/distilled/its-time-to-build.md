# It's Time to Build

This governs your reaction in the moment after something you rely on turns out not to exist: a supply that was never stockpiled, a system that was never written, a house that was never permitted. Written in the first weeks of the 2020 pandemic, the essay uses that specific failure as its hardest-to-dismiss evidence for a general claim, that the capability and the capital were already present and what was missing was the decision to build, and then it applies the same test to housing, education, manufacturing, and transportation. The rules below are the diagnostic and the response, not the pandemic case itself.

## Do

- **Ask what is being built, of other people and of yourself.** The essay's practical takeaway is a single repeatable question, what are you building, addressed to political leaders, executives, founders, investors, and the culture at large. Any answer that is not building something, enabling someone else's build, teaching people to build, or materially supporting builders is treated as unmet potential rather than as a valid answer. [Source: "The Price of Not Building and the Call to Build"]

- **Build the capacity before the need arrives, because during the emergency is too late.** The opening inventory is of things that did not require new science and simply had not been made: tests, the swabs and reagents to run them, ventilators, negative-pressure isolation rooms, ICU beds, masks, eye shields, gowns, with New York City reportedly asking for rain ponchos as improvised protective equipment. Experts had warned for years about a coronavirus crossing from an animal reservoir. The warning existed; the built response did not. [Source: "The Pandemic Proved We Stopped Building"]

- **Audit both directions of any system you depend on, not just the one that gets used daily.** The federal government collects taxes from every working American every year, and had built no matching machinery to push emergency money out to laid-off workers and small businesses quickly. Revenue collection was mature because it runs constantly; distribution was not, because it does not. [Source: "The Pandemic Proved We Stopped Building"]

- **Rule out money, market, and competence before you accept any explanation for why nothing gets built.** Roughly $2 trillion in relief spending passed in a matter of weeks once the priority was urgent enough, which closes the funding argument. Housing, education, healthcare, and manufacturing are already lucrative, which closes the markets-are-broken argument. The country has already built housing, skyscrapers, schools, hospitals, cars, computers, and smartphones, which closes the competence argument. What remains is desire, inertia, and capture. [Source: "Not Money, Not Capitalism, Not Skill"]

- **Look for the incumbent who benefits from the rule before you blame the rule.** Regulatory capture is named as a structural cause, not a mood: existing players in housing, healthcare, and education have real incentives to use regulation to keep new competitors out, which means the obstacle is often not an absence of rules but an excess of rules written to protect whoever is already there. [Source: "Not Money, Not Capitalism, Not Skill"]

- **Go looking for the known result nobody bothered to scale.** Education research already contains findings sitting unused, the Bloom two-sigma result on one-on-one tutoring outperforming classroom instruction being the named example, while K-12 pedagogy is described as having seen no major innovation since Montessori decades earlier. Meanwhile roughly four million Americans turn eighteen each year, and some 120 million people globally, against a higher education system tiny relative to that demand. [Source: "Housing, Education, and Manufacturing"]

- **Fix your own side's failure mode instead of waiting for the other side to move.** The essay indicts both: the right for letting a genuine instinct toward production get captured by crony capitalism, incumbent-protecting regulation, oligopoly, offshoring, and buybacks chosen over new factories; the left for defending existing public institutions rather than delivering better ones, with modernized VA hospitals, a Harvard scaled to 100,000 or a million students, and a large new nuclear fleet offered as what a serious version would actually look like. Nuclear is chosen deliberately because it cuts across the usual alignment. [Source: "A Build Agenda for Left and Right"]

- **Use the price curve to tell a supply choice apart from a hard limit.** Things produced at real scale, computers and televisions in his examples, get cheaper every year. Housing, education, and healthcare get more expensive every year. That divergence is treated as the clearest available evidence that the scarcity in the second group is chosen rather than physical. Run the same test on whatever is pricing you out. [Source: "The Price of Not Building and the Call to Build"]

## Don't

- **Don't accept an explanation that conveniently blames the other party.** Essentially every Western country stumbled on broadly the same shortages regardless of which party held power, which is what rules out a partisan or national cause and forces the diagnosis one level up. An explanation that lets your own side off the hook is usually the wrong one. [Source: "The Pandemic Proved We Stopped Building"]

- **Don't mistake defending what already exists for doing something.** Inertia is named as a distinct cause alongside desire and capture: a settled preference for protecting the current arrangement over accepting the disruption that new construction requires. Protecting a budget, a staff, or an institution is not the same act as improving it. [Source: "Not Money, Not Capitalism, Not Skill"]

- **Don't push more demand into a supply you have decided not to expand.** Affordable homeownership is tied directly to bending the price curve back down, and bending it requires building enough supply, not further subsidizing buyers into a market that has been deliberately constrained. Subsidy without supply moves the price, not the access. [Source: "The Price of Not Building and the Call to Build"]

- **Don't read offshoring as proof the capability is gone.** American manufacturing output remains high; what moved was production, to cheaper labor markets abroad rather than into automation at home. The counterexample held up is Elon Musk's highly automated factory concept, the alien dreadnought, as the kind of domestic capacity that could have been built instead. [Source: "Housing, Education, and Manufacturing"]

- **Don't judge a plan by its intentions.** Milton Friedman's framing is invoked directly against the case for public-sector solutions: intentions are not outcomes, and a policy has to be measured by what it actually delivered. The demand made of anyone favoring public provision is a better public hospital, school, transit system, or housing stock, not a better argument for one. [Source: "A Build Agenda for Left and Right"]

- **Don't criticize a build agenda without naming the one you would build instead.** Disagreement on any specific item is explicitly invited, on the condition that the critic proposes their own list. The specifics were never the point of the essay; the verb was. [Source: "The Price of Not Building and the Call to Build"]

## The one line

The money, the market, and the skill are almost always already there, so when something you needed does not exist, the only two real questions are what you are going to build and who benefits from you not building it.
