# Deep Work

This governs the small part of your working week that actually produces things other people cannot easily replicate. Newport's claim is that the ability to concentrate without distraction is a trainable skill, that it is becoming scarce at the same time it is becoming valuable, and that almost nobody is training it because the cost of distraction is nearly impossible to measure. The rules below are mostly about structure and scheduling, because his starting premise is that intention alone loses.

## Do

- **Pick one of the four depth philosophies and actually schedule it.** Monastic means cutting shallow obligations almost entirely, like Donald Knuth having had no email address since 1990. Bimodal alternates defined deep stretches with open connected time, like Jung at Bollingen or Adam Grant's multi-day research lockdowns. Rhythmic makes it a fixed daily slot. Journalistic fits it into whatever pockets appear. Choose deliberately rather than defaulting to whichever the week allows. [Source: "Rule #1: Work Deeply"]

- **Make the daily slot a rule rather than a decision.** Doctoral student Brian Chappell rescued a stalled dissertation by writing from 4:45 to 7:30 every morning before his day job, and Jerry Seinfeld's method was marking a calendar X for every day he wrote jokes and not breaking the chain. Robert Caro governs every detail of his office by fixed rule and Darwin structured his day around set study blocks and thinking walks. [Source: "Rule #1: Work Deeply"]

- **Batch the hard work into long blocks and finish one thing before starting the next.** Adam Grant became Wharton's youngest full professor by concentrating his research into isolated multi-day stretches rather than spreading it thin. Sophie Leroy's 2009 work on attention residue explains why: switching before a task is finished leaves divided attention behind that measurably degrades the next task. [Source: "Chapter One: Deep Work Is Valuable"]

- **Track deep hours on a visible scoreboard and review weekly.** Newport adapts the 4 Disciplines of Execution into a personal system: one wildly important goal, lead measures (hours of depth) rather than lag measures (papers published), a scoreboard you can see, and a weekly review. He credits it with nearly doubling his own academic output in a year. [Source: "Rule #1: Work Deeply"]

- **Set the finish time first and cut backward until the day fits.** Newport's fixed-schedule productivity means committing to a hard evening cutoff and then eliminating whatever shallow commitments will not fit inside it. Harvard's Radhika Nagpal protected a fixed fifty hour week by capping her academic travel and still made full professor ahead of the usual timeline. [Source: "Rule #4: Drain the Shallows"]

- **Schedule your internet windows, and treat everything outside them as training.** The inversion Newport insists on is that you take breaks from focus rather than breaks from distraction. Write the permitted online blocks on a notepad and refuse to check even briefly outside them, because a quick check outside a window is what teaches the brain that novelty is always one moment away. [Source: "Rule #2: Embrace Boredom"]

- **End the working day with a fixed shutdown routine you actually say out loud.** Newport reviews and plans, then declares "shutdown complete," specifically as a defense against the Zeigarnik effect, where unfinished tasks keep pulling at attention all evening. Idle time is also load bearing here: unconscious thought research and attention restoration research both suggest the recovery is doing work of its own. [Source: "Rule #1: Work Deeply"]

- **Run every tool against the two or three activities that most support your actual goals.** List your top personal and professional goals, name the two or three activities that most support each, then keep only the tools that substantially help those specific activities. Farmer Forrest Pritchard sold his hay baler after tallying real opportunity costs, even though the surface benefits were obvious. [Source: "Rule #3: Quit Social Media"]

- **Quantify how shallow a task really is before you agree to it.** Newport's test is to ask how many months it would take to train a smart recent college graduate with no specialized background to do the task competently. Editing a nuanced academic paper scores long, building a routine slide deck scores short, regardless of how busy either one feels. [Source: "Rule #4: Drain the Shallows"]

- **Use the time your body is busy and your mind is free.** Newport built the habit of productive meditation walking across Boston during his MIT postdoc, working a single problem while commuting, showering or shoveling snow. The two failure modes he names are chasing unrelated thoughts and looping, where the mind circles material it already knows rather than pushing to the next step. [Source: "Rule #2: Embrace Boredom"]

## Don't

- **Don't rely on intention to beat the pull toward distraction.** A 2012 study by Wilhelm Hofmann and Roy Baumeister sampled over 7,500 real-time moments with beepers and found people fight the urge toward distraction almost constantly and win only about half the time against internet and television. Newport's conclusion is that routines minimizing the willpower spent on transitions matter more than resolve. [Source: "Rule #1: Work Deeply"]

- **Don't check email between tasks and call it a gap.** The cost is not the minutes, it is the residue: attention stays partly on the interrupted thread and the next task runs degraded. This is the mechanism behind Newport's whole objection to constant reachability, not a preference about inbox hygiene. [Source: "Chapter One: Deep Work Is Valuable"]

- **Don't let visible busyness stand in for output.** Absent a clear measure of value, knowledge workers default to answering fast and attending everything, which Newport calls busyness as a proxy for productivity. Atlantic Media's CTO needed a company wide survey plus salary and typing speed data just to estimate that email cost the company over a million dollars a year, which is exactly why the cost stays invisible. [Source: "Chapter Two: Deep Work Is Rare"]

- **Don't adopt a tool because it offers some benefit.** Newport names this the any-benefit mind-set, and it is his real target rather than internet use itself. Baratunde Thurston disconnected completely for 25 days and relapsed into heavy use almost immediately, which is why total abstinence is not the answer either. Thirty days of quiet non-use, then an honest verdict on whether the month was worse and whether anyone noticed. [Source: "Rule #3: Quit Social Media"]

- **Don't fill the leftover minutes with infotainment.** Reflexively reaching for entertainment sites in idle moments erodes the same concentration you are trying to train during work hours. Newport revives Arnold Bennett's idea of treating non-work hours as a day within a day, planned in advance, because structured leisure is what actually starves the reflex. [Source: "Rule #3: Quit Social Media"]

- **Don't plan more depth than the ceiling allows.** Deliberate practice research puts the daily cap at roughly four hours even for trained experts, so a schedule assuming eight hours of real depth is a schedule that will fail and then feel like a personal failure. Everything past the cap should be deliberately allocated, not accidentally consumed. [Source: "Rule #4: Drain the Shallows"]

- **Don't accept shallow obligations without a stated budget.** Newport recommends negotiating an explicit target, commonly in the 30 to 50 percent range, for how much of your time goes to shallow work, then using that agreed number as cover for declining low value obligations and restructuring recurring status meetings into results-only reporting. [Source: "Rule #4: Drain the Shallows"]

- **Don't trust your own estimate of where the time went.** A British television ratings study found viewers self-estimated 15 to 16 hours a week against a metered 28. Newport's answer is to hand-block every half hour of the workday on paper and openly revise the plan, sometimes repeatedly, as the day breaks it. [Source: "Rule #4: Drain the Shallows"]

- **Don't model your schedule on a senior executive's.** Jack Dorsey's fragmented, meeting-heavy, interruption-driven day is genuinely lucrative and has none of the marks of deep work. Newport's rule of specificity is that top executives are paid to be hard-to-automate reactive decision engines, which is a different job from the one most knowledge workers actually hold. [Source: "Chapter One: Deep Work Is Valuable"]

- **Don't wait for a better office to start.** The trends running against you (open floor plans, mandatory social presence, instant messaging) persist because the cost of distraction is unmeasurable, not because depth has been tested and found wanting. That is the same reason an individual who prioritizes depth gains a structural advantage: almost nobody else is doing it. [Source: "Chapter Two: Deep Work Is Rare"]

## The one line

Decide in advance when the deep hours happen, defend them with structure rather than resolve, and treat everything else in the week as a budget to be capped rather than a queue to be served.
