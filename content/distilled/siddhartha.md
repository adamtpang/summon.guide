# Siddhartha

This governs the decision of whose answer to take: a teacher's, a tradition's, a friend's, or none. The novel's argument is that wisdom cannot be handed from one person to another the way knowledge can, and that whatever a wise person tries to pass over as wisdom arrives at the other end sounding like foolishness. That argument is a problem for a page like this one, since a distillation is exactly the transfer the book says does not work, so what follows is not the lesson extracted from the story but what the story is observed to do: Siddhartha leaves everything arranged for him, masters asceticism and rejects it, meets the Buddha and refuses him, ruins himself in wealth, and ends up learning from a river and a ferryman. The Do section is short because the book's own demonstrations are few, and most of the weight sits in Hold.

## Do

- **Say your objection to the person's face rather than leaving quietly.** Siddhartha recognizes Gotama's perfection on sight, from the calm of his walk and his quietly hanging hand, and still seeks him out privately the next morning to name the one gap he sees: that the teaching presents the world as an unbroken chain of cause and effect except at the point where salvation itself breaks that chain, since it describes something Gotama experienced alone. Gotama does not dispute the logic. He warns him instead about cleverness and the thicket of opinions. [Source: "Gotama"]

- **Apprentice yourself to the thing you have been dismissing, under someone who actually practices it.** Having spent years treating the visible world as a veil, he chooses the courtesan Kamala as his teacher in love and lets her send him to the merchant Kamaswami for money and clothes. He arrives with nothing to offer but three skills learned as an ascetic, that he can think, wait, and fast, and those turn out to be exactly what makes him good at trade. [Source: "Kamala"]

- **Tell one person the whole of it and let them listen without answering.** After his son runs off, Siddhartha tells Vasudeva the entire story for the first time, the walk to the city, the burning wound, his envy of other fathers, and experiences the silent listening as though he were confessing to the river itself. Only after that does Vasudeva tell him to listen again, and the river's countless voices resolve into one sound. [Source: "Om"]

- **Let the person go whom you would rather die for.** Vasudeva tells him plainly that no father has ever spared a son the necessity of living his own life, and that even dying ten times over would not take the slightest part of the boy's destiny from him. Siddhartha chases him as far as the gate of Kamala's old garden, stands there for hours reliving his own youth, and then stops. [Source: "The Son"]

## Don't

- **Don't join the order because you have concluded the teaching is true.** Siddhartha finds nothing wrong with Gotama's doctrine and walks away from it anyway. Govinda takes refuge in the teachings that same night, and the last chapter finds him decades later, an elderly monk, still restlessly searching and asking a stranger for something to carry on his path. [Source: "Govinda"]

- **Don't mistake numbing for transformation.** Three years of Samana practice taught Siddhartha to fast for stretches of fifteen and twenty-eight days, to stand motionless in blazing sun and freezing rain, to slow his own heartbeat, and to meditate himself into a heron and a jackal carcass. His own verdict is that every one of these paths led away from the self and inevitably back to it, and he compares the whole discipline to an ox-cart driver drinking himself into oblivion, the only difference being which method gets respected. [Source: "With the Samanas"]

- **Don't attach yourself to someone else's path on the grounds that it is at least a path.** Govinda's devotion is described as total and one-directional from the first chapter: he loves Siddhartha's mind and will and has already decided to follow him as friend, companion, servant, spear-carrier, and shadow, wherever that turns out to be. He never once picks a direction of his own. [Source: "The Brahman's Son"]

- **Don't read your immunity to something as being above it.** Siddhartha succeeds at trade and at love precisely because he cannot be shaken by profit or loss, treats a ruined rice deal as worth the trip, and watches the merchants and gamblers around him with contempt. Kamala tells him after a night together that he does not love her or anyone, and he agrees, offering the theory that people with an inner refuge may simply be incapable of the one thing ordinary people have. [Source: "With the Childlike People"]

- **Don't use patience as a way of not letting go.** Siddhartha refuses to force or punish his grieving son, hoping gentleness will win him over, and Vasudeva names it directly: the endless gentle patience is its own kind of coercion, a daily reminder to the boy of how unlike his father he is expected to turn out. The boy eventually screams that he would rather become a robber and a murderer, then steals the ferrymen's coin basket and runs. [Source: "The Son"]

## Hold

- **Wisdom cannot be handed over, only knowledge can.** This is Siddhartha's hardest-won thought and he offers it as one: anything a wise man tries to pass over as wisdom sounds like foolishness to whoever receives it. The book then refuses to contradict itself, and closes not on an argument but on Govinda kissing Siddhartha's forehead and seeing a wordless flow of thousands of faces. Holding this changes what you expect a teacher, a book, or a page like this one to be able to do for you. Refusing it means treating every unabsorbed insight as a communication failure rather than as the ordinary condition. [Source: "Govinda"]

- **The ruined years were the path, not a detour from it.** Standing at the river ready to drown himself, stopped only by the syllable Om surfacing unbidden, he reviews everything and concludes that his proud, spiritual self had never actually died under asceticism, it had merely retreated into pride and thinking, and that only total immersion in greed, gambling, and despair could finish it. His own formulation is that he had to become a fool in order to be able to live again. Held, a wasted decade stops being a subtraction from the life. Not held, the arithmetic never comes out and the person you are is permanently owed an apology by the person you were. [Source: "By the River"]

- **Holding a goal narrows what you are able to find.** He tells the old, still-searching Govinda that a person fixed on searching often becomes unable to find anything, because having a goal decides in advance what counts as worth perceiving. Holding this changes what you do when a search has gone on too long. Refusing it means the answer to not having found it is always to search harder along the same line. [Source: "Govinda"]

- **The self was what you were fleeing, not what you were seeking.** Walking away from Gotama, he traces the loss to its cause: what he wanted from every teacher was the self, and what none of them could give him was the self, because he had been trying to capture it by dissecting and escaping it rather than facing it. The world changes in the same moment, from a deceptive veil to be seen through into the truth itself, and he compares his old approach to someone scorning the letters of a text while trying to read only its meaning. The elation lasts minutes before the cold terror of belonging to no caste at all. [Source: "Awakening"]

- **The refuge inside you may be the thing preventing you from loving anyone.** He and Kamala both have a peace they can retreat into at any hour, which he first reads as their shared advantage over the childlike people who toil and suffer over money and honor and small pleasures. Over the same years he watches those people with a growing envy for how much they care. Held, self-sufficiency stops being an unmixed good. Not held, the composure keeps reading as maturity right up until there is nobody left who is owed anything by you. [Source: "With the Childlike People"]

- **Your ordinary, unphilosophical grief is not beneath you.** After the boy goes, Siddhartha envies every parent he ferries across, and grieves that even thieves and murderers have children who love them while he does not. He notices the grief is exactly the childlike kind he used to dismiss, and the effect is that a mother's blind love and a father's vain pride start to strike him as worthy of veneration rather than contempt. Holding this makes the low feeling usable. Refusing it means the parts of you that hurt in unsophisticated ways stay quarantined from the parts you take seriously. [Source: "Om"]

- **Nobody can be spared their own life, including by you and including by your father.** Vasudeva states it about the son, and the river states it about Siddhartha: setting out toward the city to find the boy, he hears the river laughing and realizes with a shock that he is repeating precisely the grief his own father suffered when Siddhartha walked out. The pattern is not broken by anyone in the book, only recognized. Held, this converts a parent's or a mentor's failure into a description of the arrangement. Not held, every departure gets read as something you could have prevented with more care. [Source: "The Son"]

## The one line

You cannot be given the thing you are looking for, the years you consider wasted were where you were actually going, and the people you love have to go the same way alone.
