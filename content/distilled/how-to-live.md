# How to Live

This governs the largest question the book could have picked, and it refuses to answer it once. Twenty seven chapters each make a complete, unhedged case for a different way to live, and several of them directly attack the chapter before. The conclusion does not rank them or blend them. It says the duck is also a rabbit, calls the set an orchestra, and hands the composing back to you, which means the usable output of this book is not a rule but the ability to say which answer you are living by right now.

## Do

- **Say which answer you are currently conducting.** The closing line is that you are the composer and conductor, meaning the book deliberately declines to tell you which chapter to adopt, and the skill it is actually training is knowing which one fits the moment you are in. [Source: "Conclusion: The Weird Conclusion"]

- **Let each answer fully convince you before you move to the next.** The front matter asks for slow reading, one line at a time, and the chapters are written to be persuasive one at a time rather than compared side by side. Reading them as a menu of options is how the effect gets lost. [Source: "Conclusion: The Weird Conclusion"]

- **Judge a goal only by whether it makes today's action obvious.** Goals do not improve your future, they improve your present actions, and a goal's only job is to make the next thing you do clear. [Source: "Master Something"]

- **Use surprise as the test of whether you learned anything.** If nothing changed your mind, it was confirmation, not learning. The same test appears again as the reason to try actively to disprove your own beliefs. [Source: "Learn"] [Source: "Make a Million Mistakes"]

- **Keep a written log of mistakes and what each one taught you.** A mistake only counts as experience if you learn from it, and the rule attached is never to repeat one. [Source: "Make a Million Mistakes"]

- **Treat the missing skill as the actual problem.** Most life dissatisfaction is an unlearned skill rather than a character flaw, and health, money and relationships all come from learning specific ones. [Source: "Learn"]

- **Put the work out.** Calling yourself creative means nothing without output, and the closing measure is that your work shows who you were, not your intentions and not what you took in. [Source: "Create"]

- **Aim lower until you find something you can change today.** Fix what bothers you, and if you cannot, keep shrinking the target until there is a version you can actually start on now. [Source: "Make Change"]

- **Charge for what you make.** People do not value what is free, which is the reason given for charging even when the money is not needed, and the pricing rule elsewhere is to charge more than is comfortable to your current self-image. [Source: "Create"] [Source: "Get Rich"]

## Don't

- **Don't try to resolve the twenty seven into one philosophy.** The conclusion puts up the duck-rabbit illusion, the image you can only see one way at a time, and then refuses the framing: this is a duck and a bunny. Picking a winner is the one reading the book rules out. [Source: "Conclusion: The Weird Conclusion"]

- **Don't settle on the first plausible answer.** Confidence is usually ignorance, and the first suspect in a mystery is rarely the culprit. His image for it is that the strong swimmers are the ones who drown. [Source: "Learn"]

- **Don't wait for inspiration or for the right mood.** She comes only once you have shown you do not need her, and it is better to make something bad than nothing. [Source: "Create"]

- **Don't let a failure become an identity.** The people devastated by failure are the ones who did not expect it, and the title only attaches when someone quits, which is what ends the story. [Source: "Make a Million Mistakes"]

- **Don't file your emotional reaction as something that happened to you.** Nothing is good or bad, you reacted as if it was, and reactions are trainable rather than fixed, like a badly trained dog that bites. [Source: "Prepare for the Worst"]

## Where they disagree

The contradictions below are not gaps in the book. Each side is argued at full strength in its own chapter, with no hedging and no cross-reference, and the conclusion leaves them standing.

- **Cut every dependency, or accept that you are carried by others.** "Be Independent" says almost all misery is interpersonal, so exit: blame nothing outside yourself because whoever you blame holds power over you, keep friends at arm's length, have several romantic partners or none, own a business with many small customers so it runs without you. "Live for Others" says the opposite outright, that you cannot pull yourself up by your bootstraps and are ultimately lifted by those around you, that you cannot be healthy in a sick society, and that after twenty, friendship takes deliberate work. [Source: "Be Independent"] [Source: "Live for Others"]

- **Commit once and never revisit it, or never commit to anything.** "Commit" leans on the root of "decide," to cut off, and argues that commitment is what manufactures bestness: one home, one career, one marriage, deep local expertise, no reopening the question. "Reinvent Yourself Regularly" says change jobs and locations every year or two, avoid anything permanent including tattoos, and that labelling a person is like labelling the water in a river. "Let Randomness Rule" goes further and hands the decision to dice or an app, choosing a life where you choose nothing. [Source: "Commit"] [Source: "Reinvent Yourself Regularly"] [Source: "Let Randomness Rule"]

- **Act on the world, or stop acting.** "Make Change" says the world does not need more audience, that work is whatever you want to change, and that when the bad people are mad you are doing it right. "Master Something" demands never missing a day of practice. "Do Nothing" says nearly every problem is caused by doing something, that all actions are optional, that people act, create a problem, then act again to fix it, and that you should refuse decisions because the answer becomes obvious on its own. [Source: "Make Change"] [Source: "Master Something"] [Source: "Do Nothing"]

- **Live for your descendants, or only for this instant.** "Think Super-Long-Term" opens on Franklin's two hundred year trust, tells you to keep age-progressed photos of yourself and your family visible daily, and says never spend, only invest. "Do Whatever You Want Now" says a plan is only a prediction your past self made and you should not be bound by a stranger's guess, so never make plans and move like a frog on a lily pad. "Fill Your Senses" adds a third clock entirely: nothing twice, no home, no repeated route, live as if you go blind tomorrow. [Source: "Think Super-Long-Term"] [Source: "Do Whatever You Want Now"] [Source: "Fill Your Senses"]

- **Chase what is newest, or trust only what has already lasted.** "Chase the Future" says ownership binds you to the past, give away anything unused for a week, prioritize new people over old friends who hold you to who you used to be, and avoid anywhere that lives in the past. "Value Only What Has Endured" says be the last adopter of any technology, buy old and used, proudly have no opinion on current events, and never assume something old is outdated before you understand why it was built that way. [Source: "Chase the Future"] [Source: "Value Only What Has Endured"]

- **Avoid the one thing that could ruin you, or collect failures on purpose.** "Don't Die" states the asymmetry plainly: success needs everything to go right and failure needs one thing to go wrong, so do not try to be more right, just be less wrong, and treat a good life as a lack of negatives. "Make a Million Mistakes" says your growth zone is your failure zone, take on deliberately oversized challenges, and frequent mistakes are what keep a person from calcifying. [Source: "Don't Die"] [Source: "Make a Million Mistakes"]

- **Go narrow to the point of deformity, or keep every spoke of the wheel turning.** "Master Something" explicitly rejects the well-rounded ideal, telling you to skip the spouse, the kids, the small talk and normal sleep, because legendary achievers were never balanced, they were obsessive. "Balance Everything" answers that too much of a strength is a weakness, that a one-legged giant topples easily, and that the tool for a balanced life is the clock, scheduling every domain so you act on your highest self's goals rather than your passing mood. [Source: "Master Something"] [Source: "Balance Everything"]

- **Decide for yourself, or hand the deciding to a book.** "Learn" and the conclusion both put the judgment in your hands. "Follow the Great Book" argues the opposite: your heart has been hacked, nobody facing a life-or-death diagnosis actually wants the doctor to say "you decide," so pick one authoritative text, carry it everywhere, memorize it, make the rules unbreakable, and answer challenges with "the book says so," because rules exist to save you from deciding. [Source: "Follow the Great Book"] [Source: "Learn"] [Source: "Conclusion: The Weird Conclusion"]

## The one line

Do not look for the answer in here, look for which of the twenty seven you are living by this week, and be able to name it.
