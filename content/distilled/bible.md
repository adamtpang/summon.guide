# The Bible: Wisdom & Teaching

This is scripture and living faith for billions of people, and this page treats only the scoped, advice-oriented portion of it: Proverbs on speech, money, work, and character; Ecclesiastes on mortality and meaning; a selection of wisdom Psalms; and the Sermon on the Mount. It is not one voice. A king cataloguing what makes households prosper, a preacher reporting that outcomes are not reliably distributed, a psalmist in the middle of real fear, and a teacher on a hillside raising the standard past what anyone was already keeping are four different vantage points, and where they diverge that is written down below rather than averaged away. Nothing here settles whether any of it is true; the corpus is read here for what it instructs.

## Do

- **Say less, later, and softer than the impulse wants.** The book returns to the tongue more than to any other body part and calls death and life its power. In the multitude of words there is no shortage of sin, a soft answer turns away wrath while a harsh one stirs it up, and even a fool who holds his peace is counted wise. [Source: "Speech and the Tongue"]

- **Keep honest weights, in every transaction, including the small ones.** A false balance is called an abomination three separate times in different words, which is an unusually high repetition rate for anything in the book, and the same charge is repeated later against rulers so that power gets no exemption from it. [Source: "Wealth, Poverty, and Generosity"]

- **Repair the relationship before you do the religious act.** Someone mid-ritual who remembers a grievance is told to leave the gift right there at the altar and go make peace first. The instruction is paired with a debtor urged to settle on the way to court, before a judge, a prison, and a total loss make the outcome permanent. [Source: "Anger and Reconciliation"]

- **End a conflict before it has a shape.** The beginning of strife is compared to letting out water, and the advice follows immediately: leave off contention before it is meddled with. A second lever is named for conflicts already running, that where there is no wood the fire goes out, so where there is no talebearer the strife ceases. [Source: "Anger and Self-Control"]

- **Do the good thing where it cannot be seen.** The same test is applied to giving, praying, and fasting in a row: let the left hand not know what the right hand is doing, go into a private room and shut the door, and wash your face rather than disfigure it. The refrain after each public version is the same flat line, that they have their reward, meaning the visible act was already paid in the only currency it was seeking. [Source: "Private Devotion: Alms, Prayer, and Fasting"]

- **Clear your own eye before you offer to work on anyone else's.** The image is deliberately absurd, someone volunteering to remove a speck from a neighbor's eye while blind from a beam in their own. It does not forbid correction; it makes self-correction the prerequisite for seeing clearly enough to correct. [Source: "Judging Others and the Golden Rule"]

- **Act before the conditions are right, and spread the bet.** Whoever watches the wind will not sow and whoever regards the clouds will not reap, so waiting for certainty guarantees no harvest. The stated hedge is to give a portion to seven and also to eight, because you do not know what will go wrong. [Source: "Proverbs on Folly, Rule, and Risk"]

- **Do the work in front of you at full strength.** Whatever your hand finds to do, do it with your might, and the reason given is not virtue but a deadline: there is no work, device, knowledge, or wisdom in the grave. The same passage lists what living looks like concretely, eat your bread with joy, wear white, do not withhold oil from your head, live joyfully with the person you love. [Source: "Power, Death, and the Common Fate"]

- **Count your remaining days deliberately.** The request to number our days sits at the hinge of a psalm that gives a plain figure, seventy years and eighty by strength, and refuses to sentimentalize even the extension. The stated purpose of counting is to apply the heart to wisdom, and the psalm closes by asking twice in one verse that ordinary human work be allowed to last. [Source: "Numbering Our Days"]

- **Use whatever standing you have on behalf of people who have none.** The instruction to open your mouth for those who cannot speak and to plead the cause of the poor and needy comes as a mother's charge to a king, placed immediately after her warnings against wine and self-indulgence, so the discipline and the advocacy are presented as one argument. [Source: "The Virtuous Wife"]

## Don't

- **Don't guarantee someone else's debt.** Six separate passages warn against striking hands as surety for another person, an almost obsessive level of repetition, and the book treats it as one of its clearest routes to ruin. [Source: "Wealth, Poverty, and Generosity"]

- **Don't claim the seat or supply your own praise.** Do not put yourself forward in the presence of a king, because it is better to be told to come up than to be publicly moved down, and let another person praise you rather than your own mouth. The underlying claim is that exaltation grabbed rather than granted is unstable by nature. [Source: "Pride and Humility"]

- **Don't repeat the thing you were told.** Covering another person's fault is described as seeking love while repeating it separates close friends, and elsewhere a talebearer's words are compared to wounds that sink into the innermost parts. Confidentiality is treated as a condition of the relationship, not a courtesy inside it. [Source: "Friendship and Loyalty"]

- **Don't spend time with people who cannot govern their temper.** The warning is specific and gives its reason: do not make friendship with an angry man, lest you learn his ways and get a snare to your soul. Proximity to volatility is treated as instruction in volatility. [Source: "Anger and Self-Control"]

- **Don't take popularity or visible results as evidence.** The wide gate and the broad way are explicitly the ones many people take, and the narrow way is the one few find. The companion test for teachers is entirely results-based rather than credential-based, and even so the passage names people who prophesied and did many wonderful works and were told they were never known. [Source: "The Narrow Gate, False Prophets, and the Two Builders"]

- **Don't accumulate what you have no capacity to use.** The specific evil named is a person given riches, wealth, and honor who lacks the power to eat of it, so that a stranger consumes it instead, and the passage calls this worse than never having been born at all. [Source: "Wealth Without Enjoyment and the Better-Than Sayings"]

- **Don't expect money to arrive at a stopping point.** Whoever loves silver will not be satisfied with silver, nor whoever loves abundance with increase, stated as something close to a law. The observation attached is that a labouring man's sleep is sweet while abundance itself keeps the rich person awake. [Source: "Companionship, Power, and Caution"]

- **Don't borrow tomorrow's load to carry today.** Worry is undercut on the grounds that it does not work, since no one can add a cubit to their stature by taking thought, and the section closes by localizing the burden, that tomorrow will take thought for itself and today's own trouble is sufficient. [Source: "Treasure, Anxiety, and Trust"]

## Hold

- **Death arrives on the same terms regardless of how you lived.** The bleakest line in the corpus is that all things come alike to all, one event to the righteous and to the wicked, and the surrounding passage adds that there is no discharge in that war. The response drawn from it is not resignation but urgency. Holding this removes conduct's use as insurance. Refusing it means every misfortune has to be read as a verdict on someone's character. [Source: "Power, Death, and the Common Fate"]

- **Outcomes are not distributed by merit.** The race is not to the swift nor the battle to the strong, and time and chance happen to them all. The same chapter tells of a poor wise man whose wisdom saved a besieged city and whom nobody afterward remembered, and it still refuses to conclude that his wisdom was worth less. Held, this separates the value of doing something well from the odds of being rewarded for it. Not held, every unrewarded effort has to be re-examined for the mistake that must be in it. [Source: "Power, Death, and the Common Fate"]

- **There is a pattern and you are standing inside it, which is why you cannot see it.** The famous poem of fourteen paired opposites, birth and death, planting and uprooting, war and peace, is followed immediately by its own qualification: everything is made beautiful in its time, and the world is set in people's hearts, so that no one can find out the work from beginning to end. Held, the poem stops being a formula for control and becomes a statement of limits. Refusing it turns every unexplained season into a failure of interpretation. [Source: "A Time for Everything"]

- **The experiment has already been run by someone with more resources than you.** The Preacher builds houses, plants vineyards and orchards, digs pools to irrigate them, acquires singers and wealth and status, and withholds nothing his eyes desired, then reports that all of it was vanity and that he hated life. What he lands on instead is small and repeated: eat, drink, and let the soul enjoy good in its own labour, named explicitly as a gift rather than an achievement. Holding this saves you the run. Not holding it means you will make the same acquisition and reach the same page. [Source: "The Experiment in Pleasure and Work"]

- **Safety in these texts means composure inside the danger, never removal from it.** The table in Psalm 23 is set in the presence of enemies and the valley is walked through rather than around, with the rod and staff functioning as company rather than as weapons. Psalm 91 promises thousands falling on either side without the danger coming near, and Psalm 46 issues its command to be still only after two stanzas of nations raging and mountains carried into the sea. Held, you stop reading continued danger as evidence of abandonment. Not held, every unresolved threat reads as a broken promise. [Source: "The Shepherd"] [Source: "Courage Against Armies and Earthquakes"]

- **Every plan you are depending on dies with the person holding it.** The warning is political and specific: do not trust in princes, because a ruler's breath goes out and his plans perish in that same day. What the psalm offers as trustworthy is defined by a list of actions toward the powerless rather than by attributes, feeding the hungry, freeing prisoners, and caring for strangers, orphans, and widows. Held, this puts a ceiling on how much any institution or patron can be relied on. Refusing it means arranging your life around commitments that have a heartbeat. [Source: "Where Not to Put Your Trust"]

- **Being completely known is something to invite rather than endure.** The psalm works through a sequence of failed escapes, ascending to the heavens, descending to the grave, flying to the far side of the sea, and each is met with the same answer, already there, with darkness treated as no distinction at all. Its last two verses reframe the whole thing by asking to be searched and tried on purpose. Held, self-examination stops being a punishment. Not held, the parts of yourself you have not looked at stay in a category marked as safer unopened. [Source: "Fully Known"]

- **Envy of the people currently winning is a timescale error.** Psalm 37 names the reader's feeling outright, fretting over evildoers who are visibly doing well, and treats that feeling as the actual problem to work through rather than something to deny. Its method is not doctrine but reported observation, watching a wicked man spread himself out and then looking again later to find him gone. Held, you can judge a position on a longer horizon than the one you are standing on. Refusing it means every quarter's scoreboard is the final one. [Source: "Two Ways, and Patience with the Wicked"]

## Where they disagree

- **Does diligence reliably produce prosperity?** Proverbs says yes as a rule and returns to it constantly: the hand of the diligent makes rich, poverty arrives on the sluggard like a traveler, and the ant is held up as the model precisely because it provides for itself with no guide, overseer, or ruler. Ecclesiastes reports the opposite from direct observation, that the race is not to the swift nor bread to the wise, and that the poor wise man who saved the city was forgotten. Neither book is naive about the other's position: Proverbs itself hedges that it is the LORD's blessing that makes rich, and Ecclesiastes still insists wisdom excels folly as far as light excels darkness. What separates them is scope. Proverbs describes the tendency across many lives and Ecclesiastes reports what he watched happen inside particular ones, and the corpus keeps both. [Source: "Diligence and Laziness"] [Source: "Power, Death, and the Common Fate"]

- **Is wealth a reward, a danger, or beside the point?** Proverbs holds it as a real and God-honored outcome of honest work while insisting riches profit nothing in the day of wrath and that a good name is to be chosen above great riches, with Agur asking for neither poverty nor riches on the grounds that both have a specific failure mode. Ecclesiastes has no objection to wealth and a very sharp objection to unenjoyed wealth, calling a fortune a stranger ends up eating worse than an untimely birth. The Sermon reframes the question entirely, treating stored treasure as a symptom you read backward to find what a person actually values, and stating loyalty as structurally exclusive: you cannot serve God and mammon. These are three different questions about the same object, and flattening them loses all three. [Source: "Wealth, Poverty, and Generosity"] [Source: "Wealth Without Enjoyment and the Better-Than Sayings"] [Source: "Treasure, Anxiety, and Trust"]

- **What do you actually do about someone who has wronged you?** Proverbs is tactical: a soft answer, a gift given in secret to pacify anger, patience and a soft tongue that can break a bone, and simple avoidance of hot-tempered people. Psalm 37 counsels quitting anger before it turns into vengeance and refusing to answer scheming with scheming, on the grounds that reactive imitation loses even when the provocation is real. Psalm 139 does something else entirely in the middle of an otherwise tender poem, wishing the wicked dealt with and declaring hatred toward them. The Sermon goes furthest in the opposite direction, replacing proportionate retaliation with four scenarios that each add rather than withhold, the other cheek, the cloak as well as the coat, the second mile, and then widening into praying for people who are actively hostile. The corpus contains all four and does not reconcile them. [Source: "Anger and Self-Control"] [Source: "Two Ways, and Patience with the Wicked"] [Source: "Fully Known"] [Source: "Turning the Other Cheek and Loving Enemies"]

- **How much of the future should you plan for?** Proverbs holds effort and surrendered outcome together without choosing, acknowledging that a person plans their own way while insisting the steps are directed elsewhere. Ecclesiastes treats the future as genuinely unknowable and derives an active strategy from that, sow without waiting for the wind to settle and divide the portion across seven and eight. The Sermon sets a priority order instead and then cuts the horizon down to a single day, on the argument that tomorrow will take thought for itself. All three are answering a real question about planning and they do not land in the same place. [Source: "The Fear of the LORD and Trust"] [Source: "Proverbs on Folly, Rule, and Risk"] [Source: "Treasure, Anxiety, and Trust"]

## The one line

Speak less than you want to, deal honestly in small amounts, do the good part where nobody is watching, and work at full strength now, because the outcome was never yours to guarantee and the clock is not negotiable.
