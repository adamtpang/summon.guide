# How Do I Decide What to Work On?

Most advice about choosing a direction assumes the hard part is comparing options. It usually is not. The hard part is that the options are not really comparable, you cannot run the experiment twice, and the feeling of being unable to decide is often a symptom of something else entirely.

Here is what the material on this actually says, which is less tidy and more useful than "follow your passion."

## Stop looking for the best option

Derek Sivers inverts the standard advice directly: no option is inherently best, and commitment is what manufactures bestness rather than the reverse. Confusion, unfinished projects, and half-hearted relationships all trace back to one cause, which is not a shortage of good options but a shortage of commitment. [Source: "Commit"]

He leans on the etymology of the word decide, from the Latin for cutting off, to argue that choosing necessarily means closing alternatives, and that the narrowing is the source of the power. His formulation is that sacrificing your alternate selves is what gives the remaining self its force.

The same chapter is unusually blunt about the passion question: people fail by never committing to anything, not by picking the wrong thing, because passion arrives after you start getting good. [Source: "Master Something"]

That does not mean pick anything at random. It means the search for certainty before commitment is itself the trap.

## Test the option against your own resistance, not your reasoning

Lulie Tanett makes an argument that quietly resolves a lot of decision paralysis. She notices that David Deutsch's fun criterion, Marie Kondo's spark joy, and Sivers' own hell yeah or no are three descriptions of the same underlying test: whether an option produces internal alignment or internal conflict among your own tacit, not-yet-articulated ideas. [Source: "The Fun Criterion"]

Because those three frameworks come from unrelated fields (epistemology, home organization, and startup advice), she treats their convergence as evidence that the pattern is real rather than a coincidence of vocabulary.

The practical use is diagnostic. When a plan produces dread, resistance, or a flat "meh," that is information about an unresolved conflict, not a character flaw to override. If a large part of you is quietly objecting to the plan, the objection is data about the plan.

Sivers' own version of the filter is the one the book is named after: most people fill their lives with half-hearted commitments and are then too busy to take the real opportunity when it shows up. Saying no to anything short of a genuine yes is what makes the eventual yes powerful. [Source: "Saying No, Part 1"]

## Ask what you hate not doing

"What do you love" almost never produces an actionable answer. Sivers suggests the inverse, a double-negative question: what makes you feel depressed, annoyed, or off-track when you do not do it? He argues this is a sharper indicator of what is genuinely worth doing than the vaguer feeling of love. [Source: "What's Worth Doing?, Part 1"]

The same section supplies a useful map of how life plans fail, as four incomplete combinations of happy, smart, and useful:

- Smart and useful but not happy: prestige and money at any personal cost.
- Happy and smart but not useful: the self-help addict optimizing only their own life.
- Happy and useful but not smart: well-meant effort aimed at the wrong target.
- Just happy: the contented fisherman who never builds anything.

A satisfying plan needs all three at once. His related structural advice is to keep a well-chosen, well-paying job for stability and pursue the creative work seriously without needing it to sell, so neither half has to carry every need alone.

He also observes that creators are terrible judges of their own work, since everyone's own ideas feel obvious to them, and cites songwriters who nearly did not record what became their hit songs. The thing you dismiss as unremarkable is often the thing worth doing.

## If the work is a business, find the thing others cannot see

Peter Thiel's version of the question is narrower but sharper. A great company is built on a secret: an important truth that is hard to find but not impossible. A conventional truth cannot give you an edge, because everyone already agrees with it. [Source: "Chapter 8: Secrets"]

He splits secrets into two kinds, secrets of nature and secrets about people, and suggests the best place to look is a field that matters but has never been standardized or institutionalized. His example is nutrition: it affects everyone, is barely a real university major, has few recent rigorous studies, and produced a food pyramid he argues was shaped more by lobbying than by science.

He also names four social forces that erode belief that secrets exist at all: incrementalism (schools reward small credentialed steps, not overreaching curiosity), risk aversion (a secret is unvetted by definition, so pursuing one risks being lonely and wrong), elite complacency (why look, if you can collect rents on what is already known), and the sense that a globalized world is already fully mapped.

The failure mode this prevents is visible in his cleantech post-mortem. More than $50 billion went into the sector, and over 40 solar manufacturers went bankrupt or closed in 2012 alone. His diagnosis is that most of those companies answered none of his seven questions, and that on the secret question in particular they all cited the same widely agreed justification, that the world needs cleaner energy. A socially approved motive everyone already accepts is not a reason to think you will succeed. [Source: "Chapter 13, Part 1: Seeing Green and the Seven Questions"]

## Prefer a definite plan over a hopeful one

Thiel's other contribution here is a two-by-two: definite versus indefinite, crossed with optimist versus pessimist. His diagnosis of America since roughly 1982 is indefinite optimism, the belief that the future will be better with no specific plan for how, which he sees in finance, in polling-driven politics, and in a research culture that experiments with things that might work rather than building on definite theories. [Source: "Chapter 6: You Are Not a Lottery Ticket"]

His counterexamples are concrete multi-year plans: the Empire State Building, the Golden Gate Bridge, the Manhattan Project, the Apollo program. His model definite optimist is Steve Jobs, whose sequenced plans (iPod, then iPhone, then iPad) were dismissed by analysts at launch.

He concedes the luck-versus-skill question cannot be settled scientifically, since no company can be rerun as a controlled experiment. His point is that the forward-looking question is the useful one: do you expect your future to be designed or reacted to?

## Choose an explanation, not a preference

David Deutsch supplies the deepest version of this, and it changes what "deciding" means.

His argument, made in the context of voting systems but general in scope, is that decision-making is not a process of weighing fixed preferences among fixed options. It is a creative process of generating and criticizing explanations. To choose an option rationally is to choose its explanation. [Source: "Choices"]

This has a direct consequence for the halfway option, the compromise plan, the "I will do both part time" answer. Good explanations are hard to vary, which means they cannot be smoothly blended, and something halfway between two good explanations is usually worse than either one separately. Worse, when a compromise fails, nobody actually believed in it, so nothing gets learned and no explanation gets abandoned.

What makes an explanation good, in his account, is that it is hard to vary while still accounting for what it claims to account for. A bad explanation is one whose details could be swapped for other details without changing anything. The test applies to your reasons for a career the same way it applies to a scientific theory: if you could substitute a different justification without noticing, you do not have a reason, you have a story. [Source: "The Reach of Explanations"]

He also has the most useful thing to say about difficulty. A hard problem is not one that is unlikely to be solved. Difficulty is often exactly what motivates people to solve it, which is his reading of Kennedy's 1962 line about choosing to go to the moon not because it is easy but because it is hard, where success required knowledge that did not yet exist. [Source: "Optimism"]

## Judge the decision, not the result

Annie Duke's contribution is a guard against the thing that will happen after you choose. Poker players call it resulting: treating a good outcome as proof of a good decision and a bad outcome as proof of a bad one. Her running example is Pete Carroll's pass call on the one-yard line in Super Bowl XLIX, a well-reasoned bet that lost and was declared the worst call in Super Bowl history. [Source: "Chapter 1: Life Is Poker, Not Chess"]

In her consulting work, when she asks executives for their best and worst decisions of the year, she reliably gets their best and worst results instead. Nobody volunteers a well-reasoned choice that flopped or a reckless one that got lucky.

The metaphor is load-bearing. Chess has no hidden information and little luck, so outcomes track decision quality tightly. Poker does not, and neither does a career. If you pick a direction and it goes badly, that is not by itself evidence that you picked wrong.

## The conclusion you probably do not want

Sivers ends How to Live with twenty-seven chapters, each arguing a different philosophy of life with total conviction, and a conclusion only a few sentences long. He opens it with the duck-rabbit illusion, the image you can see as one or the other but never both at once, and then rejects that framing for his own book: it is a duck and a rabbit. The second image is an orchestra, and the final line hands the work back to the reader as composer and conductor. [Source: "Conclusion: The Weird Conclusion"]

Independence and living for others, mastery and constant reinvention, doing nothing and pursuing pain: none of them is meant to cancel the others out. The skill is knowing which one fits the current moment of your life, and that skill is yours, not the book's.

Which is a way of saying the question "what should I work on" has no permanent answer, only a current one, and the current one is answerable today.

## Talk to the guide

Derek Sivers is the right guide here because he has already argued every side of this question in print and refuses to sell you a single answer. He will push you off "what do I love" and onto "what do you hate not doing," and he will notice quickly if you are collecting options instead of committing to one.

Expect him to be uninterested in your plan's cleverness and very interested in whether anyone has actually asked you for it. His standing advice to people with a business idea and no customers is to skip the name, the website, and the system, and go find real people whose problem you can solve one at a time. [Source: "What's Worth Doing?, Part 2"]
