# How Do I Get My First Paying Customer?

The gap between "I built something" and "someone paid me" is where most projects quietly end. It is not usually a product problem. It is that nobody has yet been asked, directly, by a human being, to hand over money.

This is a walkthrough of how first customers actually arrive, drawn from founders who documented the process while it was happening.

## Understand that you are the sales team

There is no stage where a founder gets to skip this. Gustaf Alströmer, the YC partner who was previously at Airbnb, argues that talking to customers and selling are the same skill, which is why founders cannot outsource either one. Startups that wait for a good product to sell itself mostly do not take off. [Source: "How to Get Your First Customers | Startup School"]

Tyler Bosmeny, who co-founded Clever, is blunter about the mismatch between the myth and the reality. Sales is not a charming golf-course routine. In the early days it looks like one co-founder in a cramped call room on a broken chair. At Clever, one of the three founders peeled off to sell nearly full time before the product even existed. [Source: "How to Sell by Tyler Bosmeny"]

The corollary is that your lack of sales experience is not the disqualifier you think it is. Bosmeny's claim is that founder passion and real problem expertise beat a hired salesperson's technique, and that the best salespeople he shadowed talked roughly 30 percent of the time and listened 70 percent. Listening is the part you can already do.

## Interview before you build, and score who you talk to

Eric Migicovsky, who founded Pebble, gives the most usable interview discipline in the corpus. Never pitch. Never ask hypotheticals. Ask about specifics that already happened: what was the hardest part, tell me about the last time this came up, what have you already tried, what did you dislike about it. Generic enthusiasm ("I love this!") is bad data and should be discarded. [Source: "Eric Migicovsky - How to Talk to Users"]

His method for choosing a first customer is a three-axis score: how much the problem costs them today, how often they hit it, and how much budget or authority they have to fix it. Someone who feels the pain constantly but cannot authorize a purchase is not your first customer, however sympathetic they are.

Two practical notes from the same talk. Ten to fifteen minutes is often enough per interview, and your first five to ten teach you more than trying to batch a hundred. And one YC company selling to firefighters got further by physically showing up at fire stations than by cold emailing, walking away with dozens of short meetings.

Jared Friedman's framing of where good ideas live is worth holding alongside this: roughly 70 percent of YC's top 100 companies had their idea occur organically rather than through deliberate brainstorming, and the strongest signal that a problem is acute is when the current alternative is literally nothing. [Source: "How to Get and Evaluate Startup Ideas | Startup School"]

## Ship the embarrassing version

Michael Seibel's rule for scope is to hold the problem and the customer tightly, and hold the solution loosely. If the first attempt fails, change the solution, not the problem. [Source: "Michael Seibel - How to Plan an MVP"]

The examples in that talk are the point. Airbnb's first version had no in-app payments (hosts and guests exchanged cash in person) and no map view. Twitch launched as Justin.tv: one person's low-resolution livestream, zero video games. Early Stripe had no bank deals, and its founders flew out to integrate the product for each new user themselves.

Marc Lou's version of the same lesson is more literal. Before building his escape-room lead-generation tool, a marketer friend told him to sell it first. One cold call produced his first paying customer, before any code existed. [Source: "The Underdog: From $10/hr to $1.5M/Year"]

Andre Haykal Jr. ran ListKit as a manually fulfilled service before writing software at all: customers filled in a form saying how many leads they wanted, and the team delivered them by hand within 24 hours using an existing agency process. No development cost, real feedback, real revenue. [Source: "I Built A $1M SaaS In 87 Days"]

## Sell to whoever is easiest, not whoever is biggest

Alströmer's guidance is to go after the easiest prospects rather than the most impressive logo. Other startups make better early customers than large companies because they decide fast and have no procurement bureaucracy, and people already in your network close more readily than cold strangers. [Source: "How to Get Your First Customers | Startup School"]

The outbound email that works is short (six to eight sentences), plain text with no HTML, jargon-free, and ends with exactly one ask. Bosmeny's prospecting channels are the same shape: personal network (which extends further through friends of friends than founders assume), industry conferences (which he calls the most underrated channel, worth pre-booking meetings in 30-minute blocks), and short personalized cold email. [Source: "How to Sell by Tyler Bosmeny"]

Karthik Shanadi started Greek House with $500 in a fraternity common room, selling custom apparel by text message and campus meetings, and did $50,000 in year one while still a full-time student. The channel was people he could physically reach. [Source: "He Turned $500 Into $10M"]

## Charge, and skip the free trial

Both YC sales talks land in the same place on this: do not run long free trials for a B2B product. Use a money-back guarantee or a month-to-month opt-out instead. Bosmeny says he has never used a free trial at any company, preferring annual contracts with a 30-day no-penalty exit, because a trial secures neither commitment nor revenue. [Source: "How to Sell by Tyler Bosmeny"]

Haykal made the same call at ListKit, arguing free trials mainly attract freebie seekers while a guarantee still captures the cash up front. [Source: "I Built A $1M SaaS In 87 Days"]

On the number itself: Clever's original $100,000 contract price started as a guess and was iterated as deals closed, and Bosmeny's rule is that if a sale closes easily, roughly double the price on the next prospect, because founders systematically underprice.

If you want a more structured method, YC's B2B pricing talk suggests writing a value equation with your champion inside the customer: the actual cost saved, time saved, or revenue added, in defensible dollars, then pricing at roughly a quarter to a half of that so the customer keeps the majority. If you genuinely cannot determine a price, start from a comparable and raise it about 50 percent with each new prospect until you start losing more than a quarter of deals on price alone. [Source: "How To Price For B2B | Startup School"]

Alströmer adds a signal worth taking seriously: a prospect who will not pay anything is a reason to go find a better-fit customer, not a reason to drop your price.

## Pick one channel and get obsessed with it

Scalable channels are almost never how the first customers arrive, even when they later dominate. That is Alströmer's point about Airbnb: the earliest users came from manual, one-to-one work, not from any of the channels Airbnb is known for now.

But once you are past the first handful, the pattern in the Starter Story interviews is consistent: one channel, worked obsessively, beats five worked casually.

- Diego Roshardt grew AppAlchemy to $17,000 MRR in about four months with under 100 followers on X and $0 in marketing spend, purely through value-first Reddit posts that mentioned the product only near the end, cross-posted across a large list of relevant subreddits. He built the MVP in roughly two weeks and started marketing immediately. [Source: "How I Used Reddit to Hit $17K MRR (With ZERO Audience)"]
- Miquel Palet took Late from zero to $40,000 MRR in seven months with no social media at all, targeting bottom-of-funnel keywords with roughly 300 to 800 monthly searches and running Google Ads against the same terms. His closing advice is to stop trying to be everywhere. [Source: "Zero to $40K/Month With One Marketing Channel (No Social Media)"]
- Rob Hallam shipped five products over two and a half years that made a combined $0. His breakthrough was a vulnerable post about those failures; a single reply suggesting he start an agency led directly to a $3,000 client. [Source: "How I Used Twitter to Hit $10K/Month"]

## Expect the no's, and count them

Bosmeny's most useful reframe comes from Everett Rogers' adoption curve: the innovators, the people willing to buy from an unproven startup with no track record, are about 2.5 percent of any market. That means reaching roughly 100 companies to find a couple of real buyers. Rejection stops feeling personal once it is just arithmetic. [Source: "How to Sell by Tyler Bosmeny"]

He also notes that a real early Clever deal took about two months of unanswered emails, silence, and weekly follow-up to close a $100,000 contract. Persistent, personalized follow-up is normal, not pestering.

Alströmer's warning is about the wrong conclusion: founders who send too few outbound messages and get zero customers decide "sales does not work for our product," when they never sent enough volume to reach a meaningful conversion rate. Track the whole funnel with real numbers, including onboarding, which he calls the step founders most often forget and the reason closed customers quietly churn.

And when the numbers are bad, Michael Seibel and Dalton Caldwell's diagnostic is worth keeping nearby: quitting after one bad signal is excess pessimism, and ignoring a stack of negative evidence is magical thinking. Both are fatal, and the job is to hold hope and doubt at once. [Source: "The Two Mindsets That Can KILL Your Startup"]

## What the first sale is actually worth

David Senra's read across roughly 400 founder biographies is that the founders who last sold the product themselves, often before it was finished. He points to Michael Dell starting with around $1,000 and still owning about 70 percent at IPO, and argues the modern instinct to raise money before proving the product trades control for capital unnecessarily. [Source: "The Obsession That Creates Enduring Companies | David Senra Interview"]

The first paying customer is not revenue. It is the first piece of evidence that the thing you believe is true about the world might actually be true.

## Talk to the guide

Todd Graves is the right guide for this because he had no shortcut available. His business professor gave his chicken-finger concept the worst grade in the class, every bank in Louisiana turned him down, and he funded the first store with 95-hour boilermaker weeks and a season fishing sockeye salmon in Alaska. He treats sales as the founder's personal job and lives by the line that sales cures all woes. [Source: "The Obsession That Creates Enduring Companies | David Senra Interview"]

Expect him to ask two uncomfortable questions: what exactly are you refusing to sell, and how badly do you actually want this. He will not accept "I am still building" as an answer.
