## Key lessons

- Writing on April 28, 1971, Kissinger says the West Pakistani military would probably regain the main towns and roads, but that physical control would not restore food distribution or normal life, because that required Bengali cooperation.
- He predicts that suppressing the resistance would leave widespread hatred in East Pakistan, and that what lay ahead was a transition to greater autonomy and perhaps eventual independence. He writes that without restored services, large scale starvation seemed unavoidable.
- He notes tension between India and Pakistan at its highest since 1965, and expects India to train and equip a Bengali resistance force. He records that China had sided with West Pakistan for the moment.
- He identifies US leverage: economic aid, multiplied by US leadership of the World Bank aid consortium, was crucial to West Pakistan, and military supply, though small, was an important symbol.
- Option 1 was to back whatever Yahya chose to do, continuing aid and food shipments without conditions on how food was distributed. Option 2 was neutrality, delaying aid and deferring lethal military deliveries. Option 3 was to help Yahya end the war and reach an arrangement leading toward East Pakistani autonomy, without withholding aid now to apply pressure.
- He recommends Option 3. He says Option 1 would encourage West Pakistan to prolong the situation, and Option 2 would be publicly defensible but would damage relations with West Pakistan without helping the East.
- He names the risk in his own recommendation: the settlement effort might fail, leaving the United States alienated from the 600 million people of India and East Pakistan and still unable to move the West Pakistani government.
- Nixon approved Option 3 and added a handwritten note to all hands: "Don't squeeze Yahya at this time." A footnote records, from a note by Haig, that Kissinger had suggested the President include a note to that effect.
- The memo treats the crackdown mainly in terms of control, aid, and regional alignment. It does not describe the violence against civilians in detail, and the editors' notes say nothing here about the secret channel to China that then ran through Yahya.
