## Key lessons

- He says the United States no longer has the predominance it had after the war and now faces the ordinary problem of nations: applying limited means to carefully defined ends, with patience instead of overwhelming force.
- He observes that people living through upheaval see a string of unconnected events, that symptoms hide trends, and that the urgent tends to crowd out the important. A useful debate, he says, compares what is possible and at what pace, not lists of what is desirable.
- He defines foreign policy as relations among sovereign states, where disagreements are settled only by negotiation or by power. A nation's values define what is just, its strength what is possible, and its domestic structure what can be sustained.
- He states a paradox of peace: one side's attempt to impose absolute justice looks like absolute injustice to the others, and total security for one means insecurity for the rest. Stability therefore rests on every party being partly satisfied and partly dissatisfied.
- He warns against both extremes. A policy that is only pragmatic has no direction or standard others can judge it by, while an excessively moralistic one can become quixotic or dangerous and blocks accommodation.
- He contrasts the critic and the policymaker. The policymaker acts in "a fog of incomplete knowledge", without information the analyst will have later, is answerable for disaster as well as success, and often must settle for the gradual and compromise with others and with himself.
- He stresses timing: opportunities cannot be stored up and usually do not return, and new relationships are fragile and should not be uprooted repeatedly to check on them.
- On the Soviet Union he sets conditions for coexistence (no bid for predominance, no use of relaxed tensions to weaken alliances or inflame trouble spots) and questions making Soviet domestic change a formal precondition for trade, calling it a real moral dilemma with serious concerns on both sides. The editors note he had told Gromyko beforehand that he would oppose the Jackson-Vanik amendment publicly in this speech.
- He argues that power is no longer of one kind: military, economic, and political strength have come apart, and in the nuclear age small gains in military power no longer convert into political advantage. He calls stability a bridge to human aspirations, not an end in itself.
