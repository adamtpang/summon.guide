## Key lessons

- The memo prepares Nixon for a National Security Council meeting on Chile. Kissinger calls Allende's election one of the most serious challenges the United States had faced in the hemisphere, with effects reaching Latin America, the developing world, and relations with the Soviet Union.
- He lists the risks he sees: loss of about one billion dollars of US investment, possible default on about 1.5 billion dollars of debt, Chile as a leader of opposition in the inter-American system, a Soviet and Cuban foothold, and the example of an elected Marxist government influencing other countries, with Italy named.
- He states the difficulty openly. Allende was elected legally, had legitimacy in Chile and abroad, and the United States was on record for self-determination and non-intervention, so acting against him would be costly and would test the credibility of US rhetoric.
- He argues that an independent socialist Chile that looked respectable would be more dangerous to US interests than an obvious Soviet satellite, because it would be harder to rally others against and its example could spread.
- He presents the agencies as split. The State Department favored a working relationship, judging that pressure would be ineffective and counterproductive. The Defense Department, the CIA, and some in State favored acting while Allende was weakest, either with open hostility or with quiet pressure behind a posture he describes as "correct, but cold".
- His own judgment is that doing nothing carried greater dangers than trying to do something. He recommends opposing Allende as strongly as possible and doing everything possible to keep him from consolidating power, in a style that made the United States appear to be reacting.
- He acknowledges uncertainty in the same memo: the agencies disagreed on how inevitable the feared outcome was, and he grants that pressure might hand Allende a nationalist issue, damage US credibility, and still fail.
- He urges Nixon to depart from his usual practice and state his position unmistakably at the meeting, warning that otherwise policy would drift toward accommodation. A footnote records that he had the meeting postponed a day and told a White House aide that Chile could become the administration's worst failure.
- The document is a recommendation, not a record of what was done afterward. The volume's own chapter headings describe US intervention in the confirmation of the Chilean president in autumn 1970 and a cool, correct response to the Allende administration from November 1970.
