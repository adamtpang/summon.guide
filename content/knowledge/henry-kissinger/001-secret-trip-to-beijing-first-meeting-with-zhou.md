## Key lessons

- He tells Zhou that it is unusual for him to speak from written notes, but that he wrote out his opening because of the importance of the occasion and so that President Nixon could see what he would say. Zhou remarks that the American side arrived with a thick book, which the editors identify as the briefing books prepared by the NSC staff.
- He explains the secrecy directly: Nixon wanted the mission kept secret until after the meeting so the two sides could talk without bureaucracy and with the widest latitude. Later he says the exchange worked because it ran through President Yahya Khan of Pakistan and not through the ambassadorial channel in Warsaw.
- He frames the meeting as the first between American and Chinese leaders on a footing of equality, acknowledges deep ideological differences, and proposes that both sides let history judge who is right while cooperating in the meantime on shared concerns.
- He lays out two purposes (arranging a Nixon visit, and laying groundwork on substance) and a seven item agenda: Taiwan, Indochina, relations with the Soviet Union and Japan, South Asia, a secure communications channel, arms control, and anything the Chinese wished to raise.
- Before being asked, he addresses what he knows to be a Chinese fear, saying the United States will not collude with other countries against China and will not take major steps affecting Chinese interests without discussing them first.
- On Taiwan he splits the problem in two. He offers that the two thirds of US forces there tied to the Indochina war would leave within a set period after that war ended, with the rest reduced as relations improved, and he says the United States does not advocate "two Chinas" or support the Taiwan independence movement.
- He is explicit about limits: formal recognition of the People's Republic as the sole government of China was not possible in the next year and a half, and he says there is no sense in the two sides deluding themselves. He argues that even correct steps sometimes have to be taken gradually.
- On Vietnam he tells Zhou the United States is prepared to set a date for complete withdrawal in exchange for a ceasefire and release of prisoners, will not take part in overthrowing the Saigon government, and will not come back if that government later falls. Zhou holds the United States responsible for enlarging the war into Cambodia and Laos and presses for unconditional withdrawal; the two do not agree.
- He describes the Nixon approach as dealing with specific communist states on the basis of their actions and not as an abstract crusade, and says the defense of distant countries must first be their own responsibility. He also argues that the US defense tie with Japan restrains Japanese rearmament, which he presents as a shared interest with China.
