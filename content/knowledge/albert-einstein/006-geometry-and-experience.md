## Key lessons

- He begins with a puzzle: how can mathematics, a product of human thought independent of experience, fit the objects of reality so well?
- His short answer is that, as far as mathematical laws refer to reality, they are not certain, and as far as they are certain, "they do not refer to reality".
- He credits axiomatics with making this clear. In the modern view, axioms are free creations of the human mind and act as implicit definitions (he cites Schlick), so words like point and straight line are empty conceptual schemes within pure mathematics.
- He notes that geometry nevertheless arose from the need to learn about real things, as the word itself (earth measuring) shows. To say anything about real objects, the empty framework must be coordinated with practically rigid bodies. The result he calls practical geometry, a natural science and in his words the oldest branch of physics.
- He says this view mattered to him personally: without it he could not have formulated the theory of relativity, because it let him reason that in a rotating reference system rigid bodies do not follow Euclid's rules.
- He presents the opposing view of Poincare fairly: since no real body is perfectly rigid, only geometry together with physical laws can be tested, so one could always keep Euclidean geometry and change the physics. He grants that, in the long view, Poincare is right.
- He still holds his position for the present stage of physics: measuring rods and clocks must be used as independent ideas because theory cannot yet construct them from first principles, and a rod's physical state can be specified accurately enough to stand in for the rigid body.
- On this view, whether space is Euclidean, and whether the universe is spatially finite, are physical questions to be settled by experience, not by convention. He points to the sharpness of spectral lines as evidence for the assumption that equal lengths and equal clock rates stay equal everywhere.
- He ends with a method for imagining the unfamiliar. Using paper discs on a globe and their shadows on a plane, then spheres that grow as they move outward, he builds a picture of a finite yet unbounded space, to show that human visualisation need not give up before non-Euclidean geometry.
