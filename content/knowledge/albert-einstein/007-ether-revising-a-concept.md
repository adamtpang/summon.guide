## Key lessons

- He begins by asking why physicists ever placed an ether beside ordinary matter. He finds the cause in the discomfort with action at a distance in Newton's gravitation, and in the wave properties of light, both of which seemed to call for a medium filling space.
- He recounts how the concept changed as theory advanced. The ether was first pictured as a quasi-rigid solid carrying light waves. Maxwell and his followers could not find a satisfactory mechanical model for it, and physicists gradually accepted electric and magnetic fields as fundamental without a mechanical explanation.
- He describes Lorentz's step as a simplification: Lorentz took mechanical qualities away from the ether and electromagnetic qualities away from matter. Einstein remarks, playfully, that immobility was the only mechanical property Lorentz left it.
- He states what special relativity changed: it removed that last property. Keeping an ether at rest in one reference system, when all uniformly moving systems are physically equivalent, creates an asymmetry in the theory with no counterpart in experience, which he finds unacceptable.
- He considers the obvious next position, that the ether does not exist at all, and then rejects the idea that relativity forces it. More careful reflection, he says, shows one may keep an ether as long as no state of motion is ascribed to it.
- He offers what he calls a halting comparison: waves on water. If nothing but the changing shape of the surface could ever be observed, with no floats to track particles, there would be no ground to think of water as made of moving particles, yet it could still be called a medium.
- He gives the argument for keeping the concept. Denying the ether amounts to saying empty space has no physical qualities, but mechanics shows otherwise, since rotation has real effects. He reviews Newton's absolute space and Mach's alternative, and notes that Mach's version is itself shaped by matter.
- In general relativity, he says, the metrical qualities of space-time vary from place to place and are partly conditioned by matter, so the ether concept regains a clear content, though very different from the old mechanical one. He concludes that in this theory "space without ether is unthinkable".
- He is open about what remains unknown: the role of this new ether in future physics, whether it shares in the structure of elementary particles, and whether gravitation and electromagnetism can be unified. He doubts Weyl's attempt will hold up and allows that quantum facts may set limits to field theory.
