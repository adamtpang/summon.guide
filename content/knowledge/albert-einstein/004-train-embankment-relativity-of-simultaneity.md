## Key lessons

- He keeps the same simple scene used earlier in the book and adds one thing: a very long train moving along the rails at constant speed. Passengers treat the train as their reference body, and the same definition of simultaneity can be applied on the train as on the embankment.
- He asks one sharp question: are two lightning strokes that are simultaneous relative to the embankment also simultaneous relative to the train? He says the answer must be no.
- He reasons from the definition. Simultaneous for the embankment means the light from A and B meets at the midpoint M. The train has its own midpoint M', which coincides with M when the flashes occur (judged from the embankment) but is moving.
- Seen from the embankment, the observer at M' is hurrying toward the light from B and riding ahead of the light from A, so he sees the flash from B first. Observers on the train must conclude that B happened earlier than A.
- His conclusion: every reference body "has its own particular time", and a statement of the time of an event has no meaning unless the reference body is given.
- He points out that physics before relativity had always tacitly assumed that time statements were absolute, independent of the motion of the reference body. The assumption had never been stated, only used.
- He shows what dropping it achieves. The conflict between the law of the propagation of light and the principle of relativity (set out in Section VII) disappears, so neither well-supported principle has to be given up.
- He traces the error back to its source. The classical rule for adding velocities (Section VI) assumed that a second on the train is a second on the embankment, which can no longer be claimed.
- He notes that the same argument rested on a second tacit assumption, about distance, and takes it up in the next section. Applied today: when two things you have good reason to believe seem to contradict each other, look for the unstated assumption that both arguments share before discarding either.
