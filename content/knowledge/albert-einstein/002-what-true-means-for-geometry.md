## Key lessons

- He starts from the reader's own confidence. Most readers remember Euclid from school, "with more respect than love", and would look down on anyone who called one of its propositions untrue.
- He then unsettles that confidence with one question: what do you mean when you say these propositions are true?
- He describes how geometry works: it starts from concepts such as plane, point and straight line and from simple axioms, and every other proposition is derived from the axioms by accepted logical steps.
- So a proposition is "true" in geometry only in the sense that it follows from the axioms. The question of truth is pushed back onto the axioms themselves.
- He argues that asking whether the axioms are true cannot be answered by geometry and, taken strictly, has no meaning. Pure geometry deals only with the logical connection of its ideas, not with their relation to objects of experience.
- He explains why people still feel geometry is true: its ideas correspond to more or less exact objects in nature, and those objects are what gave rise to the ideas in the first place. Seeing a distance as two marks on a practically rigid body is a deep habit of thought.
- He makes the step explicit. Add the single proposition that two points on a practically rigid body always mark the same distance wherever the body is moved, and geometry becomes a set of statements about how rigid bodies can be placed. It is then a branch of physics.
- Only in that form can one properly ask whether a geometrical proposition is true, meaning whether it holds for the real things associated with the ideas (roughly, for a construction with ruler and compasses).
- He adds that conviction in this kind of truth rests only on rather incomplete experience. He assumes it for now and warns that the general theory of relativity will show its limits.
