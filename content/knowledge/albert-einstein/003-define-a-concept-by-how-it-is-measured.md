## Key lessons

- He sets up a concrete case: lightning strikes the rails at two distant places, A and B, and he asserts that the two strokes were simultaneous. He asks the reader whether that statement makes sense, and expects a confident yes.
- He then asks the reader to explain the sense more exactly, and shows that the answer is harder than it first appears.
- He rejects the reply that the meaning is clear in itself and only the checking needs thought. If a theory predicted the strokes were always simultaneous, one would still need a way to test it against reality.
- He states the rule directly: "The concept does not exist for the physicist" until there is a possibility of finding out whether it is fulfilled in an actual case. Without that, he says, a person only deceives himself in thinking the statement has meaning.
- He stops and asks the reader not to go further until fully convinced of this point.
- He lets the reader propose a method: measure the line AB, put an observer at the midpoint M with mirrors so both places can be watched at once, and call the strokes simultaneous if the observer sees both flashes together.
- He raises his own objection to this: it seems to assume light takes equal time from A to M and from B to M, and checking that would already need a way of measuring time, which looks like a logical circle.
- He has the reader answer the objection. The equal travel time is not a hypothesis about the nature of light. It is a stipulation freely made in order to arrive at a definition, and the only demand on a definition is that it gives an empirical decision in every real case.
- From this he builds a definition of time: identical clocks at points along the line, set by the simultaneity rule, with the time of an event being the reading of the clock right next to it. He flags the remaining physical assumptions, such as identical clocks running at the same rate.
