## Key lessons

- Launching the Speak Mandarin Campaign in 1984, Lee argued that most children cannot truly master a dialect plus Mandarin plus English at once, so the bilingual policy could only work by replacing dialect with Mandarin as the home language, lightening the total learning load rather than adding a third language on top.
- He measured the policy's progress with specific figures rather than impressions: the share of new Primary 1 pupils from mainly Mandarin-speaking families rose from 26 percent in 1980 to 59 percent by 1984, while dialect-speaking families fell from 64 percent to 27 percent over the same years. He also tracked resistance directly through birth-registration data, noting that between August 1982 and July 1984 a fifth of parents still registered children's names only in dialect, a partial rejection of the policy he did not hide.
- He named the emotional cost of the switch honestly: dialect-speaking grandparents often could not learn Mandarin at their age, so the government responded with housing priority for three-generation families rather than pretending the transition was painless, and he admitted regretting he had not launched the campaign five to ten years earlier once he judged Singaporeans had in fact been ready.
- The transferable lesson: when pushing a difficult cultural change, measure both the adoption and the holdout rate with real data over time, and treat resistance you can quantify as information to plan around, not as evidence the policy is failing.
