## Key lessons

- Five weeks after separation from Malaysia, Lee attended a scholarship ceremony at an Indian mission in Sembawang and pointed out, approvingly, that although the ten $100 scholarships were funded by the Mission's own Indian members, the recipients included Malay and Chinese students as well. He treated this small, concrete gesture as proof of the multiracial society he wanted, rather than describing it in the abstract.
- He was blunt about what separation had cost, and to whom: not Singapore's own prospects, which he argued would keep improving, but the inability to bring Malaya, Sabah and Sarawak along on the same path, including the families of Malayan-born police officers serving in Singapore who now could not be housed there.
- He used concrete, almost defiant economic detail rather than slogans to make his case for Singapore's viability alone: roughly 40 percent of Malaysia's total purchasing power sat in Singapore despite it holding about a fifth of the population, and the city's phone lines already reached Delhi, London, Tokyo and Canberra directly.
- The transferable lesson: when arguing that a hard, unwanted separation still leaves you strong, back the claim with specific, checkable numbers and small real examples (a mixed scholarship list, a market-share figure) rather than general reassurance.
