## Key lessons

- In this combined 1978 text, Lee traced Singapore's cross-community respect for education to distinct historical roots: the Chinese imperial examination system that produced a meritocratic Mandarinate, the British Indian Civil Service entrance exam that let poor but able Indians rise, and the Malayan Civil Service built on the same pattern. He used this shared history, rather than a single ethnic tradition, to justify scholarship support drawn from the whole community.
- He made an unusually technical argument for why bilingual education was hard: citing years of failed attempts to build Russian-English machine translation, he argued that even computers could not reliably master two languages because grammar and syntax differ too deeply between them, then reassured parents that a human child's brain, unlike a computer, can genuinely hold two languages if taught young.
- His most concrete warning was practical: if a child spent school hours on Mandarin but used only English and dialect at home, with friends, and in daily life, Mandarin would become "like Latin," a subject passed for examinations and then forgotten, because it was never made part of daily life. He offered to reconsider education policy entirely if parents genuinely wanted their children to keep their own dialect instead.
- The transferable lesson: a skill or language only survives if it is used in real daily contexts, not merely tested; a policy that produces exam passes without daily use will look successful on paper and fail in practice.
