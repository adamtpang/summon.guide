## Key lessons

- Note: the checklist's source map listed a 16 April 2006 Singapore General Hospital anniversary speech with a URL not independently confirmed and flagged for follow-up. That specific record could not be located this pass, so it is replaced here with a confirmed, directly downloadable National Archives PDF from the same governing-elite theme, in Lee's final months as Prime Minister: his speech at the Dinner for the Establishment, Raffles Ballroom, 29 August 1990.
- Speaking three months before he stepped down after 31 years as Prime Minister, Lee argued that a country without a monarchy, a shared religion, or an entrenched ruling class, as Singapore had none of these, has to build its authority to govern purely on demonstrated competence and character rather than tradition, since there is no inherited mystique to fall back on.
- He gave a stark, specific measure of how fragile leadership succession had once been and how much it had improved: in 1965, Singapore could have been left leaderless if roughly 30 people at the very top, ten each in politics, the civil service and judiciary, and the armed forces and police, had been simultaneously removed. By 1990, he said that fragility had eased more than tenfold, because each core leader now had 10 to 20 trained deputies able to step in.
- He illustrated the danger of an ungoverned elite with a personal anecdote from the 1950s: he had prosecuted a British Chief Fire Officer for misusing a firemen's welfare fund, lost the case, and watched the officer's fellow British colonial officers quietly repay the missing money themselves rather than let the system's reputation suffer, calling it "a lesson in the tactics of group survival by an elite."
- The transferable lesson: measure institutional resilience concretely, by asking how many people would have to disappear before the organisation could no longer function, and treat systematically widening that number, not just picking one successor, as the real work of succession planning.
