## Key lessons

- Speaking to new graduates at a 1967 convocation dinner, Lee opened by challenging the idea that a degree marks the end of intellectual growth, calling it in Singapore's case a "full-stop" mentality inherited from a migrant society obsessed with scholarship exams as the sole ladder to success. He wanted graduates to treat the degree as one punctuation mark in a longer sentence, not the conclusion.
- He made an observational point rather than a moral one about elite credentials: he noted that, to his knowledge, no Rhodes Scholar had gone on to become prime minister or president of his own country, and that no British prime minister that century had combined a first-class Oxbridge degree, a sporting blue, and the presidency of the student union. He used this to argue that early academic brilliance does not reliably predict later leadership.
- He then made a specific, numeric policy claim: out of roughly 60,000 babies born a year, he estimated some 3,000 (doubled to be safe, effectively about 10 percent) would be unusually gifted, and argued that Singapore's limited resources had to be concentrated on identifying and nurturing that group early, before a child's mind had "set."
- The transferable lesson: state your selection criteria and your target numbers explicitly when arguing for concentrating scarce resources on a minority, rather than leaving "invest in talent" as a vague aspiration, and be willing to have that explicit approach scrutinised.
