## Key lessons

- Note: the checklist's source map listed an undated National Day Rally speech at the National Theatre. The confirmed National Archives of Singapore record for that link resolves to this Tanjong Pagar Community Centre National Day speech, 16 August 1984, used here as the closest verified substitute in the same annual-address format.
- Lee argued that governments fall into two camps: those (like postwar Japan) that make politics serve economic and social goals, and those that subordinate everything else to an ideological or procedural ideal, citing Maoist China as the extreme case and India and Sri Lanka as milder examples where heavy emphasis on parliamentary debate came at the cost of efficient government. He placed Singapore deliberately in the first camp, prioritising law, order and economic substance ahead of democratic form.
- He backed the claim that Singapore's system stayed clean with concrete mechanics: campaign spending capped near 50 cents per voter, no vote-buying transport, compulsory voting introduced in 1959 partly to blunt organised communist turnout, and MPs removed at "the slightest taint of corruption or dishonesty," including a former Minister of State.
- The transferable lesson: he treated the sequencing of priorities, substance before form, order before open contest, as the explanation for Singapore's results, and used a specific recruited candidate's own reasoning (that clean, low-money politics meant capable people had to volunteer, since there was no wealth or power to fight over) to argue why merit-based recruitment, not just rules, kept the system honest.
