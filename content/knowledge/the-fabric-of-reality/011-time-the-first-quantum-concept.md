# Time: The First Quantum Concept

> **Key principle:** The common-sense picture of a "moving present" sweeping across fixed moments is not merely false but incoherent, because a genuinely moving now would require a second, external time for it to move relative to. Deutsch's resolution, drawn from quantum theory: "Other times are just special cases of other universes." Our subjective sense of time flowing is nothing but the ordinary difference between an observer's present perceptions and present memories.

*Synthesized from Chapter 11 of The Fabric of Reality by David Deutsch.*

## Key lessons

- The grammar-textbook picture of time (a line of fixed moments with a "continuously moving point, the present moment" sweeping across it) collapses under its own logic: if the present is truly moving, it must itself be drawn as a sequence of snapshots, and once that is done there is no single privileged "now" left — every moment is simply "now" for itself, and nothing sweeps between them.
- Deutsch treats the flow of time as strictly nonsensical rather than just factually wrong: for "now" to move from one moment to another, it would have to move with respect to some further, exterior time, which itself would need its own moving present relative to a still more exterior time, generating an infinite regress that never bottoms out.
- The same argument applies to consciousness: every version of an observer, at every moment, is fully conscious and each regards its own moment as the present, so there is no single stream of awareness sweeping through time. What we actually experience is the difference between present perceptions and present memories of past perceptions, correctly read as evidence of change but wrongly read as evidence that something is moving.
- Classical "spacetime physics" (including relativity) removes the flow of time by treating reality as a static, four-dimensional "block universe" in which nothing ever moves, but this comes at a cost: since exactly one sequence of events occurs, counterfactual statements like "if Faraday had died in 1830" have no physical referent, leaving causation an unresolvable paradox rather than a real relation between events.
- The multiverse restores meaning to causation because variants of any event genuinely exist elsewhere in the multiverse, in definite proportions, so a counterfactual about what would have happened if some event had gone differently can refer to real alternate universes rather than arbitrary imagination; Deutsch defines a cause as an event without which, in most of those variant universes, the effect would not occur either.
- The chapter's central move, traced to 1960s quantum-gravity work by Bryce DeWitt and stated generally by Don Page and William Wooters in 1983, is that quantum theory provides no way to slice the multiverse into synchronized "super-snapshots" shared across universes, so the traditional boundary between a different moment of our own universe and a wholly different universe turns out not to be fundamental at all.
- The familiar sequential picture of time is only a good local approximation, holding in regions where snapshots happen to fall into mutually determining chains (the solar system is the standard example), and it breaks down badly near the Big Bang before the Planck time of roughly 10⁻⁴³ seconds, inside black holes, and possibly at a Big Crunch, where questions about "before" or "after" stop making sense at all.
- The "open future, fixed past" intuition survives once reinterpreted: before a quantum event such as a coin toss, an observer's future is genuinely open because different copies of that observer really do go on to see different outcomes, and after the event each copy's own past becomes single-valued and fixed for it, even though the multiverse as a whole still contains both outcomes as equally real.
