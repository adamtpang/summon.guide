# A Conversation About Justification

> **Key principle:** The "problem of induction" as modern philosophers pose it is not about whether induction is valid (everyone agrees it isn't) but about what, if not induction, justifies relying on a corroborated theory to predict the future. Deutsch's answer, dramatized as a dialogue at the top of the Eiffel Tower, is that no such justification is needed or even coherent to demand: theories earn provisional reliance by being the best surviving explanation among the rivals actually proposed and criticized, not by being proven or made probable.

*Synthesized from Chapter 7 of The Fabric of Reality by David Deutsch.*

## Key lessons

- Deutsch coins "crypto-inductivist" for a philosopher who agrees inductive reasoning is invalid and that theories are only ever conjectured, never derived from data, yet still believes the invalidity of induction leaves an unfilled "induction-shaped gap" that some other justification must fill before we're entitled to rely on science.
- The dialogue answers philosopher John Worrall's staged debate ("Why Both Popper and Watkins Fail to Solve the Problem of Induction") by restaging it at the Eiffel Tower: the Crypto-inductivist presses David on why it's rationally justified to expect that jumping over the railing means death, given that all past evidence is logically consistent with infinitely many rival theories, including ones predicting he'd float.
- David's first move is that only argument, not raw evidence, ever justifies a theory (tentatively), and that Popperian "corroboration" means a theory has survived tests against its actual, historically proposed rivals, not against every logically conceivable rival - a distinction the Crypto-inductivist initially finds strange but comes to accept.
- The Crypto-inductivist then constructs a rival "floating" theory, identical to the prevailing theory of gravity in every past prediction except that David alone floats when he jumps, and argues it is exactly as corroborated as the accepted theory since no past experiment could have distinguished them.
- David rejects the floating theory not on evidential grounds but because it is a bad explanation: it appends an unexplained anomaly to a good theory, solves no problem, invites unanswerable follow-up questions (what carries the "anti-gravity" signal, does it respect relativity, does it apply to what he's holding), and thereby spoils the explanatory power of the existing theory of gravity rather than improving it.
- When the Crypto-inductivist tries a Goodman-style trick, inventing a language ("x-fall"/"x-float") in which the floating theory looks unqualified and the ordinary theory looks like the one carrying an ad hoc exception, David counters that languages are themselves theories embodying substantive assumptions, so which theory is objectively anomalous is a fact about physical reality independent of the words chosen to state it.
- Pressed further on what justifies the underlying principles of rationality (such as the laws of deduction) themselves, David gives the same answer all the way down: argument, tentative and fallible like all reasoning, justified only in the sense that no explanation is improved by abandoning them - there is no bedrock of self-evident axioms, only theories that have not yet been beaten by a better explanation.
- The chapter's resolving idea is that argument was never structured like a mathematical proof running from fixed axioms to a conclusion; real arguments start in the middle, full of gaps and inconsistencies, and proceed by criticizing and replacing even the "axioms" as better explanations appear, so the demand for a prior, unchallengeable justification misunderstands what argument is for and how it works.
