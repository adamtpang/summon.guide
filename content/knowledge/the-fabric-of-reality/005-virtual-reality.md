# Virtual Reality

> **Key principle:** Deutsch generalizes "virtual reality" far beyond headsets and flight simulators: any device that overrides the senses to give an artificial experience of a specified environment counts, and he argues that a universal image generator, one that can in principle render any humanly experienceable sensation, is a real physical possibility rather than science fiction. That single idea turns out to connect computation, imagination, mathematics, and science as variations on one underlying phenomenon.

*Synthesized from Chapter 5 of The Fabric of Reality by David Deutsch.*

## Key lessons

- Deutsch defines "image generator" broadly enough to include a spice rack or hi-fi system alongside a flight simulator, since all of them supply specifiable sensory input; true virtual reality adds two further requirements, wide sensory coverage and genuine interactivity ("kicking back") between user and simulated world.
- He replays Dr. Johnson's stone-kicking refutation of solipsism inside a flight simulator: even though no engine physically exists, the computer's calculations respond to the throttle in a complex, autonomous way, so they pass the test for reality just as a real engine would, because the computer performing those calculations is itself an ordinary, real physical object.
- Using the example of simulating weightlessness on the ground, Deutsch shows that gravity, unlike light or sound, cannot simply be intercepted and replaced by external effectors, which momentarily seems to put a hard physical limit on VR's fidelity.
- That apparent limit dissolves once he considers bypassing the sense organs entirely and directly stimulating the nerves that carry their signals to the brain; once the neural code for a sense is cracked, any sensation, including weightlessness, can in principle be rendered with no theoretical limit on accuracy, only practical limits on current technology.
- A rendering is classified as excluded from VR's scope only if it would be logically impossible (like the experience of factorizing the prime number 181) or purely internal (a mental state rather than an experience of an external environment); everything else, physically possible or not, falls within VR's potential repertoire.
- Because virtual reality is interactive, its accuracy cannot be verified the way a recorded image's accuracy can: a user can prove a rendering wrong by finding one perceptible discrepancy, but can never certify it as accurate, since infinitely many untried actions might have revealed a difference; Deutsch draws an explicit parallel to how scientific theories can be falsified by experiment but never proven true.
- Any virtual-reality generator rendering a physically impossible environment (a faster-than-light aircraft, say) is simultaneously, and inescapably, an accurate rendering of some physically possible environment, namely a duplicate of itself; Deutsch uses this to argue that the line between "rendering fact" and "rendering fiction" is a matter of interpretation, not an objective property of the rendering.
- Deutsch extends the same logic to mathematics and to ordinary perception: doing mathematics means imagining entities with properties (like a truly zero-thickness line) that exist only in a rendered "virtual reality" of the mind, and everyday sense perception is itself never direct but a virtual-reality rendering assembled by the brain from raw nerve signals plus inborn and learned theories, making imagination, reasoning, and experience all instances of the same underlying phenomenon.
