# Problem-solving

> **Key principle:** Deutsch uses the history of planetary astronomy to show that theories are never derived from observations by logic; instead, following Karl Popper, he argues that knowledge grows through a cycle of conjecture (unjustified guesses) and criticism (including, in science, experimental testing), a process that resembles biological evolution more than it resembles bookkeeping from evidence.

*Synthesized from Chapter 3 of The Fabric of Reality by David Deutsch.*

## Key lessons

- Deutsch traces the discovery of planets as objects that "wander" against the fixed stars rather than holding rigid positions like them, then through Copernicus's circular heliocentric orbits, Kepler's ellipses, Newton's inverse-square gravity (which correctly predicted Neptune's existence from orbital deviations in 1846), and finally Einstein's general relativity, which corrected Mercury's orbit and predicted starlight deflection confirmed by Eddington in 1919. Each successive theory explained smaller, subtler effects while producing larger revisions to humanity's picture of reality, not a steady convergence toward one final theory.
- He poses the "problem of induction": since all we ever perceive are patterns of nerve impulses, and solipsism (only one mind exists) can never be logically ruled out by any observation, no amount of observational evidence can logically prove anything about external reality. This means scientific conclusions cannot be logical deductions from observations, so something else must justify them.
- Inductivism holds that theories are discovered by extrapolating or generalizing from accumulated observations, and are justified by repeated confirming observations. Deutsch calls this "profoundly false," arguing first that real theories (like parallel universes, or that planets are worlds orbiting the sun) are not generalizations of observations at all, and second, via Bertrand Russell's fable of a chicken that "extrapolates" from daily feedings that the farmer is benevolent right up until the farmer wrings its neck, that repeated observations cannot justify a prediction's continuation.
- The deeper flaw Deutsch presses is that extrapolation is not even possible without an explanatory framework already in place: the same set of observations (e.g., a farmer suddenly feeding chickens more) can be "extrapolated" to opposite predictions depending on which prior explanation (benevolence vs. fattening for slaughter) one already holds, so observations alone never single out one theory among the infinitely many compatible with them.
- Popper's alternative, which Deutsch adopts as one of the book's four main explanatory strands, treats science as a problem-solving process: it begins not with observations but with a problem, meaning some existing theory that seems inadequate, narrow, glib, or in conflict with another good explanation. The process then runs through conjecture (proposing new or modified theories), criticism (comparing them against the problem's criteria), and, when a conjecture fails, abandonment in favor of surviving theories, which themselves become the starting point for new problems.
- Why astronomy produced deep theory while everyday cloud-watching did not, despite clouds offering far more observational data, illustrates that unexpected observations only trigger discovery when a pre-existing explanatory framework already contains the seeds of a problem, since attributing unpredictable cloud drift to shifting winds at different altitudes already felt like a complete common-sense account, while wandering lights in the sky left people unsatisfied and searching for a deeper explanation.
- Deutsch highlights a decisive asymmetry: a false prediction refutes the theory behind it, but a true prediction never confirms a theory, since, as Deutsch puts it, "Shoddy explanations that yield correct predictions are two a penny." This grounds Popper's falsifiability criterion, illustrated by the untestable "angel" theory of planetary motion, which cannot be ruled out by any possible observation and therefore cannot genuinely explain the specific motions we see; testability, not confirmation, is what separates science from pseudoscience and from theories like the "grass cure" for colds, which can be dismissed without experiment because it explains no more than rival theories while adding unsupported claims.
- The whole conjecture-and-criticism cycle resembles biological evolution, with a scientific problem acting like an ecological niche and rival theories acting like competing genes subjected to variation and selection, though Deutsch notes real differences: biological mutation is blind and purposeless while human conjecture is deliberate and knowledge-laden, and there is no biological equivalent of argument, since genes cannot be tested by reasoning the way theories can be tested experimentally.
