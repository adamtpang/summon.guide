# Shadows

> **Key principle:** Deutsch builds his case for the multiverse not from abstract quantum formalism but from a plain physical fact: a screen behind a barrier can go dark at a specific point only because a second, new slit was cut elsewhere in the barrier, even when just one photon at a time passes through the apparatus. The only consistent explanation is that unseen "shadow" photons, travelling through the other slits in other universes, are interfering with the photon we track.

*Synthesized from Chapter 2 of The Fabric of Reality by David Deutsch.*

## Key lessons

- Deutsch starts from quantization, not interference: a torch's beam looks continuous only because huge numbers of photons arrive per second. Thin the beam enough (using a sensitive detector, or his frog-eye thought experiment) and it resolves into discrete, equally bright flickers with gaps of true darkness between them, showing that light comes in indivisible lumps rather than being infinitely divisible like a fluid.
- Ordinary shadows are not simple silhouettes once holes get small: light passing through a tiny hole "frays," producing rings and fringes rather than a sharp bright/dark boundary, because light does not travel in strictly straight lines at small enough scales.
- The core experiment uses a barrier with two, then four, parallel slits and laser light hitting a screen. A four-slit shadow pattern is not the simple sum or overlay of two two-slit patterns shifted sideways; specific points on the screen (like point X) that were bright with only two slits open go completely dark once a second pair of slits is cut.
- Deutsch explicitly rules out the naive "two photons collide like billiard balls" explanation: the interference persists even when the experiment is run with a single photon at a time (as sparse as one photon per day), and detectors placed at each slit confirm a photon never splits into fragments, since only one detector ever fires per trial.
- Because something still must be arriving through the other, unblocked slits to deflect the lone tangible photon away from point X, and because that something behaves in every measurable way like light (blocked by opaque material, passed by transparent material, reflected and focused by mirrors and lenses), Deutsch concludes it consists of real photons we simply cannot detect directly, which he names "shadow photons."
- From the sheer number of possible slit positions across an illuminated area, Deutsch estimates there must be at least about a trillion shadow photons accompanying every single tangible one, implying reality is vastly larger than what we can directly perceive, and that this hidden structure applies to every kind of particle (shadow electrons, shadow neutrons, and so on), not just photons.
- Extending the argument with a thought experiment about shadow photons hitting an opaque barrier, Deutsch reasons there must be a full shadow barrier made of shadow atoms, and that shadow particles are themselves partitioned into distinct, mutually consistent groups, meaning the "shadow" reality is not one undifferentiated mass but many separate parallel universes, each internally consistent and each behaving like a complete tangible universe.
- Deutsch coins "multiverse" for the whole of physical reality (all parallel universes together) and stresses there is nothing objectively special about the universe we call "tangible": a "shadow" version of Deutsch, in a shadow universe, is at that same moment experiencing his own universe as the tangible one. He credits physicist Hugh Everett (1957) as the first to recognize that quantum theory itself describes a multiverse, and closes by noting quantum physics is the first of the book's four explanatory strands, with epistemology taken up next.
