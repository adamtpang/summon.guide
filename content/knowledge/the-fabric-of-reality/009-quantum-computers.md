# Quantum Computers

> **Key principle:** Quantum computation is a qualitatively new way of harnessing nature, not a miniaturized extension of classical computing, because it distributes parts of a single task across parallel universes and then combines the results through interference; when a quantum computer factors a huge number, the number of computational steps involved vastly exceeds the particles available in our universe, so the extra work must be happening elsewhere in the multiverse.

*Synthesized from Chapter 9 of The Fabric of Reality by David Deutsch.*

## Key lessons

- All classical computers, however small their components, are just faster implementations of the same Turing-machine idea and share one repertoire of computations; a true quantum computer instead exploits interference to perform entirely new categories of computation that no Turing machine could do even in principle, however much time it were given.
- Deutsch distinguishes unpredictability (a system behaving differently across universes, unrelated to computing power) from intractability (a task that is in principle predictable but would require impractically large computational resources); classical "chaos" and the butterfly effect are actually a multiverse phenomenon in disguise, since near-identical initial conditions in different universes diverge into hurricane and no-hurricane outcomes without any real sensitivity to measurement error.
- Predicting the behavior of interacting quantum particles is generically intractable for classical computers because the number of interfering histories grows exponentially with the number of particles: a thousand-hole interference pattern requires calculating roughly a thousand parallel paths, and a handful of interacting particles can already require computing millions, billions, or trillions of distinct universes' worth of history.
- Richard Feynman's 1982 insight was to treat this intractability as an opportunity rather than an obstacle: since setting up a quantum interference experiment and reading its outcome effectively performs the equivalent calculation at no extra cost, a quantum system used as an auxiliary computing device could render environments that are intractable for any classical machine.
- Deutsch's own 1985 contribution was proving that a single, fixed universal quantum computer exists under quantum physics, by redoing Turing's construction with quantum mechanics as the underlying physics instead of classical mechanics; unlike Feynman's proposed universal quantum simulator, this one machine (in principle) can be programmed once to perform any computation or render any physically possible environment, and its resource requirements do not explode exponentially with the size of what is being rendered.
- Peter Shor's 1994 algorithm lets a quantum computer factor large numbers efficiently, a task that stays exponentially hard for every known classical method (factoring a 250-digit number classically was estimated by Donald Knuth to take over a million years on a million networked computers); this directly threatens RSA public-key cryptography, whose security rests entirely on factorization being classically intractable, meaning any message encrypted and recorded today could eventually be decrypted once a low-decoherence quantum factorization engine exists.
- The central argument of the chapter: when a quantum computer factors a 250-digit number via Shor's algorithm, the interfering computation involves on the order of 10^500 universes, even though the algorithm itself only requires a few thousand arithmetic operations per universe; since the visible universe contains only about 10^80 atoms, nowhere near enough to hold that many computational steps, Deutsch argues the extra computation has to be physically happening in other universes, making a working quantum factorization engine a direct demonstration of the multiverse rather than a mere abstraction.
- Beyond speeding up existing task types, quantum computers open genuinely new capabilities no classical computer has, illustrated by the Bennett-Brassard quantum cryptography system built at IBM in 1989 (the first machine to perform a computation no Turing machine could match): its security rests not on any task being computationally hard but directly on quantum interference, since any eavesdropper's measurement necessarily disturbs the interference pattern and reveals itself, making the scheme secure against unlimited future computing power, not just against present-day computers.
- Decoherence, in which a quantum computation's differently-evolving branches leak into and differentially affect the surrounding environment, destroys the interference needed for quantum computation and is described as the chief practical obstacle to building more powerful quantum computers; Deutsch also notes it is caused by the computation disturbing its environment, not (as commonly said) by the environment disturbing the computation.
