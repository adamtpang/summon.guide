# The Four Strands

> **Key principle:** Physicists compute with quantum theory while denying the multiverse it implies, computer scientists use the Turing principle while doubting artificial intelligence, most scientists follow Popperian method while rejecting his account of why it works, and biologists accept natural selection while still hunting for some missing ingredient in Darwinism — in every case the theory is accepted pragmatically but not believed as an explanation, and Deutsch traces this to real explanatory gaps that vanish only when the four strands are unified.

*Synthesized from Chapter 13 of The Fabric of Reality by David Deutsch.*

## Key lessons

- Deutsch lays out Thomas Kuhn's picture from The Structure of Scientific Revolutions in detail before rejecting it: Kuhn holds that a "paradigm" blinds its holders to anomalies, that "normal science" never questions fundamentals, and that a paradigm shift is won by sociological forces (recruitment, attrition, institutional power) rather than by the objective merit of rival explanations.
- Deutsch's central objection is that Kuhn's account cannot make sense of real, objective scientific progress; Kuhn himself was forced to deny that later theories are ever better approximations to the truth than earlier ones, which Deutsch treats as the theory's fatal flaw, since it cannot explain why we can build flying machines and the ancients could not.
- He offers the research seminar as a counterexample to Kuhnian sociology: a graduate student can openly challenge a senior professor's equation and be met with argument rather than appeals to rank, even though the same people revert to ordinary status-conscious behavior minutes later at dinner — evidence that genuine Popperian rationality does operate within science, at least when explanation itself is the topic.
- The chapter's main pattern, illustrated across all four strands, is that a "heretical" innovator's real claim is usually that the prevailing theory is true after all, not that it is false: Everett argued quantum theory (not the Copenhagen interpretation) should be taken literally, and Dawkins argued Darwin's theory, correctly understood, already explains adaptation without further patching.
- Deutsch shows this pattern was not accompanied by Kuhnian suppression: Wheeler, though he rejected Everett's multiverse conclusion, wrote a companion paper to help it get published, and Bryce DeWitt's objections were resolved by direct argument, ending with Everett's rejoinder "Do you feel the Earth move?" — a deliberate echo of Galileo's answer to why we don't feel the Earth's motion.
- Instead of a fight between paradigms, Deutsch describes "pragmatic instrumentalism": most physicists use quantum theory only as a predictive black box and never ask what it means, which is possible everywhere except quantum computation and quantum cosmology, where the internal mechanism is the whole subject and the multiverse becomes unavoidable.
- The same defensive pattern recurs for Turing (spending his life arguing brains aren't supernatural rather than extending computation theory), Popper (permanently refighting the problem of induction), and Dawkins (cast as public defender against creationism) — in each case, Deutsch argues, energy that should go into extending the theory is wasted refuting theories already known to be false.
- Deutsch's own diagnosis is that each strand alone has a genuine explanatory gap that makes it look narrow, mechanistic, and pessimistic — the Turing principle alone cannot explain consciousness or free will, Darwinism alone cannot explain why replication is physically stable, and Popperian epistemology alone cannot explain why the growth of knowledge occurs — but he argues each gap is filled by one of the other three strands, so that unified they form an optimistic, non-reductionist world-view rather than four separate narrow ones.
