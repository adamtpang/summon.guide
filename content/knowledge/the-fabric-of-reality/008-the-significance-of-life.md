# The Significance of Life

> **Key principle:** Aristotle was wrong that animate matter has special mechanical properties, and modern biology confirms it: life reduces to molecular replicators obeying ordinary physics. But Deutsch argues Aristotle was right that life is a fundamental phenomenon — not because of any vital force, but because life is the process by which knowledge, a genuinely distinctive physical structure, first got embodied in the universe, with consequences that will eventually reach the scale of stars and galaxies.

*Synthesized from Chapter 8 of The Fabric of Reality by David Deutsch.*

## Key lessons

- Aristotle held that animate matter, unlike a resting rock, can initiate motion without being "kicked" from outside; modern physics dissolves this (a hibernating bear wakes via electrochemical processes, while inanimate uranium nuclei can decay with no external stimulus at all). Deutsch credits Aristotle only with correctly sensing that life is theoretically fundamental, for entirely the wrong reasons.
- Modern biology replaces any special living "essence" with a purely molecular account: life rests on replicators, entities like DNA genes that cause their environment to make copies of them. A gene is effectively a program written in the four-letter A/C/G/T code, executed by cellular machinery that manufactures proteins.
- Not everything that gets copied counts as a replicator. A true replicator's specific physical form must causally contribute to its own copying (change the insulin gene and the organism dies, so its exact sequence matters), whereas junk DNA is copied regardless of its sequence and so has a negligible "degree of adaptation."
- Organisms don't really reproduce themselves — each generation is built afresh from the parent's gene blueprints, so an organism's body (like a bear's nose, or its den) is just part of the environment that genes manipulate to get themselves replicated. This gene's-eye view, only fully worked out in Dawkins's The Selfish Gene and The Extended Phenotype, makes organisms the stage rather than the actors.
- Taken at face value, this reductive picture seems to make life astrophysically negligible: Earth's biosphere is a tiny fraction of the solar system's mass and energy, prompting Deutsch to cite Stephen Hawking's remark that humanity is "just a chemical scum on a moderate-sized planet, orbiting round a very average star in the outer suburb of one among a hundred billion galaxies."
- Deutsch reconnects life to fundamental physics through the Turing principle from Chapter 6: a living cell is a virtual-reality generator, with genes as programs that render an interactive organism tuned to make its environment replicate those genes. Life is thus the mechanism by which universal virtual-reality rendering first appeared in nature.
- He argues knowledge, not bulk mass or energy, is the right measure of physical significance: predicting the Sun's fate billions of years hence already depends on unknowable choices intelligent descendants might make (such as deliberately intervening before it becomes a red giant), and precedents like the Great Wall of China or plants generating all atmospheric oxygen show that knowledge can move far more mass than its own size implies.
- The multiverse supplies a real physical signature for knowledge: a gene sequence and a physically identical junk-DNA sequence look alike in this universe alone, but across nearby universes the gene stays almost unchanged (a "crystal" of regularity) while the junk sequence varies randomly. Knowledge-bearing matter really is physically distinctive — just not detectably so within a single universe.
