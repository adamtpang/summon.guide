# Universality and the Limits of Computation

> **Key principle:** Most logically conceivable environments (Deutsch's "Cantgotu environments") are provably unrenderable by any computer that could physically exist, yet the Turing principle claims that one single, buildable universal machine can still render every environment that is physically possible, which is a substantive fact about the structure of reality, not a mathematical triviality.

*Synthesized from Chapter 6 of The Fabric of Reality by David Deutsch.*

## Key lessons

- Deutsch first removes an apparent limit: by having the virtual-reality generator control the speed of the user's brain (slowing or briefly halting it while a hard calculation completes, then resuming seamlessly), a machine can render arbitrarily complex environments with no upper bound on computation per subjective second, at the cost only of extra elapsed time in external reality.
- Using Cantor's diagonal argument (the same proof form later used by Gödel and central to Turing's theory of computation), Deutsch lists every program a given computer could run, then defines a "Cantgotu environment" that, minute by minute, is guaranteed to behave differently from every environment on that list, and therefore provably cannot be generated by any program on it.
- Because this construction works against the full list of programs for any given machine, and even against a combination of many different machines, Deutsch concludes that for every environment a virtual-reality generator can render there are infinitely many Cantgotu environments it cannot, no matter how the technology improves.
- This shows physics permits only an infinitesimal fraction of all logically possible environments to ever be rendered, even though that permitted set is itself infinite and vastly larger than everyday human experience.
- Deutsch then defines a weaker, more interesting target: a "universal" virtual-reality generator need only have a repertoire containing that of every other physically possible generator, and this in turn depends on the existence of a universal computer, one machine able to calculate anything any other physical object can calculate.
- He distinguishes the mathematicians' Church-Turing conjecture (an abstract claim about formal models of computation) from what he calls the Turing principle: the physical claim that a real, buildable computer's repertoire can include every computation performable by any physically possible object, later strengthened to cover every physically possible environment, not just abstract computations.
- The non-computability of some well-defined questions (illustrated with the still-open question of whether infinitely many prime pairs exist) shows that having a definite, well-defined answer does not guarantee any physical process, however powerful, can ever compute or verify that answer.
- The chapter's closing thought experiment, a scientist trapped in a virtual-reality "prison" simulating something as simple as chess, argues that explanation-seeking science can still detect the existence of an outside reality with different laws, because the trapped world is too simple to explain the observer's own existence or thought, which is why Deutsch says a self-contained illusion would require rendering practically the whole of reality.
