## Key lessons

- Situation: Murthy describes childhood loneliness in his own words: a sinking feeling each morning at school drop-off, dread of not being chosen for teams, and lunchtime in the cafeteria wondering if anyone would let him sit with them, even though his home was loving.
- What he argued: long loneliness erodes self-worth and brings shame, and self-consciousness and trying to be who others want can make connection feel forced. He kept it from his parents out of embarrassment. He separates three kinds of connection: intimate (a handful of people you can be yourself with), relational (friends for evenings and weekends), and collective (a community with a shared identity).
- Concrete point: a person can have a good marriage and still feel lonely from lacking friends or community, and a spouse may wrongly blame themselves. He also says people reach for alcohol, drugs, food or aggression to numb loneliness, which can deepen the problem.
- Transferable lesson: work out which kind of connection is missing before deciding what to do. He says, "Longstanding loneliness erodes our sense of self-worth" (Murthy, LARB, 2020).
