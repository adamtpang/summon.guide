# Steve Jobs (Make Something Wonderful)

> **Founder principle (Steve Jobs):** Jobs ran Apple, NeXT, and Pixar on a small set of repeatable moves: cut the product line down to what a small team can make excellent, describe the company by what it believes rather than what it sells, and treat direct, blunt feedback, whether from a mentor, a job candidate, or his own irritation with a bad product, as more useful than research or committee consensus. He applied the same discipline to his own attention that he demanded of the products bearing his name.

*David Senra on Steve Jobs, reading from Make Something Wonderful, the Steve Jobs Archive's collection of his own speeches, interviews, and emails. Founders Podcast · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pLKKO8DeQQk) · 121 min · published 2024-03-14.*

## Key lessons

- **Cut the product line down to what a small team can make excellent.** Returning to Apple in 1997, Jobs slashed the company's offerings from seventeen products to four, a decision that cost jobs and upset customers in the short term but let him put his best people fully behind each remaining product instead of spreading them thin.
- **Edit before you build, not only after.** Jobs pointed to Walt Disney's rule that animation was too expensive to animate more than necessary, so a film had to be edited before a single frame was drawn; he applied the same logic to Apple's own product roadmap, cutting roughly seventy percent of what was already planned rather than trimming after launch.
- **Describe the company by its values, not its specifications.** Jobs told Apple employees that marketing is about values, not speeds and feeds, pointing to Nike's ads, which never mention air soles, only honor great athletes, as the model for why Apple's think different campaign talked about believing people who are crazy enough can change the world instead of listing features.
- **Map your critics' objections in advance and answer them in order.** Jobs described an informal hierarchy of skepticism at Apple: prove survival first, then prove a stable business, then prove a product strategy, then prove growth, noting that clearing one level just reliably produces the next objection, so it pays to get ahead of the sequence rather than be surprised by it.
- **Deliberately provoke people in an interview to see how they respond.** Jobs said he would criticize a candidate's past work on purpose, sometimes unfairly, because the worst thing someone could do was agree with him; what he wanted was a candidate who would push back and defend their own judgment under pressure.
- **Ask directly for mentorship, and treat the relationship as reciprocal rather than transactional.** In his twenties Jobs cold called older operators like Robert Noyce, Andy Grove, Jerry Sanders, Bill Hewlett, and David Packard and simply asked to buy them lunch quarterly; when he later tried to charge Intel for Pixar's advice, Grove corrected him in writing, and Jobs said so himself and reversed his position immediately.
- **Get everyone aligned on the destination before you argue about the method.** Jobs described his approach as management by values: agree first on where the company is going, because once everyone actually wants to reach the same place, the argument over how to get there, or whose idea wins, resolves itself.
- **Place a new product in historical context before you bet on how fast it will be adopted.** Jobs compared the Macintosh's ease of use to the telephone succeeding where the telegraph failed, since Morse code took forty hours to learn while speaking required no training at all, and argued Apple's products needed to win the same way, by requiring nothing new to learn.
- **Let your own dissatisfaction with an existing product be the research.** Jobs said the iPhone was not driven by market studies or financial spreadsheets; it came from the fact that he and everyone he knew hated their phones, and he trusted that building something he and his team genuinely loved was a stronger signal than any survey.
