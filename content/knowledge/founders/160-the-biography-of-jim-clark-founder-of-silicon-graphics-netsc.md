# The Biography of Jim Clark (Founder of Silicon Graphics, Netscape)

> **Founder principle (Jim Clark):** Build each company so that control and equity stay with you and the people who did the actual work, not with whichever financier happens to be in the room when you're inexperienced enough to be taken advantage of. Treat old humiliations and unequal deals as a permanent source of drive rather than something to resolve and move past. When outside money will not commit, commit your own capital first and visibly, since that is what makes everyone else want in.

*David Senra on Jim Clark, reading from The New New Thing: A Silicon Valley Story by Michael Lewis. Founders Podcast · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tfns2StzgRE) · 57 min · published 2023-11-17.*

## Key lessons

- **Use the humiliation of early failure as permanent fuel rather than something to resolve and move past.** Clark rebuilt his entire life, from an expelled high school dropout to a PhD in computer science within eight years, on what the book frames as turning success into a form of payback for that humiliation, and the drive it produced never actually ran out once he had money, because moving the finish line, not reaching it, was the real engine.
- **Never let a single bad early deal happen to you twice.** After a venture capitalist took 40 percent of Silicon Graphics for $800,000 that became $400 million, Clark refused every later approach from that same investor, even when he pleaded to get into Netscape, and structured his next two companies specifically to keep both control and equity with himself and his engineers.
- **Be willing to destroy your own most profitable product before a competitor does it for you.** Clark argued that Silicon Graphics had to keep cannibalizing its own expensive workstations with cheaper technology or someone else eventually would, a position his own board and the professional CEO they installed refused to act on, which is part of why he eventually left to start over.
- **When you see a technology's user growth doubling on a fixed schedule, trust that signal even in an unfamiliar domain.** Clark moved from 3D graphics workstations into the internet the moment he heard its user base, then 25 million people, was doubling every year, saying it reminded him directly of the personal computer market in 1985.
- **Expect elite recruiting to be brutally difficult even after you're already a famous, successful founder.** When Clark set out to start Netscape, 11 of the 12 people he personally approached turned him down, and the one who said yes did so largely because he was 22 and had nothing to lose, a reminder that a track record alone does not make top people commit.
- **Correct your own earlier mistake the moment you get a second chance at the same kind of deal.** Having lost most of his own upside the first time by giving equity away to outsiders, Clark made sure his young Netscape engineers got real ownership this time, and made sure the single biggest stake in the company stayed with himself.
- **When outside capital will not commit, put your own money in first and visibly rather than continuing to ask.** When investors balked at funding Healtheon, Clark offered to supply the entire round himself, drawing on his own metaphor that distinguished the merely interested party from the one who is fully committed, and the moment he showed that level of commitment, the same financiers who had refused fought to get back into the deal.
- **Design your role and your days so that work and play are not two categories to manage, they are one activity you simply keep doing.** Clark deliberately arranged each company so he held a title and a board seat but no operating role he had to show up for, on the belief that his own restlessness was, in his case, a commercial virtue rather than a character flaw.
