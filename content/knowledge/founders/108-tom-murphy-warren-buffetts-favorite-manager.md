# Tom Murphy (Warren Buffett's Favorite Manager)

> **Founder principle (Tom Murphy):** Split the top job into two full-time disciplines instead of doing both halfway: one partner runs daily operations with fanatical, personally modeled cost control, the other allocates capital, buying only inside businesses the company already understands. Fund growth from retained cash flow and debt rather than diluting with stock, decentralize every operating decision down to the manager, and measure them on one number: margin. Wait years for the right acquisition, then size the bet to match real conviction, and buy back your own shares without hesitation whenever the market misprices them.

*David Senra on Tom Murphy, reading from The Outsiders by William Thorndike, with a passage from Ted Turner's autobiography. Founders Podcast · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qScpb2DIUxY) · 41 min · published 2024-04-15.*

## Key lessons

- **Split the CEO role into two full-time jobs and hire a partner for the other half.** Murphy handled acquisitions and capital allocation while Dan Burke ran daily operations and cost control; each became excellent at one discipline instead of average at two, a division of labor Warren Buffett called probably the greatest two-person management combination he had ever seen.
- **Treat headcount as the primary lever on cost, not a rounding error.** Murphy evaluated a manager's request to hire a $20,000-a-year assistant as an effective $3 million decision once raises, benefits, and tenure were priced in, and Capital Cities ran its headquarters with no marketing, human resources, or public relations staff at all, "forever cost conscious" in the words printed inside every annual report.
- **Stay inside businesses you actually understand instead of diversifying into what's fashionable.** While CBS spent the 1960s and 70s buying a toy company and the New York Yankees, Murphy stuck to TV and radio stations, then newspapers, then cable, because each shared the same advertising-driven economics he already knew how to run.
- **Decentralize operating authority completely, then leave managers alone as long as they hit their numbers.** Publishing chief Phil Meek ran six daily newspapers and several magazines out of a three-person headquarters, and station managers rarely heard from New York; a rival broadcaster once remarked that Capital Cities employees never sent out resumes because the autonomy made it impossible to imagine leaving.
- **Grow by acquisition slowly enough to actually integrate what you buy.** Most rollups collapse from over-rapid acquisition and too much debt; Murphy moved deliberately for three decades, let Burke prove out real operating expertise first, and only then made the handful of large bets, each over 25 percent of the company's market cap, that drove most of the returns.
- **When conviction is real, size the bet to match it.** The 1984 purchase of ABC for nearly $3.5 billion, financed with Warren Buffett's help, represented over 100 percent of Capital Cities' own enterprise value at the time, the largest non-oil-and-gas deal in business history to that point; Murphy's conviction was that Burke could cut ABC's TV station group staff from 60 to 8 people and lift margins from the low 30s to Capital Cities' own 50-plus percent within two years, which is exactly what happened.
- **Lead cost discipline personally, not just by memo.** When ABC executives kept a limousine culture of hailing a car for a few blocks, Murphy visibly took cabs everywhere instead; asked whether this was deliberate leading by example, he answered "is there any other way," and the habit spread through ABC's ranks on its own.
- **Negotiate from a fixed sense of fair value, not from a desire to win.** Murphy would ask a seller what they thought their property was worth, take it if the number was fair, counter once with his best price if it wasn't, and walk away rather than bid up; he rarely won at auction, typically coming in 60 to 70 percent below the winning bid.
- **Fund growth from retained cash flow and debt, then buy back stock aggressively when it's cheap.** Capital Cities rarely issued shares, repurchased close to half its stock during the bear market of the mid-1970s to early 1980s at single-digit price-to-earnings multiples, and spent over $1.8 billion on buybacks that alone returned 22.4 percent annually over 19 years.
