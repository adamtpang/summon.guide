# J. Paul Getty: The Richest Private Citizen in America

> **Founder principle (J. Paul Getty):** Insist on direct, personal control over your own operation rather than delegating away the decisions that matter, and back genuine conviction with real capital exactly when the consensus around you is panicking and prices are lowest. Build a small number of deep, trusted relationships with fellow entrepreneurs deliberately, because they become leverage no amount of capital alone can buy, and stay honest that this level of single-minded dedication has a real cost everywhere else in life.

*David Senra on J. Paul Getty, reading from As I See It: The Autobiography of J. Paul Getty. Founders Podcast · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WAmczA-JjGY) · 64 min · published 2024-07-09.*

## Key lessons

- **Insist on doing every job yourself before you ever direct someone else to do it.** Getty chose to be his own drilling superintendent early in his career specifically so his crews did exactly what he wanted, and he said the arrangement gave him a real sense of independence: he never had to look further than himself to place blame for a bad decision.
- **Take extreme ownership as a literal operating principle, not a slogan.** Getty adopted Truman's the-buck-stops-here standard as his own, arguing that the person in charge, whether of a candy factory or an oil company, earns freedom from feeling beholden to anyone precisely by accepting full responsibility for the outcome.
- **Move against the panic when the price is right, and do not let fear talk you out of it twice.** In 1930, with his father's estate advisors pushing to liquidate everything during the Depression, Getty instead bought undervalued shares in Tidewater Oil to build a vertically integrated company. In 1932 he let a similar opportunity in Iraq slip away out of fear during a separate price crash, a decision he later said cost the company an unknowable amount of revenue.
- **When you finally get the shot you missed before, do not flinch on price.** Seventeen years after passing on the Iraq concession, Getty committed to the Neutral Zone deal between Saudi Arabia and Kuwait on a three-word telegram from his geologist, "structures indicate oil," even though the payment and royalty terms were called outrageous across the industry. The deal went on to make him the world's richest private citizen.
- **Keep a large reserve of cash and use debt sparingly, and let your neighbors prove the alternative does not work.** Getty contrasted his own hundred-thousand-dollar house with the three-million-dollar Santa Monica estate his neighbor William Randolph Hearst built, noting that he was a third as rich as Hearst in 1935 and twice as rich by 1950.
- **Build relationships with the people you most want to do business with, deliberately and in person.** Getty bought a 72-room estate, Sutton Place, specifically to serve as an informal gathering place for entrepreneurs and executives, calling it a liaison center. A friendship built there with Aristotle Onassis later let the two men settle a tanker-monopoly dispute that threatened Getty's own shipping plans in a single conversation, sealed with a handshake.
- **A working knowledge of every part of the business earns the crew's respect that a title alone cannot.** Getty said an oilman who could do any job on the rig, not just direct it from an office, was accepted by roughnecks and drillers as one of their own, a trust he traced directly back to his father sending him to work as a three-dollar-a-day roustabout at fifteen.
- **Quitting after your first big win is a mistake worth naming honestly.** Getty retired at 24 after making his first million, then described the resulting boredom as something that "first nagged, then gnawed, and finally became intolerable" until he went back to work. He never made the same mistake again and worked until the final year of his life.
- **Total devotion to a business has a real, admitted cost elsewhere, and pretending otherwise is dishonest.** Getty was married and divorced five times, wrote that he gave more time and attention to his oil wells than to his home, and directly questioned in his own final book whether the pressure of trying to live up to him and his father contributed to the early death of his son George.
