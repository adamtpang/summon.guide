# The Biography of Kobe Bryant

> **Founder principle (Kobe Bryant):** Decide who you intend to become years before the results exist to justify that decision to anyone else, and expect the people around you to call the goal unrealistic for most of that time. Imitating the specific habits of the person you most want to become is a normal early stage, not a weakness to hide. Once you have real standing with an established master, spend it on direct, specific technical questions rather than general encouragement, and match your public commitment to the people who actually built your success or expect them to walk away.

*David Senra on Kobe Bryant, reading from Showboat: The Life of Kobe Bryant by Roland Lazenby. Founders Podcast · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YdViczJGMQg) · 60 min · published 2023-11-08.*

## Key lessons

- **Believe you will be the best years before anyone else could reasonably agree with you.** At around 12 years old he was already telling people he intended to become the greatest basketball player ever, and he was met mostly with people writing the idea off as unrealistic; the belief came long before any evidence would have justified it to an outside observer.
- **Expect your most audacious goal to be misunderstood by almost everyone around you, and treat that as normal rather than a reason to recalibrate.** From a school counselor telling him to plan for a career other than the NBA, to teammates and reporters, the people around him needed years to catch up to what he already believed about himself.
- **Copying the specific mannerisms of the person you most want to become is a normal, even necessary, stage.** As a teenager he shaved his head like Michael Jordan, adopted his phrasing, and kept a personal collection of Jordan game tape to study and imitate move by move; Jordan himself later defended this publicly, pointing out that he had copied the players before him the same way.
- **Once you've earned real standing with an established master of your field, spend it on a direct, specific technical question, not general advice.** Playing against Jordan early in his own career, he used a break in the action to ask exactly how Jordan positioned his legs on a post up move, and the detailed answer became something he kept building on for years afterward.
- **Learn how an evaluator actually judges a hidden quality like heart, then go produce visible evidence of it before you're evaluated.** Jerry West said a scout can usually see what a player can do but almost never see his heart directly, until a workout is so technically immaculate that the private effort behind it becomes the only possible explanation, at which point the heart becomes visible through the skill itself.
- **Use a concrete personal narrative to organize years of solitary effort, rather than trying to sustain motivation on willpower alone.** He treated his own path as a real quest modeled on a favorite work of fiction, nicknamed a veteran coach after a mentor figure from it, and treated picturing the outcome in advance as functionally part of the training itself, not a separate mental exercise.
- **Match the intensity of the people who helped build your success, or expect to lose them once they stop mattering to your goals.** He cut off contact entirely with the marketing figure who had originally brought him his first shoe deal and financial start, once that relationship no longer served the career he was building, a pattern the book treats as costing him a warmth other people would have preserved.
- **Set the competitive tone for a group yourself, since a team tends to convert to whatever standard its most intense member actually demonstrates.** Teammates who initially found his training schedule extreme were, within about a week of witnessing it directly at a national team camp, matching that same schedule themselves without being asked.
- **Retire on your own terms, at a moment and in a form you choose, rather than letting the decision be forced by decline.** He announced his retirement in a personally written public letter addressed directly to the sport itself, framing the end of his career as a decision he was making on his own timeline, rather than an ending that was simply happening to him.
