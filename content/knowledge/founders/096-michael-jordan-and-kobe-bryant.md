# Michael Jordan And Kobe Bryant

> **Founder principle (Michael Jordan and Kobe Bryant):** Never lower your standard to match the people around you, make them rise to yours instead, and take full personal responsibility when they don't. Protect and drill the fundamentals obsessively, treat training and recovery as business investments rather than luxuries, and build a fixed daily routine so your attention goes only toward what is actually unpredictable. State your self-belief as settled fact before the results exist to prove it, because waiting for proof first just means someone less talented but more certain will out-work you.

*David Senra on Michael Jordan and Kobe Bryant, reading from Tim Grover's Relentless and Winning. Founders Podcast · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BrkJsLFtGyg) · 84 min · published 2024-04-27.*

## Key lessons

- **Make everyone else rise to your level instead of lowering yours to the group.** Jordan inherited a losing Bulls franchise and told his new teammates plainly that the team would not come down to their level, they would rise to his, a stance Tim Grover generalizes as the actual strategy of anyone who becomes the best at what they do.
- **Take personal responsibility for the outcome, and let that justify holding the team to your own bar.** Kobe told Alex Rodriguez that history will not judge the other players on the roster, it will judge the leader, so his refusal to tolerate teammates who did not demand excellence of themselves was framed as a duty of leadership, not a personality flaw.
- **Protect the fundamentals and actively delete complexity instead of adding to it.** When Kobe asked Jordan what specific basketball technique he had been drilling at age twelve, Jordan's answer was that he was playing baseball that year, and both men pushed back on modern coaches overloading young players with advanced technique instead of repetition of the basics.
- **Pick one narrow thing, become the best in the world at it, and let everything else get built on top of that.** Grover illustrates this with an FBI marksman who trains alone on a single four-hundred-yard shot so that when the government needs exactly that shot, he is the only name anyone calls; Jordan's own version of the idea was blunt: "My game was my biggest endorsement."
- **Build an unbreakable personal routine so your attention goes only toward what is actually unpredictable.** Jordan's game-day routine, down to lacing his own shoes fresh out of the box every time, was rigid on purpose, since removing small daily decisions freed his focus for the one variable that could not be controlled, the game itself.
- **Treat training, recovery, and support staff as business investments, not indulgences.** Grover pushes back on media coverage that treats a chef, a masseuse, and a personal gym as luxury spending, arguing that for elite performers this staff is better understood as business partners than as perks.
- **Match your training intensity to what your actual talent-to-effort ratio requires, and know when to stop.** Grover contrasts the more naturally gifted Jordan, who trained hard but always slept at night, against a Kobe who was less naturally gifted but relentlessly obsessive, texting Grover for workouts at two or three in the morning until Grover himself stopped sleeping normal hours during those years.
- **Earn a dominant competitor's respect by refusing to back down, not by deferring to them.** A young Kobe telling a veteran Jordan "you can't guard me" in their first real exchange, and Steve Kerr standing his ground with Jordan in a practice that ended in a punch, both produced lasting trust rather than punishment, because both men only kept escalating against people who yielded to them.
- **State your self-belief as a settled fact before the results exist to prove it.** On a recruiting call with free agent Sam Bowie, Jordan did not sell, he simply said the Bulls were winning with or without him, matching Kobe's own answer when asked about fan expectations: "Their expectations will never be higher than my own."
