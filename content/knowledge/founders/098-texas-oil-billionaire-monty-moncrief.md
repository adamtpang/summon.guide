# Texas Oil Billionaire Monty Moncrief

> **Founder principle (Monty Moncrief):** Moncrief built a fortune, and later a multi-generational dynasty, by finding oil while the industry itself was still an open frontier: fragmented among individual landowners, barely regulated, and cheap enough to enter with borrowed money and raw nerve. He treated 29 consecutive dry wells as the price of staying in the game, financed the deal he could not afford by partnering and selling down pieces of the upside, and then stayed in one business, oil, for the rest of his life.

*David Senra on Monty Moncrief, reading from Wildcatters: A Story of Texans, Oil, and Money (1981). Founders Podcast · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=26akKrNC_pk) · 60 min · published 2024-04-25.*

## Key lessons

- **Look for industries still open to outsiders, not ones already run by giants.** Humble Oil, which later became Exxon, was dismissed in Moncrief's day as a "fly by night" operation, and the United States was then, as now, the only country in the world where mineral rights are privately owned, which let an individual landman lease directly from a farmer instead of negotiating with a government.
- **Move while the resource is still cheap to reach.** Wells at 5,000 feet cost roughly $20,000 to drill in the early days; twenty years later, once the shallow oil was gone, reaching the same amount of oil required drilling to 30,000 feet at roughly $750,000, a 37 times increase that priced small independents out of the field entirely.
- **Treat repeated failure as tuition, not a verdict.** Moncrief drilled 29 consecutive dry wells before his 30th came in at 18,000 barrels a day; the wildcatters of his generation genuinely believed they were born lucky, and that stubborn self belief, not evidence, is what kept them drilling past the point most people would quit.
- **Buy the asset before you can afford to develop it, then finance the development by partnering.** Moncrief bought a distressed 4,000 acre lease on impulse with no cash in hand, split it 50/50 with a partner down the hall, then sold off part of their interest to Getty to cover the cost of drilling the well that made him rich.
- **Avoid conflict with a bigger partner as a default, but use real leverage when it causes you real harm.** Independents usually stayed out of court with the major oil companies they depended on, but Moncrief took Humble before the Texas Railroad Commission over drainage from his fields and won more than 100 million dollars in compensation.
- **Stay concentrated in the one business you understand for the rest of your life.** The family called itself oilmen only, treated ranching and real estate as none of its concern, and kept the company entirely family owned and unincorporated by choice, even after the money made diversification easy.
- **What the majors have already rejected is not worthless.** Independent wildcatters were finding roughly 80 percent of the oil and gas discovered in America on leases the big companies had already passed on, a reminder that a large incumbent's rejection is a data point, not a verdict.
- **Wealth tied to a physical asset survives downturns that paper wealth does not.** Because Texas oil fortunes sat in the ground rather than in securities, independents like Moncrief rode out the Great Depression in a way New York's leveraged financiers could not, and it left Texans of that generation permanently distrustful of fortunes that existed only on paper.
- **Stay in the business, and keep living the way you did before the money arrived.** Moncrief was still doing oil deals in his 80s, decades past any financial need, never left the town he had helped settle, and never adopted a life of luxury, though that same permanent presence became a shadow his grandson later measured himself against, sometimes into unnecessary risk.
