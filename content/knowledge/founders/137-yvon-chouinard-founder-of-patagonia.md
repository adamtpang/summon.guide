# Yvon Chouinard (Founder of Patagonia)

> **Founder principle (Yvon Chouinard):** Chouinard became a reluctant businessman by refusing to separate his own standards from the company's: he built climbing hardware because the gear on the market offended his own philosophy, tested every product on himself first, and kept simplifying rather than adding features as Patagonia grew. When growth itself nearly broke the company, his fix was not a new strategy but writing down and personally teaching the philosophy that had guided him all along, then deliberately keeping the company under some form of stress on purpose, on the belief that comfort is what actually kills a business.

*David Senra on Yvon Chouinard, reading from Let My People Go Surfing, Chouinard's own account of building Patagonia. Founders Podcast · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OPT6sWCgauA) · 60 min · published 2024-03-08.*

## Key lessons

- **Let your own standards, not the industry's norms, set the design brief.** Chouinard built his own climbing hardware because the European pitons of the day were meant to be hammered in and abandoned in the rock, which offended the leave no trace ethic he had absorbed from Emerson, Thoreau, and Muir; his gear sold at roughly seven times the price of the market leaders and still sold out immediately.
- **Be your own first and most demanding customer.** Chouinard tested every piece of equipment on climbs where a tool's failure could kill him, and Patagonia's early product breakthroughs, like the 1980 polypropylene long underwear, came the same way, from solving a problem he had personally felt in the field before assuming anyone else needed it solved.
- **Pursue quality by removing, not by adding.** His stated design principle was that perfection is reached not when there is nothing left to add but when there is nothing left to take away, an idea he credited to the pioneering aviator and author of The Little Prince; his tools stood out at the base of a climb for being the lightest and simplest, not the most feature laden.
- **When you do not want to run daily operations yourself, hand the company over and say so plainly.** Chouinard gave both Chouinard Equipment and Patagonia to an outside CEO in the 1970s while he went climbing and surfing, and she in turn built out the business by directly asking bank presidents and industry contacts for help rather than pretending to have answers she did not have.
- **Teach your customers relentlessly and let sales follow, instead of advertising at them.** Patagonia's mail order catalog functioned as a classroom, printing field essays and layering guides rather than pure sales copy, on Chouinard's stated belief that advertising ranks dead last as a credible source of information and that teaching, informing, and inspiring is what actually drives sales over time.
- **Treat authenticity as a resource you can source from your own customers.** Rather than continuing to stage photos with hired models, Patagonia asked customers to submit their own pictures of themselves using the gear outdoors and was flooded with free, high quality submissions, because people who believe in a real brand want to help build it.
- **Deliberately reintroduce stress once the real problems are solved.** Chouinard borrowed the falconry term yarak, describing a falcon kept hungry, alert, and ready to hunt, as his management model: once a genuine crisis passed, he believed it was the leader's job to manufacture a new challenge on purpose rather than let the company settle into complacency.
- **Treat every operational excuse as a choice you are making, not a real constraint.** Chouinard kept a running list of excuses he heard inside the business, an insurance policy will not allow it, a supplier cannot be reached, something is impossible, and pushed back on each one by pointing out it was almost always a decision to deprioritize the task, not an actual wall.
- **Write down your operating philosophy and personally teach it at the exact moment the company is breaking.** After Patagonia's structure had been rebuilt five times in five years with nothing working, Chouinard did not just circulate a memo, he personally led weeklong seminars teaching employees the company's philosophy, which forced him to finally articulate why the business existed at all.
