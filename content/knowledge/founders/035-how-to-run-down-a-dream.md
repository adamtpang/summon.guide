# How To Run Down A Dream

> **Founder principle (Bill Gurley):** Pick a field you have immense, personal passion for rather than one chosen for status or money, since passion alone survives the years of unglamorous practice a dream career demands. Then become the single most knowledgeable person in that field through obsessive independent study, actively recruit mentors and peers, and once you arrive, give it all back.

*David Senra on Bill Gurley, reading from Running Down a Dream: How to Thrive in a Career You Actually Love. Founders Podcast · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=eSZ4Xep1sJU) · 31 min · published 2026-03-03.*

## Key lessons

- **Choose the field for passion, never for status or compensation.** Gurley's first and repeated rule, drawn from studying Bobby Knight, Bob Dylan, and Danny Meyer, is that a high status, high paying path pursued without genuine interest leads to burnout, because someone who actually loves the same work will always outwork you.
- **Test yourself on whether you love the practice, not just the trophy.** Bobby Knight's own line, that the will to prepare matters more than the will to win because everybody already has the will to win, is the real filter: notice whether you enjoy the unglamorous preparation itself before assuming a field is your dream job.
- **Become the most knowledgeable person in the room through sheer volume of study.** Restaurateur Danny Meyer, before opening his first barbecue restaurant, toured Texas eating at every barbecue joint he could find, then drove through North Carolina tasting fourteen distinct variations of chopped pork, cataloguing smoke type, sauce, and heat for each one. Information is freely available, so there is no excuse for not being the most informed person on your own subject.
- **Physically go to the epicenter of your field and put in the miles.** Bob Dylan hitchhiked twelve hundred miles from Minneapolis to New York City with a guitar, a suitcase, and ten dollars specifically to find Woody Guthrie and the folk scene, and Sam Hinkie flew a football stadium circuit on Southwest Airlines during his spring break to introduce himself to NFL front offices in person.
- **Pursue mentors deliberately and keep pursuing them after you succeed.** Sam Hinkie met a stranger for burritos across from Stanford and asked him two hundred questions instead of twenty; Bobby Knight spent his first five coaching years befriending the top basketball minds of his era by asking to sit beside them at luncheons. Mentorship is a habit to maintain for an entire career, not just a starting move.
- **Build peer relationships and share freely instead of hoarding what you know.** Gurley describes trading best practices with fellow venture investors without worrying about proprietary advantage, arguing that treating knowledge sharing as zero sum causes you to fail before you start, since the relationship itself compounds in value over time.
- **Pay it forward, and credit the people who helped you get there.** Bobby Knight, mentored by coach Pete Newell, later mentored a young Coach K, who eventually passed Knight on the all time wins list and asked Knight to induct him into the Hall of Fame, the exact debt Knight said he owed Newell for teaching him.
- **Accept real financial and career risk to get inside the door.** Sam Hinkie quit a lucrative job at Bain Capital, sent unpaid internship offers to NFL teams by letter, and moved his family into a Houston apartment where they slept on an air mattress while he worked from six in the morning past midnight, understanding that access itself had to be earned before analysis could matter.
