# David Packard (Founder of HP)

> **Founder principle (David Packard):** Packard measured Hewlett-Packard's success by one test: did the product genuinely improve a customer's life. He financed the company almost entirely from retained profit, distrusted debt after watching Depression-era firms collapse under it, and insisted that personal, face-to-face communication had to back up every written instruction. He judged people by what they were actually trying to accomplish rather than by whether they had followed orders, and he built HP's culture of decentralized authority and quality on that same standard.

## Key lessons

- **Ask whether the product deserves to exist before asking whether it will make money.** Packard's standard for every product was whether it offered something genuinely unique and made a customer's life better; he said that if the answer was no, he considered Hewlett-Packard a failure regardless of profit. That test, not a revenue target, was the actual finish line he built the company around.
- **Grow only from retained profit and treat debt as an existential risk.** Packard's father worked as a bankruptcy referee during the Depression, and Packard saw firsthand that firms which had mortgaged their assets were wiped out in foreclosure while unleveraged firms kept their assets and survived. A Wells Fargo banker later told him "more businesses die from indigestion than starvation," and Packard financed HP's growth almost entirely out of earnings for more than fifty years.
- **Back up every written instruction with direct, in-person communication.** While overseeing quality control at General Electric, Packard found that engineering's written instructions were not reaching the factory floor intact; once he started working alongside the line workers directly, an entire batch of parts passed final testing without a single failure. That habit of talking to the people actually doing the work became the practice HP later called management by walking around.
- **Refuse to be a me-too company.** Hewlett and Packard built HP around original, differentiated instruments instead of copying whatever competitors already sold, a discipline Packard treated as essential to the company's growth. Concentrating on one complementary family of measuring and testing instruments, rather than chasing unrelated products, gave HP a base solid enough to expand from for decades.
- **Build a talent pipeline through a real partnership with a university, not just recruiting.** HP's Honors Cooperative Program with Stanford let qualified engineers pursue graduate degrees on full salary with tuition paid by the company, and more than 400 HP engineers earned advanced degrees this way. Packard credited the program as an important factor in the company's long-term ability to hire top graduates.
- **Judge a rule-breaking employee by intent, not by whether they followed orders.** Engineer Chuck House was told to abandon a display monitor project, kept developing it on his own initiative anyway, and the product went on to sell more than 17,000 units worth $35 million; Packard later gave him a medal for "extraordinary contempt and defiance." The lesson Packard drew was that the line between insubordination and entrepreneurship is simply what the person was actually trying to accomplish.
- **Meticulous accuracy beats speed as a quality standard.** When HP handed control of its Japanese joint venture to local leadership, the new team cut circuit board failure rates to 10 per million against HP's own 4 per thousand, a 400 times improvement, by optimizing every adjustment for accuracy instead of speed. Packard said the discovery reshaped quality targets across the whole company: gains in quality come from meticulous attention to every step, not from moving fast.
- **Apply steady, sustained pressure instead of force when you need people to change.** Packard learned as a rancher that driving cattle through a gate worked best with continuous gentle pressure from behind; push too hard and the herd panics and scatters, ease off completely and they wander back to old habits. He said the same instinct guided him through decades of managing HP's people.
- **Master the opportunity in front of you and let it compound into opportunities you cannot yet predict.** In 1964, HP's $125 million in revenue came entirely from instruments, not one dollar from computers; thirty years later, computer products alone brought in $20 billion. Packard never planned that transition in advance, but by mastering the instrument business completely first, HP was positioned to be pulled into computing when the opportunity appeared.
