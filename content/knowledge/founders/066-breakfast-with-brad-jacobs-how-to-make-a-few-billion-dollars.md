# Breakfast with Brad Jacobs + How To Make A Few Billion Dollars

> **Founder principle (Brad Jacobs):** Repeated company building rests on preparation and a repeatable way of making decisions. Jacobs studies an industry from many angles, looks for changes that create an opening, and recruits people capable of acting on that opening. He treats mental composure, practical technology, and a trustworthy reputation as working assets that improve the quality of those decisions over time.

*David Senra on Brad Jacobs, reflecting on their breakfast conversation and revisiting How to Make a Few Billion Dollars. Founders Podcast · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=txzX11Zf_ZA) · 94 min · published 2024-12-19.*

## Key lessons

- **Make industry research a deliberate operating advantage.** Jacobs reads company filings, trade publications, analyst work, and employee reviews, then speaks with executives, vendors, investors, and skeptical journalists. At breakfast, Senra found him studying a large printout of Senra's book notes and taking further notes during the conversation. Before entering a field, seek perspectives that disagree and build a working account of how that field actually makes money.
- **Choose the structural opportunity before committing to a company.** Jacobs's waste-management thesis combined pressure on small landfills with an opportunity to integrate collection and disposal. The equipment-rental idea emerged through extended conversations with bankers and analysts. Identify the changes that could strengthen or undermine a business model, rather than assuming that execution alone can overcome an unfavorable market.
- **Use technology to change a measurable operating constraint.** His oil brokerage accelerated the sharing of information that had previously moved slowly across markets, and the waste business used better truck routing to improve efficiency. United Rentals acquired its industry's software supplier to develop its own platform and gain an aggregated view of market trends. Name the information delay, wasted capacity, or repeated task that a technology investment will improve.
- **Treat recruiting and incentives as central executive work.** Jacobs argues that a vacant position can be less costly than an unsuitable hire and looks for people who can reconcile conflicting information and revise their views. He links strong compensation to valuable contributions and milestones. Evaluate the profit or capability a candidate could create, alongside salary, before choosing a cheaper but weaker alternative.
- **Express the objective clearly enough that others can act on it.** Senra repeatedly notices how easily Jacobs communicates his aim: working with talented people to produce strong shareholder returns. At breakfast, Jacobs describes the leader's sequence of setting a vision, recruiting the team, and establishing incentives. Refine the message until colleagues can explain both the intended result and their role in producing it.
- **Create distance between an anxious reaction and a business decision.** Jacobs describes examining the worst plausible outcome, considering how he would respond, and asking how he would advise a friend facing the same situation. His mentor also taught him to approach business problems as opportunities to improve an operation. Use those questions to identify a next action while keeping a passing emotion from becoming the entire assessment.
- **Respond differently when the thesis is broken and when the price is wrong.** Jacobs sold road-rental operations after the expected government spending failed to materialize, accepting a substantial loss. In the XPO short-seller episode, his team instead examined the allegations in detail and concluded that buying back depressed shares was attractive. Reassess the underlying evidence before deciding whether a setback calls for exit, repair, or additional investment.
- **Ask employees directly where the company is wasting effort.** After acquisitions, Jacobs sometimes discovered that people close to the work had never been asked how to improve it. He sought their strongest improvement idea and the practice they considered most foolish. Give those questions enough focus that frontline knowledge can reach decision makers without being buried in a broad survey or an optimistic management summary.
- **Build a reputation that makes the next opportunity easier to pursue.** Senra connects Jacobs's long relationships, continued learning, and help for younger entrepreneurs to the trust surrounding later ventures. Jacobs also credits lessons from his own mentor that remained useful decades later. Treat small interactions and generous exchanges of experience as part of a long record, because future partners encounter that record before they assess the next proposal.
