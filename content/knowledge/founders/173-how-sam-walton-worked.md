# How Sam Walton Worked

> **Founder principle (Sam Walton):** Walton admitted his idea was absurdly simple: buy cheap, sell low every day, and do it with a smile. What made it powerful was how seriously he took it. He spent years perfecting a single store, copied every good idea in retail without shame, kept costs lower than anyone in his industry, and stayed willing to reverse course the moment new facts arrived. Once the system was proven, he poured fuel on it, going from four Walmarts at 48 to the largest retailer ever built.

*David Senra on Sam Walton, reading from Sam Walton: The Inside Story of America's Richest Man by Vance Trimble. Founders Podcast · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dAx0W29-FWE) · published 2026-09-21.*

## Key lessons

- **Take a simple idea very seriously, and drop the plan when the facts change.** Senra ties Walton's buy cheap, sell low approach to Charlie Munger's advice about taking a simple idea very seriously. Like Henry Singleton and Michael Bloomberg, Walton had no grand five year plan. He held firm principles but was neither too vain nor too blind to turn 180 degrees on a tactic that was failing. He later built this into the culture as a low RC, meaning a low resistance to change.
- **Apprentice inside the best operator in your field first.** Walton started at J.C. Penney for $85 a month, when the chain already had more than 1,500 stores. He took away three lifelong ideas: small towns can be big markets, customer satisfaction comes before profit, and managers should share in the profits. He also took away a lesson from J.C. Penney himself, who rewrapped a package with less paper and string and told the clerks the profit lived in what they saved.
- **Master one store before you scale, and own the ground you build on.** At 27 he borrowed $25,000 from his father-in-law to buy a Ben Franklin franchise in Newport, Arkansas, and grew it to about $225,000 a year in sales. His landlord saw how well it was doing and refused to renew the lease so he could take over the store. Walton told his lawyer he was not whipped. He moved to Bentonville, insisted on buying the building this time, and started over from zero at 32, working off a plywood desk on sawhorses.
- **Hardship can be the source of the next idea.** Driving about 250 miles of winding Ozark roads each way between his two stores, Walton realized that if he could run two towns he could run a dozen. Hearing a small plane overhead, he chartered one, saw an eight hour drive shrink to 90 minutes, and became a pilot. Flying was what made a chain spread across rural towns possible.
- **Stay a merchant, not a part-time developer.** Seeing the rise of shopping centers, he decided he could develop them all over the country with no money. The first one failed and he ran out of cash, and he went back to building stores and never lost that focus again. When he could not get hula hoops as a tiny buyer, he and his team made thousands at night from plastic pipe and hauled them to the stores on a john boat trailer.
- **Copy shamelessly and study the competition on your knees.** Walton was fascinated by discounting and openly modeled Walmart on Harry Cunningham's Kmart, which ignored him in small towns long enough for him to learn. When Ben Franklin's executives laughed at his pitch to franchise discount stores at about 12.5 percent wholesale margin instead of 20 to 25, one of them visited a Kmart the next day and found Walton already there, on hands and knees looking under a display. In 1983 he flew to San Diego to study Sol Price's wholesale club and copied it into Sam's Club, which reached 105 clubs and over $5 billion in sales by 1990.
- **Fight the palookas before the champions.** Munger described Walton's early strategy as a prizefighter building a record by knocking out small town merchants with a more efficient system, then taking on the big chains once he was big. The book compares Walmart with a similar chain: Walmart was 26 times larger yet earned profit margins three times higher and nearly twice the sales per square foot.
- **Count costs down to the letters on the sign.** Part of the reason for the name Walmart was that fewer letters meant cheaper signs to buy, light, and repair. The first Walmart was only 16,000 square feet, but it made money from day one on $700,000 in first year sales. The eighth went into a shuttered bottling plant with pipes in the floor and 28 window fans in place of air conditioning. Administrative costs eventually ran around 2 percent of revenue.
- **Manage by walking around, and fix the front line on the spot.** He flagged down his own semi to ride 100 miles with a driver, brought donuts to the loading dock at 2:30 a.m. to ask workers for ideas, and kept the headquarters hidden in Bentonville so people would have to come find the company. When a friend's daughter could not return a pair of shorts, he had the manager hold them up at the Saturday meeting and recite the motto, satisfaction guaranteed. Shoe returns were supposed to come with a free pair of socks.
- **Once it works, be intolerant of slowness.** At 48 he had 18 variety stores and just four Walmarts. In 1969 he told skeptical lenders that $21 million in sales would reach about $230 million by 1975; it came in at $236 million. Between 1974 and 1977 the chain went from 78 to 153 stores and from $167 million to $478 million, and he then bought whole chains, including one with 104 stores, to convert at once. At 61 he dropped his view that computers were overhead and spent $500 million linking stores and warehouses.
- **Plan for your family early, then get back to work.** On his banker father-in-law's advice, Walton put one fifth of the business in trust for each child in 1954, when each share was worth about $5,000. Decades later those stakes were worth billions each. When the October 1987 crash cut his Walmart holdings by about a billion dollars, reporters asked for his reaction and found he had not even heard about it.
