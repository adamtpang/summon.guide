# Steve Jobs in Exile

> **Founder principle (Steve Jobs):** The twelve years at NeXT show a brilliant founder making the opposite of every good decision because he was overfunded, motivated by revenge, trapped by his own perfectionism, and surrounded by people who would not tell him the truth. Survival came only when he sold the hardware he loved, sold the software that customers were actually using, and let facts rather than personality settle arguments. The leader who returned to Apple was built by that failure: precise, humble about talent, and willing to let others present.

## Key lessons

- **Check your motive before you build.** Much of NeXT's early energy went into sticking it to Apple, including Wall Street Journal ads mocking the company at a time when NeXT had no product, no revenue, and no plan. The older Jobs said that the longer he lived, the more convinced he was that motives make all the difference. At Apple the motive had been insanely great products; at NeXT it was revenge, and the spending reflected it.
- **Too much money kills the hustle that made you.** He paid $100,000 for a logo before there was a product, and staff started measuring purchases in units of that fee. With nothing shipped, offices got $2,000 chairs, $10,000 sofas, and $450 desk phones, and the burn reached a million dollars a month. Ross Perot later named his own error plainly: he had given Jobs far too much money, and hunger disappears when the bank account is full.
- **Perfectionism without a deadline is just indecision.** Every chip layout got changed as it neared completion, so engineers who knew the honest answer was a year kept saying one more month. Two chip designers collected $25,000 bonuses for a finish they announced, walked out, and the chip did not work, costing a full year. He built a factory before deciding the circuit board, then fired the manufacturing chief who had prepared for both options, and pushed a magnesium case with matte black paint despite warnings that air bubbles would ruin the finish.
- **Build a system in which people tell you the truth.** Executives complained freely for an hour, then went silent one by one the moment he walked in. Sales staff joked that he would send you north until you hit the Arctic and then explode. The team counted units shipped to distributors as sales to hide poor numbers from him, and he refused to show a CFO candidate the books. Ed Catmull's counter-method was to present facts and wait, sometimes for months, until Jobs said he was right; Jobs later fired board members because they never disagreed with him.
- **Never overplay your hand with the people funding you.** He walked out of an airport rather than present to 800 IBM engineers because there was only one set of slides, and IBM read it as lack of commitment and killed a deal worth tens of millions. He refused at the last minute to sign a government-sales deal Perot had spent months arranging, and Perot was done. NeXT had burned $39 million against a plan to lose three; he later told Catmull the lesson was never to overplay your hand.
- **Be able to answer what business you are in.** Six years in, Andy Grove asked NeXT's leadership that question and nobody could agree. The machine that academics said had to cost under $3,000 shipped at $10,500 to $12,500, and 205 units went out in the first year while the plan required 16,000. Perot's simple questions, such as not announcing a product before you can ship it and what it will take to get sales, had no answers either.
- **When the world changes, change with it, even if it means selling what you love.** The obvious pivot to software was resisted for years because it meant giving up beautiful hardware, until Canon bought the hardware division for $20 million with the doors otherwise closing on Monday. Corporate customers had been saying all along that they built mission-critical apps five to ten times faster on NeXTSTEP. After nine years of losses the company posted its first profit, a million dollars on nearly $50 million in revenue.
- **Sell the outcome, not the license.** On Larry Ellison's advice that most IT projects fail, NeXT built a professional services group that both guaranteed customer success and surfaced new product ideas. With WebObjects, Jobs told the company to burn the boats and copied Oracle's consulting model, deploying teams on site for months. Dell had been quoted two years by IBM for an online configurator; NeXT delivered in a week, and Dell's web sales grew to $3 million a day.
- **Let the hierarchy invert around talent.** By late 1995 he was saying that talented people can get another job in ten minutes, so the CEO effectively works for the people doing the brilliant work; the team assembled by then stayed with him for the rest of his life. In the pitch that sold NeXT to Apple he was pragmatic and precise, handed the demo to his head of software, acknowledged engineers' concerns as solvable, and settled the price in a five-minute kitchen conversation. The whole reunion began because a NeXT product manager asked why nobody had simply called Apple.
