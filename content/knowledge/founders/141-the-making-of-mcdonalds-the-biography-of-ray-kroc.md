# The Making of McDonald's: The Biography of Ray Kroc

> **Founder principle (Ray Kroc):** Copy an existing product exactly if it works, but change the business model underneath it, read every contract clause as a future liability, and treat advertising, visible details, and total personal sacrifice as investments you make without apology, even when they cost you the people who paid the same price you did.

*David Senra on Ray Kroc, reading from Grinding It Out: The Making of McDonald's, the autobiography of Ray Kroc. Founders Podcast · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=E6OSZ8uRONA) · 58 min · published 2024-02-25.*

## Key lessons

- **Treat total schedule sacrifice as the entry price, not a phase you grow out of.** During his seventeen years selling paper cups he worked the streets from early morning to late afternoon, then played piano at a radio station most nights until the small hours, six or seven days a week, and at 52, while still selling multimixers full time, he added helping open his first McDonald's before and after that same workday.
- **Change the business model underneath a product you did not even invent.** Kroc copied the McDonald brothers' stripped down restaurant format exactly, but his fortune came from Harry Sonneborn's insight that McDonald's should buy the land under every restaurant and lease it back to the franchisee, turning a company collecting a sliver of gross sales into one with steady upfront revenue and real collateral banks would actually lend against.
- **Read every clause in a contract as a liability before you sign it, because the one you wave off will run your life for a decade.** Acting as his own attorney, Kroc agreed he could not change any of the McDonald brothers' plans without their written, registered mail approval, and the brothers spent years verbally approving changes by phone while refusing to ever put it in writing, leaving him in technical default from his very first store.
- **Obsess over the small, visible details customers actually notice, and treat any lapse as a genuine crisis.** Kroc would get "screaming mad" over an unlit sign or a scrap of litter in the parking lot, because to him perfection in those small things was the whole game and everything else was secondary.
- **Treat advertising as an investment you underspend at your peril, not a line item to minimize.** He fought the McDonald brothers for years over a shared advertising fund they refused to pay into, and later, offered an expensive California television campaign paid for by raising the price of a hamburger a single penny, he ran the campaign anyway and refused the price increase.
- **A partner's loss of faith in the business costs him more than it costs you.** Harry Sonneborn wanted to halt construction ahead of a feared 1967 recession, Kroc refused, Harry resigned, and Harry was so sure McDonald's would decline without him that he sold his stock for a few million dollars, stock that would later have been worth over 100 million.
- **Structure a buyout so the seller's own incentive pulls in your direction, even if it costs you a stream you could have kept.** To buy out the McDonald brothers' half point of every dollar of systemwide sales for 2.7 million dollars, Kroc borrowed the money from twelve charitable and educational institutions by handing them that same royalty stream, a stream Kroc figured was already worth roughly 15 million dollars a year by the time the book was written.
- **Demanding total sacrifice from the people around you is a real cost, not a side effect you can wave away.** Kroc asked the same all in commitment from Harry Sonneborn and from June Martino, the company's first employee, who by her own account missed every one of her two sons' birthdays and graduations building the company, and he later fired Harry after a dispute and pushed June into retirement once neither fit where he wanted to go next.
- **An idea everyone else dismissed as too small can be the biggest opportunity in the room, if you go see the actual mechanism yourself.** Kroc had sold paper cups and then milkshake mixers for decades before he flew out just to understand why one small California restaurant was running eight machines at once, and only by watching the McDonald brothers' high volume system in person did he see what its own owners had no interest in scaling, later joking that people called him an overnight success and "30 years is a long long night."
