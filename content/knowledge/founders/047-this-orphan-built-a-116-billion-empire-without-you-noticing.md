# This Orphan Built a $116 Billion Empire Without You Noticing

> **Founder principle (Leonardo Del Vecchio):** Never accept minority control over your own work. Eliminate every intermediary between you and the end customer one layer at a time, reinvest almost everything back into the business rather than into your own comfort, and treat any sense of arrival, at any age or scale, as the actual danger to what you built.

*David Senra on Leonardo Del Vecchio, reading from an untranslated Italian biography of Del Vecchio and a magazine profile on the Luxottica-EssilorLuxottica merger. Founders Podcast · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bijdxv0lG7w) · 68 min · published 2025-07-14.*

## Key lessons

- **Refuse minority ownership even when it's the only offer on the table.** When his own best customers proposed a factory partnership with him as a minority shareholder, he said it would "castrate his ambitions," and structured himself as general partner instead so operating control stayed with him.
- **When a partner tries to starve you out, go find another lender rather than fold.** After his partners pulled his credit line to force him out, he found financing elsewhere, bought them out entirely a few years later, and took their customers with him.
- **Make quality, not price, the thing your best customers actually pay for.** He learned early that clients ordering eyeglass parts cared more about precision than cost, and built his entire strategy around segments of the industry willing to pay for excellence.
- **Eliminate intermediaries between you and the end customer, one layer at a time.** He moved from subcontractor to frame maker, from frame maker to his own brand, then to owning distributors, and finally to owning the retail stores themselves, removing one party he didn't control at every stage.
- **Reinvest almost everything back into the operation instead of your own comfort.** He kept a plaque on his desk reading "every euro saved is an extra euro in profit," and routed profits into research, automation, and new machinery rather than personal spending.
- **Price a contested acquisition using your own numbers, not the seller's story.** For Ray-Ban, he calculated units produced, cost per unit, and expected sale price himself, arrived at a number every other bidder called overpaying, and then sold roughly ten times as many units as before.
- **Never depend on a partner who won't defend your standards as fiercely as you would.** He chose to own frames, lenses, distributors, and stores outright rather than stay reliant on outside partners who might compromise on quality.
- **Come back from retirement rather than watch your life's work drift.** After stepping back and installing an outside chief executive, he was unhappy with the change in direction and returned to run the company himself in his late 70s.
- **Keep raising your own bar regardless of age or scale already reached.** Still reviewing individual store sales data on his phone in his 80s, he said "I have never been satisfied," treating any feeling of having arrived as the real risk to the business.
