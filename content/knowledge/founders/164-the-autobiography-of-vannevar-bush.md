# The Autobiography of Vannevar Bush

> **Founder principle (Vannevar Bush):** Written in his own words at eighty, Bush's central operating claim is that organizations lose for only two reasons, confused authority or a leader with poor judgment, so make sure everyone always knows exactly who decides. Route around bias in the system rather than fighting it directly, control the display of your own emotions even when you cannot control the feeling, and never mistake a bare invention for a finished business: it still needs promotion, financing, and marketing before it is worth anything.

*David Senra on Vannevar Bush, reading from Bush's own autobiography, Pieces of the Action. Founders Podcast · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=07Kp7epbfY0) · 65 min · published 2023-10-26.*

## Key lessons

- **Eliminate confused authority before anything else.** Bush wrote that there are two ways to lose a campaign between roughly equal opponents: unclear lines of authority, or a top commander with poor judgment. He insisted there should never be any doubt inside an organization about where a decision actually gets made or how fast it will come.
- **Refuse to negotiate under a collective ultimatum.** When an entire section of his staff once walked into his office and resigned as a group, Bush's response was that "one does not resign in a time of war," told them plainly to get back to work, and looked into their complaint afterward on his own terms. Meeting a coordinated power play with a firm no, then investigating calmly once the pressure is off, kept authority from shifting to whoever could organize the loudest walkout.
- **Define what you owe each other, then hold to it.** Bush said his working relationship with Roosevelt came down to one exchange: he was handed a hard job, never interfered with, and always backed up when it mattered, and in return he gave the president absolute loyalty even where he privately disagreed with his politics. He treated that trade, a hard job plus real backing in exchange for loyalty, as the actual contract underneath the formal one.
- **Rename the function to defeat the bias, without changing the substance.** Older military officers at the time looked down on engineers as little more than salesmen, so Bush simply relabeled his engineers as scientists. Nothing about their work changed, but the same people who had been dismissed were now listened to, because he fixed the label the bias attached to rather than arguing the bias away.
- **Sort the people around you into three types, and manage each differently.** Bush divided organizational actors into tyros, freewheelers who exploit unclear authority and should be circumvented or removed, amateurs, still learning but capable of becoming professionals, and professionals, masters of their craft worth deferring to. Knowing which type you are dealing with, he argued, tells you immediately whether to trust, teach, or route around someone.
- **Control the display of the emotion, not the emotion itself.** Bush later judged his own worst mistakes to be the moments he let his anger show rather than the moments he actually felt angry. Feeling something strongly is unavoidable in a demanding job; broadcasting it to the room in front of you rarely helps and often costs you standing.
- **An invention is worthless until it is joined to everything else a business needs.** Bush wrote plainly that an invention has no value on its own unless it is paired with promotion, financing, development, engineering, and marketing, and that an inventor who believes otherwise is left holding a liability instead of a business. He treated the idea itself as only the first, and smallest, part of the work.
- **Invent for the act itself, not only for the payoff.** Bush compared inventing to writing a poem: a poet can find real joy in a poem even if it is never published, and an inventor can find the same "elation of creation" in a device whether or not it ever makes money. He traced part of his own persistence to that satisfaction existing independently of practical results, not only to ambition or duty.
- **Step aside deliberately once your own students surpass you.** Teaching a graduate course late in his career, Bush realized his teaching assistants now knew the subject better than he did, and decided on the spot to get out of younger men's way whenever he could no longer genuinely compete with them. He described real relief and satisfaction in that choice, rather than treating it as a loss of status.
