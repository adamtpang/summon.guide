# The Financial Genius Behind A Century of Wall Street Scandals: Ivar Kreuger

> **Founder principle (Ivar Kreuger):** A business built on real operations can still be destroyed by a financing structure that only works if it keeps growing. Kreuger ran two legitimate, profitable companies and then layered a dividend promise on top of them that only fresh investor cash could sustain, so the day he could no longer raise new money, decades of real construction and manufacturing work collapsed along with the fraud built on top of it.

*David Senra on Ivar Kreuger, reading from The Match King by Frank Partnoy. Founders Podcast · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cdVeSJHewDM) · 76 min · published 2024-05-15.*

## Key lessons

- **Shift the risk your customer fears most onto yourself, and charge for it.** Early in his career, Kreuger's construction firm was the first in Europe to guarantee a fixed completion date, paying the client a penalty for every day it ran late and collecting a bonus for every day it finished early. Clients paid a premium for that certainty, and the firm became the best regarded builder in Sweden within a few years, the collateral he later used to enter the match industry.
- **Copy the how, not the what, of a proven monopoly builder.** A confident 28 year old Kreuger, writing home that "I shall do great things," studied Rockefeller's and Carnegie's methods in close detail, then applied the same playbook (vertical integration, buying up the suppliers of raw materials, undercutting and choking off anyone who would not sell) to a completely different industry: Swedish matches. The tactics transferred even though the product did not.
- **Manufacture legitimacy before you ever ask for money.** Before meeting his key American banker, Kreuger paid an intermediary to plant a casual mention of his companies in conversation and let rumors of his success circulate through the finance industry, then deliberately made himself hard to reach. By the time the banker requested a meeting, the appetite was already built.
- **A simple, legitimate story can carry an enormous amount of weight.** Kreuger's pitch to American investors reduced to seven words: government loans in exchange for match monopolies. Backed by two real, high dividend paying businesses, it required no further explanation, and that simplicity is what let hundreds of millions of dollars flow in behind almost no financial disclosure.
- **Pick directors who are well connected and too busy to look closely.** Kreuger seated the nephew of John D. Rockefeller, a man already serving on more than sixty other boards, believing correctly that his name would lend credibility without ever auditing the underlying numbers.
- **An auditor who depends on you financially is not a check on you.** Kreuger's own auditor kept raising questions about undisclosed subsidiaries and inconsistent numbers, then kept dropping them, because the growing fees, a paid vacation timed around a critical negotiation, and an eventual partnership all flowed from staying agreeable. Incentives, not diligence, decided what actually got investigated.
- **Financial complexity can substitute for an actual answer, for a while.** Rather than clarify his structure, Kreuger built hundreds of interlocking subsidiaries, forged rubber stamp replicas of foreign officials' signatures, and staged fake phone calls and hired actors to impress visitors. Confusion bought him years of runway, but it never bought him solvency.
- **A dividend that requires new capital to fund itself is a countdown, not a return.** Kreuger paid double digit dividends that operating profits alone could not cover, so paying last year's investors required raising money from this year's. That arrangement survives only as long as the pool of new money keeps growing, and he kept promising more than the business could earn until the 1929 crash cut off the capital he needed to keep the promise.
- **The cost of the fraud landed on people who never chose the risk.** When Kreuger could not close his largest deal right after the crash, a 70 year old, previously prestigious bank that had backed him for a decade went bankrupt alongside him, and investigators estimated total losses near two billion dollars, more than Sweden's national debt at the time. The night before a meeting he could not survive, he wrote that "I made such a mess of things" and shot himself; the two real, profitable businesses he had spent 25 years building were destroyed by the financing structure stacked on top of them, not by anything wrong with the businesses themselves.
