# David Ogilvy (Confessions of an Advertising Man)

> **Founder principle (David Ogilvy):** Treat the agency as the visible extension of one formidable, opinionated individual rather than a committee. Sell with facts and a single, clear benefit, repeat a winning campaign for years instead of chasing novelty, refuse mediocre standards in your own staff and your own work, and protect real, unstructured rest so new ideas keep arriving.

*David Senra on David Ogilvy, reading from Confessions of an Advertising Man by David Ogilvy. Founders Podcast · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Y3jtPRJwsSM) · 48 min · published 2024-03-24.*

## Key lessons

- **Don't let a client negotiate your fee down when the real leverage sits in their much larger media budget.** Ogilvy's recurring answer to clients who haggled over the agency's cut was blunt, "pay peanuts and you get monkeys." His point was that shaving a few cents off the agency's 15 percent does nothing compared to what better work could do with the 85 percent the client was already spending on time and space, and no manufacturer ever got rich by underpaying the people making the ad.
- **Sell one specific, factual benefit, not a mood.** He told his staff to decide exactly one promised benefit before writing anything, whether it was better flavor, a whiter wash, or more miles per gallon, then to back it with real detail instead of adjectives. His own Rolls-Royce advertisements carried no gracious-living language at all, only facts he had mined from an internal engineering report most copywriters would never have bothered to read.
- **The headline carries most of the sale, so give it most of the work.** Ogilvy estimated that five times as many people read a headline as read the body copy beneath it, so writing the headline was spending 80 cents of the advertising dollar. He never wrote fewer than sixteen headlines for a single ad, and found that longer headlines with the selling promise built in consistently outsold short, clever ones.
- **A winning campaign is not a fad to retire, it is an asset to keep running.** He argued that an advertiser is never selling to the same fixed audience twice, since a new wave of customers reaches the same life event, like a wedding or a first car, every year, so a proven advertisement should keep running until it visibly stops working rather than being replaced out of boredom. His eye patch campaign for Hathaway shirts ran 21 years, and his Dove soap campaign was still running after 31.
- **Set standards of service you would enforce in a professional kitchen.** Working under a head chef at the Hotel Majestic in Paris taught him that a team cannot tolerate incompetence without demoralizing everyone competent around it, and that a promise made to a customer has to be honored no matter what it costs the agency in extra effort. He carried both rules directly into the agency and praised his own staff as rarely as that chef had praised him, so that praise, when it came, actually meant something.
- **Choose to stay small and excellent rather than large and average, and say no to being acquired.** He deliberately capped the agency's client list instead of chasing bureaucratic size, and turned down larger agencies that tried to buy him out because he wanted to remain the formidable individual at the center of his own firm rather than an employee inside someone else's. His summary of the whole philosophy was that a great company is just "the lengthened shadow of a great individual," not the product of a committee.
- **Recruit for genius, then tolerate the difficulty that comes with it.** Ogilvy believed mediocre people instinctively resent and try to destroy real talent, so a leader's job is to protect the difficult, nonconformist people who actually produce great work instead of smoothing them into conformity. He enforced this discipline even in his personnel policy: to keep office politics out of the agency, he had a standing rule that when two employees married, one of them had to leave.
- **Protect real, unstructured rest as part of the job, not a reward for finishing it.** He described himself as nearly incapable of logical thought and said his ideas surfaced instead through long hot baths, gardening, bird watching, retreats with the Amish, and full vacations meant to let his mind go completely idle. He paired that deliberate rest with hard work, an open mind, and an insatiable curiosity, treating all of it as necessary inputs rather than opposites.
- **When you have no reputation yet, send value before you ever ask for the business, then close in person.** Starting out with no track record, he targeted companies that did not already have an agency and mailed them genuinely useful research and progress reports instead of self-promotion, building enough credibility that a prospect once recited his own paragraphs back to him from memory. When Shell's president left for London mid-pitch, Ogilvy flew to London himself rather than wait for a reply, because closing the deal in person, in his account, was what actually won it.
