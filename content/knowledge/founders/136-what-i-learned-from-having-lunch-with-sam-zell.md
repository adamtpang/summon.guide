# What I learned from having lunch with Sam Zell

> **Founder principle (Sam Zell and William Zeckendorf):** At 81, decades after selling his company for tens of billions of dollars, Sam Zell still read constantly, still chased new deals, and still treated sharing what he had learned as an obligation rather than a favor, telling David to go read the very book, William Zeckendorf's autobiography, that had shaped his own thinking about real estate as a young man. Zeckendorf's own story is the warning inside that same lesson: the same aggressive, all in appetite for deals that built one of the largest real estate empires in American history, run without ever building a cash reserve, is also exactly what erased it.

*David Senra recounts a personal lunch with Sam Zell, then reads from Zeckendorf, the autobiography of William Zeckendorf. Founders Podcast · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZPsJ3FlaRUQ) · 90 min · published 2024-03-11.*

## Key lessons

- **Treat learning from the people who came before you as a lifelong habit, not a phase you grow out of.** At 81, having sold his company for close to forty billion dollars, Zell could still name the company and the biography of almost any obscure historical operator David raised in conversation, evidence that the habit of reading and studying history built the fortune rather than following it.
- **Optimize for freedom over possessions, and let the money follow.** Zell's repeated advice across the lunch was that greater wealth mostly buys nicer versions of the same clutter you now have to manage, while freedom, control over your own time and work, is what actually compounds; he said plainly that he chased freedom, not money, and the money came from that.
- **Treat sharing what you learned as an obligation, not a favor.** Long after he had any financial need to work, Zell still flew around the world unpaid to speak to other entrepreneurs and named reading strangers' letters about how his own book changed their lives as his favorite part of the week.
- **Prove your value before you ask to be paid for it, and quit the moment you are lowballed.** As a young broker Zeckendorf leased almost an entire half-empty building on his own initiative; when his employer offered him a small raise instead of the commission that work was actually worth, he quit the next day rather than accept underpayment for a proven result.
- **When you lack capital, sell pieces of the deal instead of walking away from it.** Chronically short on cash, Zeckendorf routinely formed syndicates bringing in banks, insurance companies, and outside investors to fund options and purchases he could never have financed alone, most notably the land assembly that became the United Nations headquarters site.
- **Break one asset into its component parts and sell each piece to whoever values it most.** Zeckendorf's own technique split a property into its land, ground lease, operating lease, and residual position and financed each piece separately; by his own account this turned a Manhattan building the open market valued at ten million dollars into fifteen million dollars in his hands.
- **A cash reserve is what lets you buy while everyone else is forced to sell.** The episode points to families like the Astors, who owned their real estate free of debt and could ride out the Great Depression, against heavily leveraged owners who were forced into distress sales, a contrast Zeckendorf identified himself without ever applying it to his own company.
- **Saying yes to every deal that crosses your desk eventually drowns you in complexity, not competitors.** By 1960 Zeckendorf's firm had spread across dozens of undercapitalized projects that all needed cash at once; he later diagnosed the collapse himself as a case of taking on too much rather than too little, the exact opposite failure of running out of business.
- **A damaged reputation reverses the mechanism that built your fortune in the first place.** Early in his career, the Zeckendorf name alone raised a property's price because buyers assumed anything he touched had hidden value; once the firm was visibly in trouble, owning a property under his name instead signaled buyers to lowball him and wait for a forced sale.
