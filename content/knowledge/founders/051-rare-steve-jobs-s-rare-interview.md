# Rare Steve Jobs's Rare Interview

> **Founder principle (Steve Jobs):** The gap between an average person and the best in a field isn't small, it can be fifty or a hundred to one, so build your team around the outliers, not the average. Build to a private standard of excellence nobody else will inspect, put your invention inside a historical story big enough to explain why it matters, and don't be afraid to just ask for help, because almost nobody actually says no when asked directly.

*David Senra reading Steve Jobs's 1985 Playboy interview. Founders Podcast · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=yY1Llgq1VbY) · 41 min · published 2025-06-10.*

## Key lessons

- **Chase the outliers, not the merely competent.** Jobs said that in most fields the gap between average and the best is at most two to one, but in the field he cared about it was fifty or a hundred to one, so "you must find extraordinary people" instead of building a bigger team of average ones.
- **Build to satisfy yourselves first, not a market research report.** Describing the Macintosh team, he said, "We built it for ourselves," comparing it to a carpenter who finishes the invisible back of a drawer with quality wood purely so he can sleep at night. The unseen parts of a product still have to meet the maker's own bar.
- **Be a missionary, not a mercenary.** Jobs drew a sharp line between people obsessed with their product and customers and people just trying to flip a stock, calling his own team "missionaries" against IBM's "number crunchers." He noted the paradox that the missionaries, focused on the product rather than the money, are usually the ones who end up making more of it.
- **Strip complexity out until the product needs no manual.** He pointed out the telephone beat the telegraph despite doing the same job, because the telegraph required forty hours of training in Morse code while anyone could already talk and listen. His standard for Apple's own products was the same: fewer choices, fewer prices, turn it on and it works.
- **Place your invention inside a bigger historical story.** Jobs compared the personal computer's infancy to the petrochemical revolution and to the early telegraph, and reframed the target market as not just the Fortune 500 but the "Fortune 5 million" small businesses IBM was ignoring. Context this large made a two-year-old company's ambitions sound inevitable rather than reckless.
- **Just ask directly for what you need.** At twelve years old he looked up Bill Hewlett's home number, called him, and asked for spare parts, which led to a summer job. He said years later, "I have never found anyone who said no," and treated that willingness to simply pick up the phone as the real dividing line between people who build things and people who only imagine building them.
- **Recruit talented troublemakers on purpose.** Jobs called Apple "an Ellis Island company," built from bright individual contributors who'd been misfits everywhere else, and pointed to Polaroid's Edwin Land, ousted from his own company, as proof that brilliant people are "package deals" you have to accept, quirks included, if you want their talent at all.
- **Watch for the moment a growing company stops seeing its own people.** He warned that as companies scale into multi-billion-dollar entities they add layers of middle management between the people running things and the people doing the work, so creative employees must convince five layers just to act, and the best of them eventually leave for it.
- **Frame the work as part of something larger than the company.** Jobs said humans didn't invent the clothes they wear, the food they grow, or the language and math they use, and that building something to hand back into that shared pool, however uncertain the outcome, is a rare and worthwhile use of a working life.
