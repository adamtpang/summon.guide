# The Greatest Moneymaker of All Time: Jim Simons

> **Founder principle (Jim Simons):** Solve a problem nobody else is working on instead of competing head to head, and gather a data and computing advantage so large that competitors cannot catch up before you have already moved on. Once a tested system proves itself, trust its output even when you cannot explain its reasoning, and reward the people who built it by keeping outside money and outside attention as far from the operation as possible.

*David Senra on Jim Simons, reading from The Man Who Solved the Market by Gregory Zuckerman, with a supplementary passage from Softwar: An Intimate Portrait of Larry Ellison and Oracle by Matthew Symonds. ## Key lessons

- **Pick a problem where you have no competition instead of racing the pack.** Simons's own rule was to do something new rather than run with other people chasing the same trade, reasoning that if he was one of many people working the same problem his odds of winning were slim. He left tenured academic life at 40 to build an automated trading system nobody else believed was possible, precisely because almost no one else was trying it.
- **Build a data advantage before you have the technology to use it.** Years before computers were fast enough to run his models, Simons had staff buy stacks of historic commodity and bond data and send someone to the Federal Reserve to hand copy interest rate histories no one else had bothered to digitize. By the time computing caught up to the idea, the firm already owned decades of clean historical data that took competitors years to assemble.
- **Recruit by selling the problem, not the paycheck.** Simons pitched mathematicians who had no interest in finance by framing trading as the ultimate unsolved puzzle rather than a business. That approach brought in James Ax, then Sandor Straus, then a pipeline of hires poached from IBM's computational linguistics group who saw that predicting markets and predicting speech were the same kind of problem.
- **Shorten your holding period so the law of large numbers works for you.** When a partner's longer-term trades kept producing losses, the firm switched to holding positions for about a day and a half instead, treating the business like a casino that only needs to win a bit more than half its bets to profit reliably. The new approach worked almost immediately and turned 1990 into a 55.9 percent gain after a losing year.
- **Trust a tested system even when you cannot explain why it works.** Simons was uncomfortable for years that the model's trade suggestions came out of a black box he could not reduce to an equation, but he learned to treat it the way he treated planetary motion: he didn't need to know why the trades worked to act on the fact that they did, so long as the historical testing held up.
- **Study your winners, not just your losses.** The insight that pushed the firm toward short-term trading came from analyzing which trades had actually made money rather than dwelling on the losers, the same discipline Senra notes another famous investor practiced: most people in finance spend their time studying what went wrong instead of what is already working.
- **Cap the fund and keep the money inside the building.** Once the strategy was proven, Simons refused pressure to keep growing outside assets under management, raised fees on outside investors, and eventually removed every non-employee from the fund entirely so that all future profits stayed with the people doing the work.
- **Guard the edge with total secrecy.** Renaissance stayed quiet about its methods even after wild success invited outside curiosity, on the reasoning that visibility invites competition and the only real defense in a discovered edge is a low profile; Simons preferred, in his own words, no attention at all rather than the mixed blessing of fame.
- **Do not chase adjacent businesses you do not understand.** After studying the collapse of Long-Term Capital Management, Simons's team took the lesson that the firm had drifted into markets it did not fully grasp, and used it as a reminder to keep sharpening their existing edge rather than expanding into new territory just because it was available.
