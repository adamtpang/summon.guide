# The Simple Genius of Haruki Murakami

> **Founder principle (Haruki Murakami):** Choose the craft that actually fits your temperament rather than the one that looks impressive, then protect it with a fixed daily routine held at sustainable intensity instead of sporadic bursts. Build for the minority who genuinely want what you make, and grade your own failures honestly instead of blaming circumstance, because no one else can tell you whether the work is truly yours.

*David Senra on Haruki Murakami, reading from What I Talk About When I Talk About Running by Haruki Murakami. Founders Podcast · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BWoRH5AtYF0) · 56 min · published 2024-08-15.*

## Key lessons

- **Pick a craft that fits your actual temperament, not what looks impressive.** Murakami is an introvert who dislikes needing other people or special equipment for anything he does, and says plainly of himself, "that's just the way I am," a line he treats as one of the most important in the book.
- **Stop each day's work while you still want more, not after you have emptied the tank.** He deliberately halts writing at the point where he still feels he could keep going, the same discipline he notes Hemingway used, so the next day starts with momentum instead of dread.
- **Hold a fixed daily routine at sustainable intensity, because the flywheel only works if the pace never breaks.** His schedule of a four a.m. start, five to six hours of writing, a run, a swim, reading, and sleep by nine p.m. repeats seven days a week unchanged, on the reasoning that once you set the pace, the rest will follow.
- **Build for the minority who genuinely want what you make, and stop worrying about the rest.** Running his jazz bar, he judged the business a success if just one customer in ten became a repeat customer, held that same standard once his novels split readers the same way, and let his readership compound slowly as that minority grew and told others.
- **When you find the real commitment, cut off the profitable fallback instead of hedging with it.** He sold his money making jazz bar rather than keep it as backup income while writing part time, reasoning that half hearted effort guarantees regret even where full commitment might still fail.
- **Let one core discipline force out habits that conflict with it, instead of trying to fix them directly.** He chose running specifically because it required no partner and no special place, mirroring the solitude that writing already demanded, and the running habit is what finally displaced a sixty cigarette a day habit he had been unable to quit any other way.
- **Grade your own failures honestly, and rebuild from fundamentals instead of making excuses.** After under training for a marathon and having to walk to finish it, breaking his own rule against walking, he diagnosed the cause as simply not enough training and rebuilt his preparation from scratch rather than blaming the course or the day.
- **Know the difference between pushing to your limit and pushing past what the practice can survive.** Grinding through a roughly sixty mile ultramarathon by telling himself he was "a piece of machinery," he burned out so completely that he lost his desire to run at all for several years afterward, proof that the same discipline that builds a decades long practice can also break it.
