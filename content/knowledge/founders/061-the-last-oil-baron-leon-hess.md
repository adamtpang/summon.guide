# The Last Oil Baron: Leon Hess

> **Founder principle (Leon Hess):** A large integrated company can begin with a very small distribution problem solved unusually well. Hess started by finding customers for residual fuel oil, then expanded into storage, refining, retailing, and production as the business required more capacity and control. His attention to physical operations and the people running them remained central as the scale changed.

## Key lessons

- **Question the product economics before merely working harder.** After working in his father's coal business, Hess saw the limits of carrying heavy loads for narrow margins. He shifted toward delivering fuel oil and found buyers for residual material that refiners treated as unwanted. Look for a customer use that changes the economics of an overlooked input, rather than assuming the inherited business model is the only available one.
- **Make the difficult delivery requirement your operating specialty.** Heavy fuel oil had to remain warm enough to move and reach customers reliably. Hess's wartime experience supplying fuel gave him practical knowledge of moving large volumes under pressure, which became useful as industrial demand expanded afterward. Build a capability around the constraint that makes the product awkward for less committed competitors.
- **Integrate to solve a dependency that growth has made expensive.** Hess moved from distribution into storage and refining, then combined with Amerada as his need for crude increased. One business had refining and marketing capacity while the other had oil production. Map the dependencies that limit your current operation, and assess expansion by how directly it addresses those dependencies.
- **Include the regulatory setting in the location decision.** The episode describes how the St. Croix refinery combined shipping treatment, tax terms, and other negotiated advantages that strengthened its economics. Hess also studied how transportation rules affected his vessels. Before committing to a major facility, investigate the actual rules governing each stage of operation and account for the possibility that those rules can change.
- **Simplify the service around the customer's main job.** Early Hess stations emphasized refueling instead of adding mechanics, replacement parts, and other services common at competitors. Clean premises and bathrooms made that focused offering more attractive to motorists. Decide which service matters most, then remove activities that slow it down without adding enough customer value.
- **Know the physical details behind the financial result.** Hess questioned managers about truck tires, checked refinery equipment during visits, and was known for remembering station margins. His concern with cleanliness extended from tankers to curbs and storage facilities. Use direct observation and a few revealing operating questions to understand whether reported performance rests on a well-maintained system.
- **Build relationships through attention and reciprocal loyalty.** Hess remained loyal to the bank that took his early business seriously, and he invested time in employees and counterparties as people. At refineries he ate with workers and greeted them individually. Repeated personal attention can strengthen the cooperation needed to obtain capital, solve difficult problems, and retain experienced staff.
- **Train successors through the work they will eventually oversee.** John Hess learned through gas-station work, refinery accounting, and field operations, while his education and language study prepared him for an international business. Family membership did not replace exposure to the company's practical functions. Sequence a successor's learning across the business so that later authority is supported by firsthand understanding.
- **Design succession around the core business you want preserved.** Leon required the Jets to be sold after his death and restricted family participation in the purchase. The episode presents avoiding distraction from the oil business as a possible explanation, rather than a confirmed motive. Whatever the rationale, the case shows why a succession plan must specify how peripheral assets will be treated instead of assuming heirs will infer the founder's priorities.
