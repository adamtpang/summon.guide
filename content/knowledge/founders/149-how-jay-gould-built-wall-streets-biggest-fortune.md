# How Jay Gould Built Wall Street's Biggest Fortune

> **Founder principle (Jay Gould):** Gould taught himself every skill directly in front of him, from surveying to bookkeeping to the leather trade, then kept moving toward whichever position in an industry actually captured the profit. He won early, undercapitalized fights through single-minded focus and patience against richer, more distracted rivals, and built a fortune out of railroads and telegraphs most people assumed he had no business touching. His most infamous move, cornering the gold market in 1869, is documented in the episode as a national financial injury, not a technique to admire.

*David Senra on Jay Gould, reading from American Rascal: How Jay Gould Built Wall Street's Biggest Fortune by Greg Steinmetz. Founders Podcast · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2_0cxxf7Hgo) · 59 min · published 2024-02-01.*

## Key lessons

- **Teach yourself the skill directly in front of you, then look for the version of it that pays more.** As a poor teenager, Gould taught himself surveying and basic bookkeeping simply to afford schooling and escape farm labor, turned surveying into his first real income by mapping and selling maps of his home county, then used a tannery job to teach himself the leather trade from the ground up.
- **Find out who actually captures the profit in your industry, then move toward that position.** While co-running a profitable tannery, Gould studied the New York leather traders and realized the merchants and brokers kept the largest and easiest share of the money while manufacturers carried the expense, the risk, and the labor, which is what pushed him to abandon manufacturing for Wall Street.
- **Win a mismatched fight through single-minded focus, not through more capital.** At twenty-one, with a fraction of the money and experience of his forty-five-year-old business partner, Gould focused on nothing but their tannery while his partner's attention was split across a bank, a farm, and other ventures, and it was Gould, not the wealthier man, who ended up dictating the buyout price.
- **Recognize a hard ceiling on a business and exit deliberately.** His first railroad investment taught him that too many competing lines chasing too little freight capped how valuable that line could ever become, so he sold his stake after eighteen months for many times what he paid rather than staying attached out of sentiment.
- **Turn a rival's own aggressive move into your opportunity.** When a competing railroad baron slashed freight rates to a token amount hoping to bankrupt the line Gould partly controlled, Gould's response was not to keep fighting the rate war but to ship his own cattle at those same bargain rates, profiting directly from a price war aimed at hurting him.
- **Treat patience itself as a competitive weapon.** Gould's own explanation for surviving repeated Wall Street conflicts was to wait and let the other side tire first, and the version of him with less money but a longer time horizon repeatedly outlasted richer, more established opponents in both business standoffs and drawn out legal fights.
- **Character habits compound the same way capital does.** Raised by an alcoholic father, Gould built part of his early advantage on staying reliably sober and disciplined while rival crews drank, and he kept a fixed daily schedule of work, family, and reading for the rest of his life rather than the social routine expected of a man of his wealth.
- **A notorious tactic is not proof of a technique worth copying.** Gould's 1869 attempt to corner the national gold market triggered a financial panic that nearly toppled a sitting president and is presented in the episode as a systemic injury to the country, not a savvy maneuver, and it made him the most distrusted man on Wall Street for the rest of his life.
