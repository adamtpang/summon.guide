# Robert Caro on Power, Poverty, Ruthlessness, Obsession & Running.

> **Founder principle (Robert Caro):** Treat the willingness to do the work no one else will do, reading the file everyone else skips, living where your subject actually lived, demanding a real number instead of a hopeful guess, as the entire edge. Talent explains far less than the refusal to take the comfortable shortcut, sustained over years past the point where quitting would have been reasonable.

*David Senra on Robert Caro, reading from Working by Robert A. Caro. Founders Podcast · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ulAWE6d4pVE) · 55 min · published 2024-03-23.*

## Key lessons

- **Read the file everyone else assumes is empty, because that is where the edge hides.** An editor's early instruction, "turn every page, never assume anything," became Caro's permanent method: he spent years reading folders in the Lyndon Johnson Library that no other researcher had likely ever opened, and that habit of over-mining kept turning up material nobody else had found.
- **Build in deliberate friction so you think before you write, not while you write.** A writing professor once told young Caro he would never achieve what he wanted so long as he kept "thinking with your fingers," meaning drafting fluently without first working out the idea. Caro's fix was to write every first draft in longhand, the slowest method available to him, on the theory that speed of output and depth of thought were in direct conflict.
- **When research alone will not get you inside your subject's head, go live in the actual place.** Caro realized he could not understand Lyndon Johnson's hardened character from documents and distant interviews, since he was a New Yorker writing about a rural Texas poverty he had never experienced, so he and his wife rented a house on the edge of the Texas Hill Country and lived there for most of three years.
- **Demand a real number, not a hopeful guess, from anyone reporting information back to you.** Johnson had watched his father's romantic optimism about a piece of land destroy the family financially, and he carried that lesson into politics: when a staffer reported back that a senator seemed likely to vote a certain way, Johnson would snap that thinking was worthless to him, saying only "I need to know," and he became known in Washington as the most accurate vote counter anyone had seen.
- **Find backers who see the long-term value of the work, and leave the ones who only see the downside.** Caro's first editor told him bluntly that not many people would read a book about a New York planning official and that he should expect a very small printing, but a new agent and a new editor who believed in the project immediately removed his money worries and committed to the book on its own terms; that book, The Power Broker, reached its 47th printing and remains a fixture on best nonfiction lists decades after its 1974 publication.
- **Get yourself around other people doing equally hard, equally long things, because isolation is what actually breaks resolve.** Caro spent his first five years on his first book without a single conversation with another writer attempting something similar, and the isolation itself, not the workload, was what nearly convinced him he had gone badly astray. Meeting two other biographers years into their own multi-year projects at the New York Public Library's Frederick Lewis Allen Room undid years of self-doubt in a single conversation.
- **When you need to move someone, find their specific point of leverage instead of relying on charm.** Robert Moses, the subject of Caro's first book, mapped out exactly where an opposing legislator was personally vulnerable, such as a mortgage payment coming due at a bank he had influence over, and applied pressure there until the vote flipped, rather than trying to win the person over in general.
- **Ask for the good memory and the bad memory separately, and let the silence answer what you did not ask directly.** When Caro asked Johnson's younger brother to repeat the warm childhood stories after already hearing him recreate the family's dinner-table shouting matches, the brother's long pause and admission that no warm stories existed told Caro more about Johnson's character than any direct question could have.
- **Notice your own compulsive patterns and work with them instead of fighting them.** Caro catches himself mid-research calling himself out for always needing one more detail when he already had enough to write, yet the same restless part of him kept pushing past that point anyway, a pattern he came to accept rather than resist. Johnson ran the same loop out loud in the other direction: his driver described him talking to himself between campaign stops, naming exactly which precinct he had just lost and rehearsing what he would say differently to those voters next time.
