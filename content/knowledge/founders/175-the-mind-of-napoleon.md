# The Mind of Napoleon

> **Founder principle (Napoleon Bonaparte):** Napoleon's own chief doctrine was that he had sacrificed comfort, self-interest and happiness to a destiny that had to be fulfilled, and that thought has value only as a step toward action. He learned by studying the campaigns of earlier commanders, planned by imagining every disaster, then committed without hesitation, and he believed the clever man neglects nothing that might add to his chances. He wrote his own bulletins and steered public opinion himself. Senra's takeaway is to do whatever you do constantly and to enlarge the scope of your ambition, while remembering that people this extreme will flatten anyone who expects them to play fair.

*David Senra on Napoleon Bonaparte, reading from The Mind of Napoleon, a collection of Napoleon's written and spoken words edited by J. Christopher Herold. Founders Podcast · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WWQFtt6Hm10) · 56 min.*

## Key lessons

- **Name your destiny first; everything else serves it.** The book is about 300 pages of Napoleon speaking directly, first published in 1955 and long out of print, and Senra thinks its core is one line Napoleon repeats: he sacrificed everything to his destiny, and destiny must be fulfilled. His ambition, he says, felt as natural as his blood or his breath. Senra joked to a friend that he was being lectured by Napoleon for being soft and forgettable.
- **Redefine luck as exploiting accidents.** Napoleon held that a run of great actions is never chance. Great men aren't great because they're lucky; being great, they learn to master luck, which he defines as the ability to exploit accidents. Because war is full of accidents, a commander must overlook nothing that could let him exploit them. Senra pairs this with Emerson's line that strong men believe in cause and effect.
- **Neglect nothing that might add to your odds.** One maxim Senra flags as among the best in the book is that the less clever man, by neglecting one thing, sometimes misses everything. He links it to Lyndon Johnson's belief that if you do everything, you will win, and to Napoleon's view that a single small circumstance can decide a battle.
- **Plan timidly, act boldly.** Napoleon calls himself the most fearful man alive while planning a campaign: he exaggerates every danger and possible disaster on purpose and is in real agitation. Once decided, he forgets everything except what leads to success, and an offensive once begun must be sustained to the last extreme. The editor notes that boldness is the common quality Napoleon picks out in all seven great generals he cites. Senra compares the planning half to Paul Graham's advice to be hard to kill.
- **Study the great captains like game tape.** Napoleon says the principles of war can be learned only from military history, the battles of the great captains, and experience, and lists the campaigns of Alexander, Hannibal, Caesar, Frederick the Great and others as a complete course. He takes traits from some and rejects others: Alexander planned deeply, executed audaciously and managed wisely, while Saint Louis spent months praying when he should have been marching. Napoleon also noted that most nations and individuals learn only from their own experience, usually misfortune.
- **Assume people move on fear and self-interest.** Napoleon says men are moved by two levers, fear and self-interest, and that he starts by believing the worst of people. Senra connects this to the advertising founders, Lasker, Hopkins and Ogilvy: stop talking about your product and talk about what it does for the customer, since self-interest is what they act on.
- **Expect the extreme competitor to ignore your rules.** When the surrendered Austrian general Mack complained that Napoleon had marched through neutral Switzerland and Prussia, gaining days that let him crush the Austrians, Napoleon said he had never recognized that neutrality, then asked why Mack hadn't done the same. Senra borrows a point a friend made on a podcast about Robert Caro's subjects: if you won't pay the full price for a goal, assume someone else will sacrifice everything, ethics included, and win. He studies people like this partly to avoid becoming roadkill on their path.
- **Win the team's mind; winning itself fixes morale.** A leader's main talent, in Napoleon's words, is knowing his soldiers' mentality and winning their confidence. Arriving in 1796 to a demoralized army, he reorganized supplies and let victory do the rest. His speech to unpaid, ragged troops promised fertile plains, rich cities and glory. He judged people only by the results of their actions, and of Washington he concluded that "it is success which makes the great man."
- **Be your own minister of propaganda.** Napoleon dictated every important army bulletin himself, to inform the public, counter rumors, mislead the enemy and build enthusiasm, and believed opinion could be steered with ink and paper if the effects were told simply and in detail. Senra ties this to Steve Jobs personally approving every external marketing piece, and argues that if your product really helps people, you are obliged to get good at telling them about it.
- **Read the contradictions as part of the lesson.** Napoleon said the art of ruling is making others work rather than wearing yourself out, then dictated 21 large sheets of instructions for Josephine's trip to a water cure. In 1806 he said he wanted no censorship of books; in 1810 he imposed it. Senra presents him as one of the most extreme people who ever lived, unmoved by battlefields of dead men, moved to tears by an injured dog, and treats the book as a study of human nature as it is, not a character reference.
