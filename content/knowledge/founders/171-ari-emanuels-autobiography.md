# Ari Emanuel's Autobiography

> **Founder principle (Ari Emanuel):** A dyslexic kid told he was stupid turned that rage into the engine of a career: he started as an assistant who did not know what an agent did, made more calls than anyone, and built Endeavor from four people with borrowed chairs into a company valued in the billions. The method is volume, refusal to take no for an answer, and a willingness to embrace change and get big before consolidation kills you. The memoir is equally honest that every summit faded within days, and that the rage which built the business eventually had to be let go.

*David Senra on Ari Emanuel, reading from Roll the Calls, Emanuel's own memoir. Founders Podcast · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6mhN6WkZnXc) · published 2026-10-05.*

## Key lessons

- **Turn the wound into fuel, but name it.** Emanuel was dyslexic, and being treated as stupid as a child left him full of self-loathing, anxiety, and rage. His diagnosis did not fix those feelings, it only explained them. Senra notes how many founders covered on the show were dyslexic, and the book treats that sense of not fitting in as the source of a lifelong drive.
- **Listen in on the best and learn the whole game as an assistant.** At 24 he answered a listing to assist a New York literary agent, with no fixed hours and no idea what agents did. Listening in on his boss's calls was how he learned. When a rival he called a pirate stole a star client and then made his boss's voice quiver on the phone, Emanuel's private thought was that he should go work for the pirate. The pirate was Michael Ovitz.
- **Do not accept the answer.** CAA's mailroom gatekeeper rejected him as too old at 26. He called five times a day, left late night messages, and after roughly ten more days of ten or eleven calls a day got the job, on one condition: be in Los Angeles within a week. His theory was that anyone who finds eleven messages from you every morning will either call back or call the police. Senra confirms from Jimmy Iovine that he still works this way.
- **Volunteer for the expert, then hand him proof you were paying attention.** In the CAA TV department he asked co-founder Bill Haber if he could help on nights and weekends just to learn. Then he wrote and handed over a white paper on the emerging international TV market, and Haber promoted him to agent. His working rule was never to say he could not do something: say yes, then figure it out.
- **Roll the calls: volume is the moat.** The title comes from his habit of having the next call waiting the instant he hangs up, at a pace of hundreds a day. Startup scarcity made it sharper. Endeavor's cell plans had strict minute allowances, so he began hanging up mid sentence with no goodbye, which became his trademark. Senra connects it to Les Schwab's view that success is a case of volume.
- **Life is not a dress rehearsal, so jump.** Unhappy in ICM's me-first culture, Emanuel was hit by a pickup truck in 1994. Five days later he was back in the office, and soon after he turned down a long term deal worth a million dollars a year. Endeavor launched on his 34th birthday, March 29, 1995. Partners mortgaged houses, maxed out credit cards, and borrowed $100,000 from a friendly producer; there was no travel budget, and he slept on friends' floors in New York.
- **Never fight the future.** He points to how the arrival of sound wiped out silent film and calls hiding from that kind of change stupid. Vince McMahon told him over dinner how he saw cable coming, ignored the regional wrestling territories, went national, and absorbed rivals. Emanuel signed McMahon as a client, and years later his company bought the wrestling business.
- **When the industry consolidates, get big fast, and know that mergers are purchases.** With around 200 agencies in town, he concluded only the biggest one or two would survive. David Geffen's advice on which agency to merge with was that it did not matter: in traffic, the one thing you cannot do is stand still. A Harvard professor told him no merger is a true merger, only one company swallowing another. After years of failed talks with William Morris, whose leaders had once laughed at him, Endeavor took control, and he called the roughly $44 million spent buying out the old leadership a bargain for the oldest agency in town.
- **Build a private business school by phone.** He reads about people, cold calls them, and turns calls into meals and friendships, which he calls his MBA on his own terms. That is how he met Marc Andreessen and then Silver Lake's Egon Durban. Durban's line on overpaying for IMG became his rule for the UFC bid: you will never remember the extra $400 million, but you will remember losing the deal. Endeavor's take-private later valued it at $13 billion, up from $350 million a dozen years before.
- **Get the yes with a favor delivered in minutes.** His first pitch to Dana White failed because he compared the UFC to wrestling. A month later White said he would sign if Emanuel got him a meeting with HBO that same day. With Entourage and other hit shows on the network, Emanuel called back three minutes later with the meeting booked. HBO said no, but White signed with him, and years later Endeavor bought the UFC.
- **Fame and success are force multipliers, but the highs never last.** Entourage's Ari Gold made people take his calls ten times faster. Yet after every big win, the merger, the IMG deal, the IPO, he felt empty within days. Two collapses from overwork taught him what a billionaire friend told him: when you are on the field, be on the field, but there has to be a halftime. A meditation teacher's advice was that rage had taken him as far as it could. Even so, after a victory round at Augusta he skipped lunch to fly back for a signing meeting.
