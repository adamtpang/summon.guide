# How Tamara Mellon Built Jimmy Choo

> **Founder principle (Tamara Mellon):** Retain majority ownership and control above almost everything else, because giving up even one percentage point hands the final say to someone else. Design for the customer you already are, place your store exactly where she already shops, and turn media and celebrity exposure into compounding free marketing. Once outside owners arrive, defend the product's quality against anyone optimizing only for the next resale.

*David Senra on Tamara Mellon, reading from her memoir In My Shoes. Founders Podcast · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AOh9nnXRic4) · 56 min · published 2025-06-08.*

## Key lessons

- **Never trade away control for convenience.** Desperate to remove a difficult minority partner, Mellon let a private equity firm take 51 percent instead of 50, and she calls it the single decision she most regrets, since it handed a stranger the final word on a company she had built from nothing. Her standing advice to future founders is to retain majority ownership no matter how tempting the shortcut looks.
- **Design for the customer you already are.** Mellon built Jimmy Choo's earliest shoes for a woman exactly like herself, since she knew where that woman shopped, what she read, and what she watched, and that first-hand knowledge shaped decisions from product design to store location far faster than market research could have.
- **Put your store exactly where your customer already stands.** She chose her first shop on a busy London street rather than the cheaper, harder-to-reach location her main competitor used, reasoning that a working woman would walk twenty feet out of curiosity but wouldn't detour twenty minutes. The same logic Steve Jobs later used to justify expensive Apple Store locations.
- **Turn stylists, not just celebrities, into a distribution channel.** Instead of only gifting shoes directly to famous women, the company built relationships with the stylists who dressed many clients at once, so a single relationship multiplied the odds of a visible placement, and a single unpaid mention on Sex and the City did more for the brand than any paid campaign could have.
- **Prove your commitment in person before a skeptical partner will sign.** Jimmy Choo wanted to see her commitment in action rather than hear a pitch, so Mellon showed up at his workshop every day for three months before he agreed to the partnership. Sustained, visible effort closed the deal that words alone couldn't.
- **Stay frugal and self-funded from day one.** The company started on roughly $150,000 from her father, ran out of a windowless basement office with two desks, and was profitable in its first year without ever needing outside capital until she chose to bring in a partner years later.
- **Defend quality against owners optimizing for the next sale.** Once private equity partners took over, they capped the cost of leather per square meter and refused to fund store refurbishments or a signature industry sponsorship, prioritizing near-term margins that made the company easier to resell rather than the brand equity that made it valuable in the first place.
- **Track how ownership changes shape what you're allowed to build.** Over roughly fifteen years, Jimmy Choo passed through several private equity owners before an IPO and an eventual $1.2 billion sale, and at each handoff her own influence over product and marketing decisions shrank further, a direct consequence of the ownership stake she gave away early.
- **Trust your own instinct over the noise of conflicting advice.** Her closing advice to entrepreneurs was to follow your own instincts and change course only when they prove wrong through real evidence, since being pulled back and forth by other people's opinions produces no direction at all.
