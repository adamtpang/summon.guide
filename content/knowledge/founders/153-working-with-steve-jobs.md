# Working With Steve Jobs

> **Founder principle (Steve Jobs):** Jobs ran Apple's product development through a steady stream of concrete demos rather than meetings about hypotheticals, and he reviewed them himself so the company's taste stayed decisive instead of dissolving into data and committee. He judged a browser, a keyboard, or a whole company by a single measurable priority, treated every visible and invisible detail as part of the product, and understood that one bad customer experience could undo a hundred good ones.

*David Senra on Steve Jobs, reading from Creative Selection: Inside Apple's Design Process During the Golden Age of Steve Jobs by Ken Kocienda, with a passage read from Becoming Steve Jobs. Founders Podcast · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bVQnCjfmf-U) · 41 min · published 2024-01-16.*

## Key lessons

- **Push ideas forward through concrete demos, never through meetings about hypotheticals.** Apple's engineers built a working demo for nearly every feature because an unsophisticated idea is hard to discuss constructively without something physical to react to, and each demo's reactions, positive or negative, became the direct input for the next demo, on the belief that the sooner real decisions started, the more time remained to refine them.
- **Make the verdict fast and unambiguous, then let it stand.** Reviewing two iPad keyboard designs, Jobs studied each option in silence, unhurried, asked the engineer directly which one he preferred, and closed the whole review in four brief sentences ending with a simple "we'll go with the bigger keys." There was no committee vote and no follow-up meeting, just a decisive taste applied straight to the work.
- **Earn your next invitation to the room by contributing, not by attending.** Engineers only kept getting demo slots with Jobs if they made substantive contributions that pushed the product forward, and showing up with nothing valuable to add was the one guaranteed way to never be invited back.
- **Give the product a single, measurable priority instead of a list of them.** When Apple built its own browser to replace Internet Explorer, Jobs set exactly one standard to judge it by: speed. That single-minded definition of greatness, not a broader feature list, became the team's entire mandate.
- **Put yourself in the customer's shoes, then hack away everything unessential.** Jobs pictured a specific busy, distracted customer, a mother running late and answering email in the hallway, and asked whether a feature actually served her or just added to what she had to learn, treating the removal of non-essential features as a form of respect for the people who would use the product.
- **Treat every interaction as a deposit or withdrawal from the company's reputation.** Buying a song, calling support, or unboxing a Mac all either added to or subtracted from what Jobs called the Apple experience, and one bad experience could undo dozens of good ones, so design had to protect against negative moments, not just create positive ones.
- **Choose a trusted person's taste over data when the decision is about quality, not facts.** Apple never A/B tested the colors or software choices Jobs approved in minutes, in contrast with a rival's design team that once tested forty one shades of blue against each other and still could not decide, since removing subjectivity from every decision just turns data into a crutch.
- **Design is how something works, not how it looks.** Jobs insisted that appearance is only a veneer and that real design has to explain how a product functions, so form should follow function even for something as intangible as pixels on a screen.
- **Build the organization to prevent the anti-patterns that quietly kill good work.** Apple avoided discussing projects without concrete examples, avoided long-lived paper mockups, disbanded an advanced research group that was disconnected from real shipping products, and had no room for a detached, seagull-style executive who swoops in, disrupts, and leaves the mess for others to clean up.
