# The Biography of Tiger Woods

> **Founder principle (Tiger Woods):** Woods became the most dominant golfer of his era through a practice regimen that started before he could properly talk and never let up: thousands of hours rehearsed alone, mostly hidden from public view, with every shot mentally played out the night before it mattered. He measured himself against Jack Nicklaus's age by age record rather than against whoever he was currently beating, and he was willing to tear down a winning swing and rebuild it from nothing rather than coast on what already worked. The same intensity that built him also overrode its own warnings on rest, privacy, and pain, and that is where the discipline eventually turned on him.

*David Senra on Tiger Woods, reading from Tiger Woods by Jeff Benedict and Armen Keteyian, the biography behind the HBO documentary Tiger. Founders Podcast · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MkuNdIeKwT4) · 64 min · published 2024-03-18.*

## Key lessons

- **Put the extreme practice in private and let the public only see the result.** By 18 months old Woods was already being driven to the range to hit balls before naps, and as an adult he still averaged more hours on the practice range than on the course, living out the idea that people praise what they see in public without ever seeing what was practiced in private.
- **Measure your progress against history's record, not against whoever you are currently beating.** After watching a 46 year old Jack Nicklaus win the 1986 Masters, 10 year old Woods taped Nicklaus's list of career achievements, organized by the age each one happened, to his bedroom wall, and spent his career trying to beat Nicklaus's age at every milestone rather than chasing a raw number of wins.
- **Rehearse the outcome before you perform it.** The night before a final round he would sit alone in a room and mentally play all 18 holes shot by shot before falling asleep, the same deliberate visualization practice the episode traces through Estee Lauder and a recurring list of other high performers.
- **Notice the detail your competitors will not even think to check.** Handed six supposedly identical titanium driver prototypes, Woods insisted one felt heavier than the rest; when Nike pulled the club apart, one head genuinely was about two grams heavier from a stray dab of adhesive left by an engineer.
- **Be willing to dismantle a method that is currently winning.** Twice while ranked the best player alive, Woods scrapped his own swing and rebuilt it from scratch, accepting a real slump, at one stretch nine months without a win, in exchange for a swing he believed would hold up longer.
- **Play to your own scorekeeping, not for money, fame, or anyone watching.** He insisted on personally carrying his own trophy off the course rather than letting an official take it, because winning and collecting hardware, not endorsements or applause, was the actual measure he was chasing.
- **Treat rest as part of the training, not a break from it.** The episode contrasts Woods's refusal to slow down after surgery with Bill Bowerman's stress, recovery, improvement model for track athletes; ignoring that cycle is tied directly to Woods's repeated surgeries, painkiller use, and the clouded decisions that followed.
- **Guard your privacy on purpose once fame turns everyone around you into a potential informant.** Tabloids paid ordinary people, waitresses, bartenders, even a mother informing on her own daughter, for information on Woods, and his response was to name his two boats privacy and solitude and to take up cave diving partly because it was one of the only places no one recognized him.
- **After a public collapse, rebuild your motivation on something that cannot be taken from you again.** Woods's own stated conclusion once he returned to golf was that he would play for himself from then on, not for his parents, his sponsors, his caddy, or his fans, replacing an audience-dependent motivation with one that could survive scandal.
