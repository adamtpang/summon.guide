# Les Schwab (Charlie Munger recommended this book)

> **Founder principle (Les Schwab):** Turn every store into its own small business with its own sharp incentives, cap the downside on every contract before signing it, and push decision making down to whoever is closest to the customer. Pay more than the market for people instead of less, on the theory that better pay buys better employees and better employees buy loyal customers.

*David Senra on Les Schwab, reading from Pride in Performance, Keep It Going and Charlie Munger's analysis of it in the new Stripe Press edition of Poor Charlie's Almanack. Founders Podcast · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SCV64-7iMWA) · 94 min · published 2024-04-17.*

## Key lessons

- **Turn store managers into partners, not employees, from day one.** Once he could no longer stand behind the counter of more than one shop himself, Schwab began sharing fifty percent of each new store's profit with the person running it, reasoning that heavy incentive was the only way to guarantee excellence when he was not there to supervise.
- **Structure the company as many small businesses, not one big one.** Every store operated as its own separate entity, and employees shared only in the profits of the specific store they worked in, so reward stayed tied directly to local performance instead of the health of the whole chain.
- **Get every contract's downside capped before you sign it.** He refused to sign a lease without a five year option to buy, and built an escape clause into every deal letting him hand a property back without penalty if that particular store failed, protecting the rest of the company from any one bad location.
- **Pay above market wages on purpose, even under margin pressure.** Watching a fast expanding rival collapse after competing on low wages convinced him that underpaying store staff was a false economy, since better pay bought better employees and better employees kept customers, so he refused to cut wages even when tempted to match a cheaper competitor.
- **Do a free favor that creates a paying customer later.** His free flat repair offer for lady drivers cost little to deliver at scale but reliably converted a one time kindness into a repeat tire customer, betting on the simple fact that people feel obligated to repay a favor.
- **Invert the standard layout of your industry.** Where every other tire shop hid its inventory in a back warehouse and kept a small showroom, Schwab made the showroom the warehouse, displaying far more tires than any competitor and having them cleaned daily, purely because of how it changed what a customer felt walking in the door.
- **Push decision making down, not up.** He considered it a serious mistake for authority to drift toward head office as a company grows, and instead insisted that store managers keep the power to make their own daily calls, on the theory that the person closest to the problem should be the one solving it.
- **Promote only from inside, without exception.** In more than three decades, not one manager or assistant manager was hired from outside the company, every one of them started at the bottom changing tires, which meant each manager had personally done, and understood, every job in the store they ran.
- **Turn a supplier's mistreatment into your structural advantage.** When the major American rubber makers colluded on price and favored their own company owned stores, Schwab treated the unfairness as an opening, finding an independent Japanese tire supplier and later refusing any single brand affiliation at all, buying tires the way a grocery store buys groceries, whichever brand served the customer best.
