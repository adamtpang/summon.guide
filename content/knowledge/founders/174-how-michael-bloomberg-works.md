# How Michael Bloomberg Works

> **Founder principle (Michael Bloomberg):** Getting fired at 39 with a $10 million payout was the start, not the end, because fifteen years at Salomon Brothers had already taught him to sell, to show up before everyone else, and to never let planning get in the way of doing. He built Bloomberg on the one thing he knew better than anyone, how technology could help investors, sold it to Merrill Lynch before it existed, kept doing every job himself in the early years, and treated news, radio and television as nothing more than new ways to sell the same terminal subscription. Fights should never be fair, bets should be small enough to abandon, and the company should answer to no outside owner.

## Key lessons

- **Treat a firing as seed capital, and never look back.** After fifteen years of twelve-hour days, Bloomberg was pushed out of Salomon Brothers when it was sold, one of a handful of partners not kept on. He admits he would have stayed forever if asked, but once it was over he never visited or second-guessed it. He put $300,000 of the payout into a corporate checking account in a one-room office facing an alley, and he says that fifteen years later it was a billion-dollar business.
- **Learn to sell in the job everyone else considers beneath them.** In 1966 trading and sales were seen as second-class work because they meant picking up the phone and asking for business. Goldman offered him $14,000 and Salomon $9,000; he wanted Salomon, so he asked for what he needed to live, and got the $9,000 salary plus a $2,500 loan. He started in the cage counting securities by hand in a hot vault, and told friends he was studying workflow procedures rather than admit he was a clerk. Senra's read is that those years taught him the meta-skill of selling, which made him the company's best salesperson later.
- **Make yourself indispensable to whoever runs the place.** Bloomberg arrived at 7 a.m., before everyone but managing partner Billy Salomon, and stayed later than anyone else, so he was the person available for a match, a sports chat, an after-hours client call, or a complaint about people who had gone home. By 26 he was the boss's buddy. He says he never understood why everyone doesn't do the same.
- **Prefer a decisive leader to a consensus seeker.** He contrasts Billy Salomon, who decided once and was followed, with his successor John Gutfreund, who consulted every interested party. Bloomberg's view is that consulting everyone looks egalitarian but produces indecision, because smart people start preparing for both directions and split resources.
- **String together small earned steps instead of writing a five-year plan.** His rule is to keep improving your skills, work as many hours as possible, plan only the next few moves, and adjust to what actually happens. He mocks long-range schemes directly, "central planning didn't work for Stalin or Mao," and says every real advance at his companies was evolutionary. Asked what Bloomberg had failed at, he said nothing, though what worked often wasn't what they set out to do.
- **Start from what you know better than anyone, then sell before the product exists.** He wasn't the best trader, manager or programmer, but he believed nobody knew more about both the securities business and how technology could help it. Consulting work brought in early cash and credibility, and led to a sale to Merrill Lynch with a six-month delivery date for a machine that didn't exist. The deadline fell on a Saturday, so they delivered on Monday, in June 1983, something that worked, sort of. Merrill also took 30% of the company for about $30 million, a stake Bloomberg later bought back for billions.
- **Treat a demanding first customer as part of the product team.** Merrill assigned two traders to the system, and Bloomberg expected them to be pains. Instead they showed exactly when and why something failed, and the product improved every day. His stated preference is a smart, fair, demanding client over a nasty one or an indifferent one.
- **Do every job yourself in the early years.** For the first three or four years he sold, negotiated every supplier and customer contract, signed every check, hired and fired, bought the snacks and emptied the wastebaskets. The founding team pulled cables under client desks on weekends, drilling holes in other people's furniture without permission. He calls handing those jobs to specialists painful for the parent, and those years the most fun he had in business.
- **Know what business you are in, and make every new medium sell the core product.** Bloomberg built computers and keyboards only because personal computers didn't yet do the job, never confusing hardware with the business, which was information. When reporter Matt Winkler joined, Bloomberg's brief made each news story a demonstration of terminal functions, so more stories meant more subscriptions and more reporters. He resisted radio and television until a persistent caller pitched him three times, then hired that caller and two colleagues on the spot, because broadcasting was simply another way to reach people who might lease a terminal.
- **Let proof and personality do the persuading.** Pitching Winkler, he handed over a printout of every customer by name, firm and phone number and invited him to call them. When customers started asking for "the Bloomberg" instead of the product's original Market Master name, he accepted the market's verdict, renamed the company after himself, and became, in his words, the Colonel Sanders of financial information.
- **Never enter a fair fight, and keep new bets small.** He says business battles should never be even, so Bloomberg goes into contests only with an advantage, and asks what it can do that competitors can't rather than copying them. Expansion starts with a little capital the company can afford to lose and people who can be reassigned, so nobody feels committed to a mistake. New ventures get no appointed manager at first; the leader is whoever colleagues go to for help.
- **Fight the creep of "why we can't," and stay private.** Late in the book he describes wanting to scream at legal sign-offs, keep-in-the-loop meetings and policies buried under exceptions, and calls keeping that out his top management focus. He rejects an IPO because it means answering to more owners, saying "answering to no one is the ultimate situation."
