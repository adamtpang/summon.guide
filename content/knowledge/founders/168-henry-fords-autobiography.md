# Henry Ford's Autobiography

> **Founder principle (Henry Ford):** Treat the business as an instrument of service, not a machine for extracting profit, since "money comes naturally as a result of service." Chase one idea, a simple, durable product priced within reach of the ordinary buyer, for as long as it takes to perfect it, and refuse every request to complicate it or add a second one. Hold enough ownership to protect that one idea from partners who will always prefer more models and higher margins over price cuts and volume, and run the whole company on maximum service at minimum cost.

*David Senra on Henry Ford, reading from My Life and Work, Ford's own 1922 autobiography. Founders Podcast · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KzXxxbuTAAg) · 74 min · published 2023-10-06.*

## Key lessons

- **Treat profit as the byproduct of service, never the target.** Ford used the word service so often across the book that it reads less like an autobiography and more like a manifesto: judge pricing, wages, and product design by whether they serve the customer first, and trust that revenue follows a satisfied customer rather than the other way around.
- **Find one product idea and refuse to trade it for a second one.** Ford built and discarded eight prior models over roughly twelve years before he trusted a Model T for mass production, then sold only that one car for close to two decades; his own rule was that one idea is about as much as anyone can actually execute at a time.
- **Compound simplicity by removing the useless part, not by adding an impressive one.** He treated a car built with thirty pounds of unnecessary water locked in its wood as typical of every product on the market, and had the factory hunt down and strip that kind of waste from every single part, since real simplicity is whatever gives the best service at the most convenient use.
- **Chase volume with price cuts, not margin with price increases.** When Ford's own cars sold for 2,000 and 1,000 dollars, the company moved around 1,500 of them a year; cutting the same models to 750 and 600 dollars lifted sales to roughly 8,500, and Ford treated that trade, lower profit per car for far higher unit volume, as the entire financial strategy rather than a discount.
- **Take direct ownership control before your partners can outvote your one idea.** Ford bought enough additional stock in 1906 to reach 51 percent ownership specifically because his partners kept pushing him toward building three separate models instead of one; without that controlling stake, the decision he considered his whole competitive advantage would have been voted away.
- **Turn a self-declared expert into a hiring red flag.** Ford treated the word expert as a warning sign that someone had stopped looking for the next improvement, and said he would deliberately staff a rival's company with experts if he wanted to sabotage it without anyone noticing, since real mastery shows up as always seeing more left to fix, never as confidence that the work is finished.
- **Bet everything on your own conviction when the entire expert consensus disagrees.** When steam and electric power were the fashionable bets and the internal combustion engine was dismissed as a toy with no future, Ford's own employer offered him a promotion on condition that he drop his gas engine experiments; he quit instead, with no cash cushion, certain the car was already bound to succeed. One of his own executives later summed up the trait in three words: "intransigence is my only weapon."
- **When a supplier's failure blocks your own operation, buy it and run it your way.** Ford purchased a struggling railroad only because its poor service was interfering with construction at his River Rouge plant, then applied the same waste elimination he used on cars: consolidating a bloated administrative layer and cutting the payroll from about 2,700 to 1,650 people, which turned the line from losing money into a profitable one under his ownership.
- **Study the operating genius without laundering the character.** The episode is direct that this autobiography leaves out real, serious problems with Ford's personal conduct, and that other biographies cover them in more detail; take the service-and-simplicity playbook on its own terms, and do not let a great operating record double as a character reference.
