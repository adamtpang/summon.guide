# Charlie Munger (the NEW Poor Charlie's Almanack)

> **Founder principle (Charlie Munger):** Treat the acquisition and transmission of wisdom as a moral duty. Study exactly how misery and failure happen so you can invert toward their opposite, engineer incentives and information flow so bad news travels fast, and give your scarcest resources, attention, capital, and practice time, to the people and ideas that deserve them most.

*David Senra on Charlie Munger, reading from the new Stripe Press edition of Poor Charlie's Almanack and the companion John Collison interview with Munger. Founders Podcast · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=e2YgnLewpN4) · 113 min · published 2024-04-16.*

## Key lessons

- **Study how to guarantee misery, then do the opposite.** In a commencement address, Munger opened not with advice for happiness but with a precise recipe for a miserable life: mood altering substances, envy, resentment, and unreliability, on the theory that naming a failure mode in sharp detail changes behavior more reliably than a positive exhortation does.
- **Give resentment a container instead of pretending it is not there.** Rather than deny a grudge outright, he borrowed what he called the Disraeli compromise, writing the name of anyone who wronged him on a slip of paper and dropping it in a drawer, letting time take them down without his own assistance.
- **Choose relationships that prevent problems, not ones that pay you to fix them.** His father did far more legal work for a difficult, overbearing client than for his genuinely admirable friend, because the admirable man treated employees, customers, and problems right and simply generated less legal business, a pattern Munger summarized as wisdom is prevention.
- **Never give an instruction without attaching the reason.** Engineer Carl Braun's company fired anyone who sent a directive without stating who, what, where, when, and why, twice, because people comply more fully with a reason attached, even one they do not fully understand, than with a bare order.
- **Treat bad news as the news that most needs to travel fast.** Berkshire's standing rule was to tell headquarters the bad news immediately, since only the good news could wait, a direct countermeasure to what he called the Persian messenger syndrome, in which leaders who punish the bearer of bad news end up deciding inside a cocoon of unreality.
- **Redesign the incentive before you try to redesign the behavior.** Federal Express could not get its night sorting crew to finish on time no matter what it tried, until switching from hourly pay to a fixed per shift wage, go home once the planes are loaded, fixed the problem immediately, because the employees were never the issue, the pay structure was.
- **Borrow the fundamental concept from outside your own field.** In a business school case about two inexperienced women who inherited a struggling shoe factory, the only student to earn top marks solved it using undergraduate psychology and economics rather than anything taught that term in business class, recommending they sell rather than try to manage what they could not.
- **Give your scarcest resource, attention and practice time, to whoever will do the most with it.** UCLA basketball coach John Wooden stopped splitting practice reps evenly across his twelve man roster and let his bottom five serve mainly as practice partners so his top seven got the bulk of the game time, and the team won more, not fewer, games as a result.
- **A business can sit on a dormant advantage for decades and still cash it in.** Disney's old film library was worth little until the videocassette let the company take decades old movies out of the can and resell them at almost no additional cost, the lesson being to stay in the game long enough for an outside invention to unlock value you already own.
