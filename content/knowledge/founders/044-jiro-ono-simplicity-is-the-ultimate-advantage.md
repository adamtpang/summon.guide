# Jiro Ono: Simplicity Is The Ultimate Advantage

> **Founder principle (Jiro Ono):** Pick a single craft, strip it down to the fewest details that matter, and perfect every one of them through decades of daily repetition rather than novelty. Constantly taste and judge your own work as its harshest critic, and treat any sense of having arrived, at any age, as the actual danger to the craft.

*David Senra on Jiro Ono, reading from the documentary Jiro Dreams of Sushi. Founders Podcast · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=x2Rj0sgjDSw) · 38 min · published 2025-08-04.*

## Key lessons

- **Narrow to one craft and never leave it.** Jiro held the same job for 75 years, running the same routine every single day, treating disciplined repetition itself, not variety or novelty, as the actual path to mastery.
- **Constantly taste and judge your own product before anyone else does.** He tastes every batch before it's served and calls himself the restaurant's first customer, a habit that sounds obvious but almost no one actually practices with real rigor.
- **Assign one specialist per ingredient and demand they be the best at that one thing.** His tuna dealer sells only tuna, his rice dealer sells only rice, and each refuses to sell anything but their own top pick, even to famous customers.
- **Refuse to relax standards no matter how long you've done the work.** At 85, still adjusting his technique daily, he says "even at my age, I'm discovering new techniques," treating any feeling of having mastered it completely as the moment you start fooling yourself.
- **Calibrate every detail to the specific person in front of you.** He watches which hand a guest favors and adjusts where the next piece is placed, because small details fitted to the individual customer compound into an experience competitors can't replicate at scale.
- **Make apprentices earn each stage of the craft before they touch what matters.** New hires start by learning to properly wring a hot towel, and one apprentice made over 200 rejected attempts at egg sushi before Jiro accepted a single one.
- **Treat a hard-won skill as the only real safety net, not money.** Jiro's insistence that a skill "no one else can take from you" is what keeps you from ever starving again traces directly back to being abandoned and left to fend for himself at age nine.
- **Keep working long after most people would call it enough.** Even at 85 he says "I'd be bored out of my mind" if he stopped, since idle hands, not old age, are what actually end a craftsman's usefulness.
