# Phil Knight: Founder of Nike

> **Founder principle (Phil Knight):** Find a crazy idea that seems worthy and fun, then chase it with an athlete's single-minded focus and refuse to stop. Sell belief in the activity rather than the shoe, choose partners and employees who are more obsessed with the product than you are, and plow every dollar back into growth even when bankers throw you out. Redefine winning from merely not losing to creating and contributing, and be honest that the price is paid in years away from the people you love.

*David Senra on Phil Knight, reading from Shoe Dog by Phil Knight. Founders Podcast · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wu5SsVzcfx0) · 63 min · published 2026-05-07.*

## Key lessons

- **Take the crazy idea seriously when nobody else does.** His Stanford paper argued that Japanese running shoes could take the American market the way Japanese cameras had taken it from the Germans; classmates met it with bored silence and not one question. His father, who worshipped respectability, called the venture "jackassing around" for years. Knight's advice to himself on a morning run at 24 was to let everyone call it crazy and simply not stop, and half a century later he still calls that the only advice worth giving.
- **Belief sells; product pitches do not.** He had failed at selling encyclopedias and felt dead inside selling mutual funds, yet selling shoes from his car trunk at track meets he could not write orders fast enough. The difference was that he believed running made the world better and these shoes were better to run in, and people wanted a piece of that belief. His mother made the same point by loudly buying a pair for $7 in front of his father.
- **Choose the partner who is more obsessed with the product than you are.** Bill Bowerman stole his runners' shoes from their lockers to tear them apart and rebuild them lighter, calculating that an ounce off a shoe saved 55 pounds over a mile. He called himself a "professor of competitive responses," asked for a 50-50 partnership over lunch, and gave his design improvements back to the supplier. His book on jogging sold millions and expanded the market by celebrating the activity, which Knight had assumed nobody would read.
- **Hire the true believer, tell him what to do, and let him surprise you.** Jeff Johnson wrote letters daily, built an index-card database of every customer's size and preferences, sent birthday and race-congratulation cards, and turned the first store into a sanctuary with comfortable chairs and a library for runners. Knight's management rule was to say what, not how; he set an absurd sales target as the condition for the store and Johnson hit it. Knight never sent the encouragement Johnson begged for, and Johnson stayed anyway.
- **Grow or die, but know you are playing chicken with your bankers.** He left no cash in the account, doubled each order, was thrown out of bank after bank, and signed over his house. An employee's parents lent their entire $8,000 life savings, later worth $1.6 million at the IPO, the box supplier fronted cash because his business depended on Nike, and only a Japanese trading company that judged there are worse things than ambition kept the company alive. He is explicit that this was taking it to the absolute limit, and sometimes past it.
- **Turn a betrayal into independence day.** When his Japanese supplier secretly courted other American distributors, he worked the problem in his recliner as a chain of questions: what do I know, what does the future hold, what is the next step. He quietly developed his own line, took the name Nike from a Johnson dream, and when the supplier cut him off told a slumping team that the crisis was their liberation, since $2 million of sales already had nothing to do with the old brand.
- **Fix cash flow with structure, not just more borrowing.** Late shipments cut the daily pair count, which meant missed payments, which meant no more credit, which meant late orders, round and round. His answer was to ask the biggest retailers for large nonrefundable orders six months ahead in exchange for discounts up to 7 percent, producing longer lead times, fewer shipments, steadier cash, and commitments he could use to extract more credit. When an employee protested he was in over his head running a factory, Knight laughed that everyone was.
- **Make the work feel like play, then redefine what winning means.** He kept a full-time accounting job for years, spent nights and weekends on Blue Ribbon, and could not pay himself a salary until near his 31st birthday despite doubling sales annually. Winning at first meant only not losing, because losing felt like death. Later he expanded it to creating and contributing, arguing that business is no more about money than being human is about making blood.
- **Count the cost honestly and seek a calling.** He promised repeatedly to spend more time with his sons and relapsed each time; one refused to wear Nikes in protest. The night of the IPO, worth $178 million, he felt not joy but regret, and he wished he could do it all over again. His closing advice: do not settle for a job or career, seek a calling; expect a bull's-eye on your back that grows with your success; and know that giving up on one thing is sometimes genius, as long as you never stop.
