# The Biography of Napoleon

> **Founder principle (Napoleon Bonaparte):** Own the story told about you through media and performance you actually control, give people a reason bigger than pay to want your win as much as you do, compete only where you hold a real structural edge, and treat rapid expansion and your own physical decline as compounding liabilities rather than as two separate problems.

*David Senra on Napoleon Bonaparte, reading from Napoleon: A Concise Biography by David Bell. Founders Podcast · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_KY09iCsjRs) · 49 min · published 2024-02-28.*

## Key lessons

- **Own the channel that tells your story instead of leaving it to others.** Napoleon founded two French language newspapers for the specific purpose of reporting his own military campaigns, on top of using painting, sculpture, and public oratory to shape his image, on the theory that once you have genuinely accomplished something worth knowing about, deliberately controlling how it gets told is not vanity, it is a duty.
- **Give people a reason bigger than pay to want the win as much as you do.** He gave speeches and had 100 sabers specially engraved for soldiers' acts of valor, telling them plainly that for the rest of their lives they would get to say they had been part of his conquering army, turning a single campaign into a permanent piece of every soldier's own identity.
- **Stage the moment you need people to choose you, don't just wait for the vote.** Returning from his first exile with only a handful of men, he walked alone toward a battalion sent to stop him, opened his coat and said "acknowledge me," daring anyone to fire, and the entire battalion threw down its weapons and joined him without a single shot.
- **Compete only where you actually hold a structural edge, and don't assume dominance in one arena transfers to another.** France could build as many ships as Britain, but it could not manufacture generations of specialized naval expertise overnight, and no amount of will closed a gap that came down to who had trained sailors and officers the longest.
- **Deliberately show weakness where you want to be attacked, then punish the opening it creates.** The day before one of his most celebrated victories, he intentionally weakened his own right flank to draw a massive Allied assault there, and when the Allies took the bait, the French tore through their now undefended center.
- **Treat fast expansion as a liability until it is proven durable, not as a trophy.** The author describes his empire in these years as "a skyscraper built in haste," growing faster than Napoleon could actually administer, defend, or supply, springing new holes faster than he could plug them even at the height of his power.
- **Study your own predecessor's exact mistake, and still be capable of repeating it anyway.** He carried an account of the disastrous invasion of Russia by a Swedish king a century earlier into his own invasion of Russia, telling an aide "we shall not repeat the folly of Charles," and then made nearly the same mistake regardless.
- **Physical decline degrades judgment exactly when the stakes are highest, and it compounds with every other problem instead of staying separate.** At the decisive clash with the main Russian force he was reportedly shaking with fever and urinary pain and directed his own army from behind the lines with a caution his younger self would have scorned, while an oversized, harder to maneuver force and an early, brutal winter closed in on him at the same time.
