# Paul Graham's Essays Part III

> **Founder principle (Paul Graham):** Graham argues wealth is the actual stuff people want, not the money used to move it around, and that you create it fastest through leverage and measurement, both of which only compound inside a small, excellent team working directly for customers. He treats a real technical or process advantage as something to keep quiet rather than announce, argues for deliberately choosing the harder of two paths because difficulty itself repels slower competitors, and insists that taste is a real, checkable skill rather than a matter of pure preference.

*David Senra on Paul Graham, reading from Hackers and Painters: Big Ideas from the Computer Age by Paul Graham (the essays How to Make Wealth, Hackers and Painters, The Other Road Ahead, Mind the Gap, Beating the Averages, and Taste for Makers), plus a short passage from his separate essay Why to Start a Startup in a Bad Economy. ## Key lessons

- **Remember that wealth is what people want, not the money used to measure it.** Graham defines wealth as the actual stuff, food, houses, travel, useful software, while money is only a way of moving that wealth around, and a company only makes money by first making something people want, which is the idea behind Y Combinator's later motto to make something people want.
- **Get leverage and measurement by working directly for customers in a small team, not inside a large company.** You cannot ask a boss to let you work ten times harder for ten times the pay, but a small startup effectively lets you make that offer directly to customers, since the value of a tiny group's work is far easier to measure than one employee's contribution buried inside a large one.
- **Understand that technology is simply a new and better technique, even in a business that looks low-tech.** Sam Walton got rich not by being a retailer but by designing a new kind of store, which is why Graham argues no one should assume leverage only exists inside software, since any real improvement in how something is done multiplies in value by everyone who adopts it.
- **When two paths are equally valuable, deliberately take the harder one.** Graham's rule of thumb at his own startup was to run upstairs: given two equally valuable features, choose the harder one on purpose, because the extra difficulty is exactly what keeps larger, slower competitors from following you there.
- **Build through gradual, visible refinement instead of complete plans made in advance.** Graham compares good software to a sketch a painter builds up stroke by stroke rather than a design finished on paper before any work begins, arguing that makers in every field learn mostly by doing the work and revising it, not by planning it perfectly first.
- **Keep a real technical or process advantage quiet instead of announcing it.** While building Viaweb, Graham's team used an unusual programming language they believed was a genuine edge and deliberately said nothing about it publicly, on the reasoning that a real advantage is worth more the longer competitors stay unaware it exists.
- **Run the business cheaply enough that it is hard to kill, regardless of the economy.** Success or failure depends far more on who the founders are than on when they started, so the most reliable way to survive a downturn is simply to operate as cheaply as possible in the first place, since the immediate cause of nearly every startup's death is running out of money.
- **Expect the gap between the best and everyone else to be enormous, and expect new leverage to widen it further.** A top chess player can beat an ordinary club player in ten thousand games without a single loss, and Graham argues the same variance applies to making money, since each new wave of technology, from the tractor to the personal computer, increases how much one person or a small team can produce relative to everyone who has not adopted it yet.
- **Judge your own work by concrete, checkable standards instead of assuming taste cannot be judged.** Graham treats simplicity, timelessness, and a willingness to redesign early work as real, checkable tests of quality, arguing that taste improves with practice the same way any other skill does, and that calling taste purely subjective is mostly a way to avoid an argument, not an accurate description of how good work actually gets made.
