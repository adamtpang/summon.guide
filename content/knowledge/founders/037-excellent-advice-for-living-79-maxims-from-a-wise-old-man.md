# Excellent Advice For Living: 79 Maxims from a Wise Old Man

> **Founder principle (Kevin Kelly):** Turn your best behaviors into automatic habit so that willpower is never required to perform them, and default to seeing every negotiation, disagreement, or relationship from the other person's point of view before asserting your own. Most of what looks like discipline or charisma is really just one of these two moves, repeated.

*David Senra on Kevin Kelly, reading from Excellent Advice for Living. Founders Podcast · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RUKafK3xCfY) · 37 min · published 2026-01-25.*

## Key lessons

- **Build habits so the behavior no longer requires a daily decision.** Kelly writes that habit is far more dependable than inspiration, and that the entire purpose of a habit is to remove an action from self negotiation, so you never have to talk yourself into doing it again.
- **Demand a deadline before starting serious creative or business work.** A deadline weeds out the extraneous and forces a project to be different rather than merely more polished, since without one people can always justify spending a little more time and money trying to make something perfect.
- **Understand what a yes means for the other party before you negotiate.** The fastest route to a deal is not a stronger pitch for your own position, it is genuinely understanding what a yes would actually get the other side, then building toward that.
- **Separate the act of creating from the act of editing.** Kelly's rule is that you cannot write and edit, or sketch and inspect, at the same time, because the editor in your head will stop the creator before an idea is finished being made.
- **Protect a new idea the way you would protect something newborn.** Fresh ideas are fragile in their first moments and need time and patience to develop before being exposed to criticism or judgment, since exposing them too early kills them before they can mature.
- **Own mistakes bluntly instead of explaining them away.** Kelly's advice to fess up quickly echoes the discipline of acknowledging errors harshly to yourself rather than rationalizing them, since nothing elevates a person's credibility more than visible, unhedged ownership of what went wrong.
- **Filter future commitments through a single question: would you do this tomorrow.** Applying that immediacy test to any invitation quickly reveals which promises are worth making, since very few requests survive being asked whether you would actually do them right now instead of at some vague future date.
- **Redo the work; the first version is only ever good, not great.** Kelly's formula is that to make something good you do it once, but to make something great you redo it, since the real craft lives in the revision, not the first draft.
- **Never stop tracking incentives, even after you think you understand them.** Kelly's advice to pay attention to incentives is reinforced by the idea that people who study incentives their whole careers still get surprised by their power, so the habit of asking what is actually being rewarded has to be renewed constantly, not learned once.
