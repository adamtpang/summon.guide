# Robert Kierlin: Founder of Fastenal

> **Founder principle (Robert Kierlin):** Organize the whole company around one repeated common goal, in Fastenal's case growth through customer service, and treat anything that does not serve it as a distraction to be eliminated. Push real decision-making authority down to the person closest to the customer instead of centralizing it at headquarters, and eliminate the status markers, executive perks, and thick rulebooks that quietly tell people they are not equally important. Deliberately suppress your own ego as the founder so pride never gets in the way of delegating, listening, and staying close to the front line.

*David Senra on Robert Kierlin, reading from The Power of Fastenal People by Robert Kierlin. Founders Podcast · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FDuAfkR6bck) · 52 min · published 2024-09-03.*

## Key lessons

- **Reduce the whole company to one repeated common goal.** Kierlin's stated goal for Fastenal, growth through customer service, appears on the back cover of his book and again on nearly every page, and he argues that keeping an organization simple is itself difficult because human nature constantly pulls people toward side goals.
- **Give every location full ownership, not just a manager.** Each Fastenal store operates as a standalone business with its own leader and full profit-and-loss responsibility, choosing its own inventory and filling one-off special customer requests directly rather than waiting on approval from headquarters.
- **Remove the visible markers that tell people they are not equal.** Kierlin explicitly rejects executive dining rooms, reserved parking, special dress codes, and separate building entrances, arguing that every such perk quietly signals to the rest of the company who does and does not matter.
- **Pay out incentives fast and only for behavior that serves the common goal.** He advises rewarding people monthly rather than waiting for an annual bonus, and warns against incentives that look productive but undercut the goal, such as punishing scrap reduction that actually came from guaranteeing a customer's full order arrived on time.
- **Lead like a basketball coach, not a baseball manager.** Kierlin contrasts a baseball manager who calls every pitch with a basketball coach who sets the goal and then lets players make their own decisions in the moment, and says Fastenal deliberately promotes leaders who coach rather than managers who control.
- **Promote from inside and stay close to the front line yourself.** More than 95 percent of Fastenal's general managers were promoted from within, his successor as CEO started as a store clerk and personally earned a commercial driving license to cover deliveries when drivers were out, and Kierlin recalls that at a company he once worked for, staff brought problems to the accessible assistant, not the manager isolated in his office.
- **Ask every employee simple questions and act on the answers.** Kierlin pushes leaders to solicit ideas from the quietest people in the room, warning that ignoring half the workforce cuts your odds of finding the best solution in half.
- **Practice ego suppression as a specific, repeatable discipline.** His concrete suggestions are to treat everyone as equal, stay silent sometimes, be willing to get dirty, and do good things anonymously, on the grounds that pride is what breaks a company's commitment to its common goal.
- **Build for the business you are becoming, not the one that already made you successful.** Kierlin writes that Fastenal avoided growth through acquisition because it would import old baggage, and the company's vending-machine distribution system, invented after his book was first written, grew into a large share of total sales precisely because leaders kept inventing ahead of the current business.
