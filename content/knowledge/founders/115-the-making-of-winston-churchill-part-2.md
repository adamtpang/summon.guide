# The Making of Winston Churchill Part 2

> **Founder principle (Winston Churchill):** Trust your own read on your ability over the crowd's lower opinion of it, even when nearly everyone around you insists you tone it down. Bet early and often on capability nobody else has built yet, precisely because nobody else has it. When a public disaster costs you the position you spent years building, answer by taking on more personal risk, not less.

*David Senra on Winston Churchill, reading from Young Titan: The Making of Winston Churchill. Founders Podcast · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-qXbHKJ0OZQ) · 56 min · published 2024-04-07.*

## Key lessons

- **Hold your own opinion of your ability above the crowd's.** Surrounded for years by peers and family who told him to tone down his self-belief, Churchill kept operating on his own internal read of what he was capable of instead of theirs, a stance the book credits directly with the fact that he was finally ready to lead when the role of prime minister arrived at 65.
- **Let a long, humiliating setback function as preparation rather than proof you were wrong.** Forced out of high office in disgrace at 40 after a catastrophic military campaign, he did not reach his defining role for another 25 years; the book argues he would not have been ready for it any earlier, and that the wilderness years built the character the later role required.
- **Build oratory through obsessive private rehearsal, not natural talent.** He prepared major speeches by rehearsing alone in front of a mirror for days at a time, sometimes striking the furniture for emphasis, so that even his supposedly extemporaneous remarks came out sounding fully composed; a friend joked he spent the best years of his life composing his impromptu speeches.
- **Read at a volume that dwarfs your peers.** While colleagues were content with a scattering of newspapers, he devoured whole shelves of books on history and biography, a habit rooted in a childhood spent closer to books than to an absent father, and one he never gave up.
- **Bet on capability nobody else has built, precisely because nobody else has it.** As the civilian head of the Royal Navy he pushed for an unprecedented 15-inch naval gun that had never been manufactured and converted the fleet from coal to oil, then secured the oil supply by having the government buy a majority stake in what became British Petroleum, reasoning that "the only punch worth throwing was a knockout punch."
- **Refuse "reasonable" when the people saying it do not believe the threat is real.** When the cabinet told him two new battleships was a reasonable number against his request for four, he pushed for four anyway, arguing afterward that his opponents only called him unreasonable because they had already decided a war with Germany was not actually going to happen.
- **Act on a threat years before it is fashionable to name it.** Touring German military maneuvers as the Kaiser's personal guest eight years before the First World War, he read the superior numbers, discipline, and organization on display as clear preparation for war and pushed Britain to match it, well before most of his peers took the threat seriously.
- **Push the pace of everyone around you once you are in the room.** Colleagues who worked alongside a rival before and after Churchill joined the cabinet described a marked change once he arrived, crediting him with forcing a man who might otherwise have coasted to actually compete for the same ground.
- **When a public disaster costs you the position, answer with more risk, not less.** After catastrophic losses in a campaign he had helped plan ended his run at the Admiralty and left him, by his own account, finished in everything he cared about, he did not retreat to safety; he chose to fight in the trenches of the war himself, writing a farewell letter to his wife to be delivered only if he did not return.
