# Paul Graham's Essays Part II

> **Founder principle (Paul Graham):** Judge which problems to work on by your own curiosity, not by apparent importance or conventional approval, and aim as close as you can to the hard center of a problem rather than nibbling its easier edges. Watch for the moment spending gets relabeled as investing, in money or in time, since that relabeling is exactly what disables the alarm that would otherwise stop you wasting either one. Founders who struggle usually cannot yet tell which of their several real problems is the one that will actually kill the company.

*David Senra on Paul Graham, reading from Paul Graham's essays How to Think for Yourself, How to Work Hard, How to Lose Time and Money, Schlep Blindness, and What I've Learned from Users. Founders Podcast · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fnTOGVDXe0w) · 50 min · published 2023-12-08.*

## Key lessons

- **Use curiosity, not conventional approval, as your compass for choosing work.** Independent-minded people are marked by a curiosity that keeps eating even after it's full; when a piece of work stops engaging yours, treat that as a real signal to change something rather than pushing through on discipline alone.
- **Deliberately surround yourself with independent-minded people, and widen that circle across time by reading history.** Hearing other people say surprising things trains you to think more independently yourself, only one or two such people are enough, and history lets you get inside the heads of people who lived through problems you haven't faced yet.
- **Go looking for opportunity specifically in unfashionable ideas.** Intellectual fashions spread through a field the same way clothing trends spread through a crowd, so the ideas nobody is currently looking at are disproportionately likely to be worth pursuing.
- **Aim at the hard core of a problem, not its easy edges, and expect to sustain that aim only some of the time.** On days you can't push all the way to center, working the easier peripheral stuff is fine, as long as you keep returning to the center whenever you can.
- **Find your own work-hour ceiling only by deliberately crossing it, then recalibrate.** The limit is different for every kind of work, roughly five hours a day for the hardest writing or programming versus nearly unlimited hours while running a startup, and it has to be rediscovered continually rather than set once.
- **Notice the exact moment spending gets relabeled as investing, whether the currency is money or time.** That relabeling is what disables the internal alarm that would otherwise flag the waste, which is why a bad bet is easier to make than an obvious splurge, and why busywork that merely resembles productivity, like clearing email, escapes scrutiny that idle time would not.
- **Go looking for the schlep everyone else is avoiding.** A schlep, a tedious and painful task, is exactly what creates a defensible business, because schlep blindness keeps most competitors from even considering the idea; reframe the search away from what problem you should solve and toward what problem you wish somebody else would go solve on your behalf.
- **When you're advising or diagnosing a struggling founder, separate what's merely uncomfortable from what will actually kill the business.** Founders often complain about the wrong thing, worrying about fundraising when the company is actually just doing badly, so ask directly whether they'd use their own product if they hadn't built it before accepting their stated problem at face value.
- **Correct course often enough to be decisive at the small scale while staying flexible about the larger path.** Frequent small corrections let you move fast without committing early to the wrong direction, which is why speed defines startups and focus is what enables the speed.
