# I had dinner with Charlie Munger

> **Founder principle (Charlie Munger):** Size every bet by real, knowable odds, cultivate powerful relationships and protected time before you need them, turn cheap and legally complicated situations into float and redeploy that float into wonderful businesses, and stay loaded with idle cash and undivided attention so you can move within days once a rare, unmistakable opportunity appears.

*David Senra on Charlie Munger, reading from The Tao of Charlie Munger, compiled by David Clark, in the week surrounding David's own dinner with Munger. Founders Podcast · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HJ3FsO-9oFU) · 78 min · published 2024-03-02.*

## Key lessons

- **Learn to size a bet by the real odds, not by how much you want it to work.** Munger picked up serious probability playing poker with his Army buddies during World War Two, and the discipline stuck for life: fold when the odds are against you, and when a genuinely good idea is rare enough to find, size the bet like it matters, the logic behind his own line that going without elementary probability is like being "a one legged man in an ass kicking contest."
- **When the odds and the payout are both precisely knowable, commit everything you can raise.** In one early arbitrage, a takeover priced British Columbia Power at 22 dollars a share against a 19 dollar market price, and Munger bought every share he could find, putting in his own money, his partnership's money, and everything he could borrow, because the spread was certain enough to bet the full stack on.
- **Cultivate powerful relationships on purpose, long before you need them.** Rejected by Harvard Law School for lacking an undergraduate degree, Munger got in only after a single phone call from a Nebraska family friend who happened to be the school's retired dean, and he built the rest of his career on the same lesson, deliberately seeking out "friends in high places."
- **Protect a fixed slice of time for your own compounding project, even while fully employed elsewhere.** While still practicing law full time, Munger set aside one hour of every working day for his own real estate deals, completed five of them, and later said the first million dollars he assembled that way was the hardest money he ever earned.
- **Turn a legally clouded, temporarily cheap situation into float, then redeploy that float into a wonderful business.** Blue Chip Stamps was priced low because of a pending antitrust suit that Munger, trained as a lawyer, correctly judged would resolve in the company's favor; its float eventually grew to roughly 100 million dollars, which he used to buy Sees Candies outright, the same move Buffett made pulling capital out of Berkshire's dying textile mill to buy an insurer.
- **Stay loaded with idle cash so you can move in days, not months, once the world hands you a price.** At the end of 2022 Berkshire was sitting on 92 billion dollars of Treasury bills earning very little, and when Munger was asked about it at dinner decades into this same pattern, his answer was simply that "the world came around to us on that."
- **Push cost discipline to an extreme on the one or two variables that actually matter, however trivial each unit looks.** Costco's refusal to give out shopping bags saves only a few cents per transaction, but across some 150 million checkouts a year that single, almost embarrassing policy is worth an estimated 45 million dollars annually, the same instinct that led Geico to drop its agents and sell insurance direct.
- **Read for decades waiting on the one unmistakable idea, then hand execution to the best specialist you know.** Munger has said he made four or five hundred million dollars from fifty years of reading Barron's, almost entirely from a single idea he acted on for an 80 million dollar, nearly risk free gain, money he then handed to Li Lu to invest and watched turn into four or five hundred million dollars more.
- **Treat undivided attention as a compounding advantage, not a personality quirk.** Asked separately what trait most explained their success, Bill Gates and Warren Buffett gave the identical one word answer, focus, echoing Munger's own account that he did not succeed through raw intelligence but by keeping an unusually long attention span, while multitasking quietly taxes everyone who does it.
