# The Practical Genius of Honda

> **Founder principle (Soichiro Honda):** Take an existing product, notice what is clumsy or ignored about it, and improve it obsessively until it is simpler and more pleasurable to use. Solve every problem at its root, build your reputation on the jobs others call hopeless, and waste nothing that happens to you, whether an earthquake, a firebombing, or a night out. Then keep the company staffed with young people who insist things must change, so the improvement loop never stops.

*David Senra on Soichiro Honda, reading from Honda: The Man and His Machines by Sol Sanders. Founders Podcast · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=oA_FT515VFM) · 48 min · published 2026-06-28.*

## Key lessons

- **Improve one product forever, and keep the company young enough to do it.** James Dyson took a whole career framework from Honda: find what is bad or overcomplicated about an existing product, improve it continuously toward simplicity, retain enough control to keep improving, and repeat. The Super Cub has been in uninterrupted production for more than sixty years with over 100 million sold, and Honda wrote youthfulness into company policy, deliberately recruiting young engineers, letting anyone propose and own their own research, and retiring when he judged the technology had moved beyond him.
- **Build your reputation on the jobs nobody else will take.** As a young repair-shop owner, older competitors got the routine work because customers thought he was too young, so he made his name on repairs other shops had failed at or refused. Two decades later, starting with the hardest problem first became a founding principle of Honda, not a slogan but the habit that had been forced on him.
- **Nothing that happens to you is wasted.** When wooden wheel spokes burned in the fires after the great earthquake, he designed cast metal spokes, the first of more than a hundred personal patents, and trading companies exported them as far as India. When American firebombing destroyed two factories, the dropped fighter-plane fuel tanks supplied scarce metal he called "Truman's gift." He even credited his drinking and carousing years with teaching him the human nature he later designed for.
- **When hand work cannot hold quality, automate it.** His early piston rings were made by hand and Toyota sent most of a fifty-unit order back. That rejection turned him into a disciple of interchangeable parts, and he designed automatic equipment so untrained wartime workers could produce reliable rings. That same production know-how carried directly into post-war motorcycle manufacturing.
- **Pair the product genius with an operator who collects the money.** Honda was so pleased that people used his motorbikes that he sold on credit and never chased payment, and the company nearly went bankrupt. Takeo Fujisawa, a partner of completely different temperament, made dealers pay up front and wrote one letter to 18,000 bicycle shop owners that built a 5,000-dealer network almost overnight. Honda said a person who cannot work with different temperaments is useless, and that talent should be sought far and wide.
- **Design for the people who are afraid of your category.** The Dream motorcycle was heavy, expensive, and built for the small cult of enthusiasts, so demand was moderate. The Cub was designed as a substitute for the bicycle, for the ordinary man, his wife, and his kids, the same move Steve Jobs made with the Apple II when he built for the thousand buyers who wanted a finished machine rather than the one hobbyist who would assemble it.
- **When the category has a bad name, sell the activity rather than the product.** Motorcycles in America meant gangs and delinquents, so Honda advertised in Time and Life, became the largest motorcycle advertiser on network television, and showed students, housewives, and businessmen riding under the line "You meet the nicest people on a Honda." The ads courted parents as much as riders; one mother told a dealer she would never let her son have a motorcycle, but she would buy him a Honda.
- **Buy the best tools and refuse protection, even at the risk of bankruptcy.** He declined to join other manufacturers lobbying the government to block imports, insisting the answer was to build the highest-performing engines in the world. He then spent roughly ten times the company's assets on imported machine tools, a recession hit, and he could not pay. A supporter inside his bank argued the tools were resalable assets and bought him six months, and Honda repaid that bank with decades of loyalty after it no longer needed them.
- **Separate research from the profit machine, and treat racing as research.** After seeing European bikes at the Isle of Man produce three times the horsepower from the same cylinder count, he spun research out as its own company funded by a fixed share of sales, reasoning that 99 percent of research fails and inside a profit-seeking parent it would be treated as a stepchild. He called entering races floating research, refused joint ventures with American automakers that his rivals accepted, and insisted every Honda product must contain an engine.
