# Rose Blumkin (Warren Buffett's Favorite Founder)

> **Founder principle (Rose Blumkin):** Run the business on a philosophy simple enough to repeat under oath: sell at a thin, honest markup, never lie to a customer, treat every disaster as a sales opportunity in disguise, and never stop showing up for work. For Mrs. B, quitting was the one decision she never let herself make.

*David Senra on Rose Blumkin, reading from The Women of Berkshire Hathaway: Lessons for Warren Buffett's Female CEOs and Directors by Karen Linder. Founders Podcast · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KKGxbEatgnc) · 33 min · published 2024-03-20.*

## Key lessons

- **Pick one thin, honest markup and never deviate from it.** Blumkin priced everything at 10 percent above cost from her very first store, a basement room she opened with a 500 dollar loan from her brother, and her own description of her business plan was as simple as "sell cheap and tell the truth." That single rule did the marketing for her: customers who saw the actual invoice trusted her completely and kept coming back for decades, along with their children and grandchildren.
- **When a competitor tries to punish you for being too fair to customers, let the fight become your advertising.** Rivals who could not match her prices had her hauled into court for unfair trade practices over carpet she sold at a sliver of a markup, and with no lawyer and limited English, she simply told the judge she sold everything 10 percent above cost. The case was thrown out, the judge bought carpet from her the next day, and when competitors branded her a bootlegger for underselling them, her reply was "you bet you, I'm the best bootlegger in town."
- **Treat every disaster as an opportunity in disguise, not a reason to pause.** When a cash crunch nearly sank the business in 1950, she rented the city auditorium for a three day sale and earned five times what she needed to clear her debts, and never borrowed again; when a fire destroyed half the store in 1961, she ran a smoke damaged fire sale that drew a line two blocks long; when a tornado leveled the building entirely in 1975, she bought the land underneath it and expanded.
- **Make your operating rules simple enough that you never have to stop and re-decide.** Because her standards were fixed, sell cheap and tell the truth, she could commit to a 5,000 table order, a thirty year lease, or a new hire instantly and never look back or second-guess it, a decisiveness that mattered as much to Nebraska Furniture Mart's edge as her honesty did.
- **Pair a relentless, hard-edged founder with someone calmer who can hold the team together.** Her son Louis joined the business after returning from World War Two and became the necessary counterweight to his mother's temper, frequently rehiring employees she had just fired, and the combination let the company keep both her sales genius and a workforce willing to stay.
- **Keep a visible reminder of what failure looks like where you will see it every day.** Blumkin framed the going out of business advertisements of competitors who had failed to compete with her and hung them on her own office wall, a daily marker of how badly things can go and proof that it happens to real businesses, not just hypothetical ones.
- **Let an early humiliation become permanent fuel instead of letting it go.** Wholesale merchants in Chicago once threw her out of their showrooms and told her not to bother them, and decades later, after building one of the most successful furniture retailers in the country, her own account of the story ends with a simple, satisfied punch line, "I outlive them all."
- **Refuse to fully retire, because continuous work is what keeps you sharp and alive.** Blumkin worked 12 to 14 hour days, seven days a week, into her hundreds, walked out of her own celebratory luncheon because she had a store to run, and could not be pulled away from the floor even for Berkshire's own annual shareholder meeting held in her honor.
- **Defend the part of the business you consider your personal domain, completely and immediately, whatever the cost.** At 96, after a dispute with her grandsons over control of the carpet department, she quit the company she had built, opened a competing store right next to it, and told a reporter she wanted it to fail, forcing Warren Buffett to negotiate a second buyout, this time at 99 and with a non-compete clause attached.
