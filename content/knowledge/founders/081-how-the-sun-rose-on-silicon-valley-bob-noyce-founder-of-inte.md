# How The Sun Rose On Silicon Valley: Bob Noyce (Founder of Intel)

> **Founder principle (Bob Noyce):** Recruit raw talent straight out of school and hand them full ownership of real decisions immediately, fund research risk with broad equity rather than profit sharing, and keep the hierarchy flat enough, down to the furniture, that a junior engineer can challenge the CEO face to face. When the invention is so new the market cannot yet understand it, become the educator yourself rather than wait for comprehension to arrive on its own.

*David Senra on Bob Noyce, reading from The Tinkerings of Robert Noyce: How the Sun Rose on the Silicon Valley by Tom Wolfe, with a supporting passage from The Intel Trinity by Michael Malone. Founders Podcast · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QNP0QqLThyI) · 52 min · published 2024-07-18.*

## Key lessons

- **Make yourself a big target for luck by staying curious and keeping in touch with people from your past.** Noyce's college mentor got him access to two of the very first transistors ever built at a tiny Iowa college in 1948, simply because that mentor had written to an old classmate at Bell Labs; that access started the exact chain of events that produced the integrated circuit.
- **Choose the environment with more room to move fast over the most prestigious one.** Noyce turned down Bell Labs and IBM out of MIT for a smaller, less advanced firm specifically because it was starting from near zero in semiconductor research, where the chances for rapid advancement looked better for a young engineer.
- **Hand real ownership to junior people immediately, and refuse to make the call for them.** At Fairchild, and even more deliberately at Intel, Noyce gave major projects to engineers straight out of graduate school with no one supervising the decision, telling them to weigh the factors themselves because "it's your ass," a policy meant to make every employee feel they could go as far as their own talent allowed.
- **Fund research risk with broad equity, not profit sharing.** Noyce gave stock options to engineers and most office workers from Intel's first days, reasoning that profit sharing pushes people toward whatever is already making money while equity rewards the willingness to gamble years of work on research that might not pay off at all.
- **Keep hierarchy visibly flat, down to the furniture, and route problems to the people doing the work instead of up a management chain.** Noyce worked at the same old scratched metal desk as the company grew and gave new hires bigger, newer desks than his own, while interdepartmental problems were settled by the working councils involved rather than escalated to executives above both sides.
- **Demand honest, explicit grading of performance, because the people worth keeping want to know exactly where they stand.** Noyce backed a five level rating system on the belief that ambitious, hardworking people do not want to be left alone or vaguely encouraged, they want the truth about how they are actually doing.
- **When you invent something the market cannot yet understand, become the educator yourself instead of waiting for comprehension to catch up.** Facing a market that Intel found not just confused but frightened by the microprocessor, the company ran more seminars and workshops on how to use it than the local junior college's entire course catalog.
- **Step back from day to day operations once the company outgrows the small, hands on phase you actually love.** Noyce and Moore started Intel the same way they had started Fairchild, a dozen engineers in lab coats working at bench tables on pure research, and once Intel's headcount and revenue had grown large, Noyce turned over the actual running of the company to Moore and Grove in 1974 and moved himself upstairs to chairman, since lab coat invention, not running an established corporation, was what he actually loved doing.
