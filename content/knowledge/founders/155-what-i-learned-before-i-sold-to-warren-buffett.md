# What I Learned Before I Sold to Warren Buffett

> **Founder principle (Barnett Helzberg Jr.):** Helzberg spent years attending Berkshire Hathaway's annual meetings and studying Warren Buffett before he ever pitched him, so when a chance sidewalk meeting appeared he was ready to act in seconds. He ran his family's jewelry business by concentrating resources on his already-strong stores rather than his weakest, keeping a second bank and a second source for anything critical, and treating even trusted advice as input rather than as a command.

## Key lessons

- **Prepare for years so you are ready the moment a rare opportunity appears.** Helzberg bought a handful of Berkshire Hathaway shares just to attend its annual meetings and learn Buffett's philosophy years before he ever met him, so when he recognized Buffett on a New York sidewalk in 1994, he introduced himself and pitched his family's company in about thirty seconds, because he had already done the preparation that made the moment usable.
- **Concentrate resources on what is already working instead of propping up what is weak.** Rather than pour effort into a struggling store trying to grow from $800,000 to $850,000 in annual volume, Helzberg's team shifted attention to a store already doing $4.5 million that could plausibly grow to $6 million, calling this upgrading the herd annually: closing the weakest performers each year and treating a talented person's time on a weak store as a real opportunity cost.
- **Never depend on a single supplier or lender, and set up the second one before you need it.** When a bank that had served the family for thirty years suddenly refused a routine loan, a second bank asked only how much they needed, and his rule afterward was to always maintain a second source arranged in advance, while still doing business with the first bank rather than burning the relationship out of anger.
- **Test a major operating change with your best people first, not your weakest.** Before Helzberg Diamonds outsourced its customer credit business to focus purely on selling diamonds, the company deliberately ran the experiment through one of its best store managers, so a failed test could not be blamed on weak execution and a successful one could be rolled out with confidence.
- **Turn ordinary hospitality into a competitive advantage available to a company of any size.** Where standard mall stores posted signs banning food and drink, Helzberg stores openly welcomed ice cream cones and hot dogs, deliberately signaling that the store operated on the customer's terms rather than its own convenience.
- **Treat advice, even from a trusted mentor, as input rather than as a command.** Acting on a prized mentor's warning against shopping center locations, the company pulled out of an early covered mall and nearly went out of business as malls became the dominant retail format, and the lesson he kept afterward was that advice is advice, not a command, and the person who follows bad advice owns the result.
- **Cut every product line outside your actual core, even when you expect revenue to fall.** Helzberg Diamonds discontinued china, crystal, silver, luggage, and radios to concentrate purely on jewelry, expecting a drop in dollar volume in exchange for higher margins, and instead both volume and profit rose, confirming that focus itself was a lever of growth, not just a cost saved.
- **Ask a third, more specific question to get past polite feedback.** Beyond asking what people liked or disliked about his leadership, Helzberg made a habit of also asking what he was not doing that people wished he would, treating that third question as the one most likely to surface something genuinely useful.
- **Let results carry your reputation instead of announcing them yourself.** Both his father and a fellow retailing family taught him to be a "sleeper," staying quiet about success rather than broadcasting it, on the reasoning that visible success invites competitive attention a private business does not need.
