# How To Make A Few Billion Dollars by Brad Jacobs

> **Founder principle (Brad Jacobs):** Jacobs treats the founder's own mind as the first thing to engineer: manage your inner monologue like a discipline, research an industry's real megatrend obsessively before you commit capital to it, and then pay without limit for the rare people who can execute on it. Across five startups built into billion dollar companies, his repeated edge was treating a stock crash, a bad law, or a hostile short report the same way he treats any other problem, as the raw material the next opportunity is made of.

## Key lessons

- **Reframe an anxious thought as data to examine, not a verdict to accept.** Jacobs describes treating his own negative internal chatter as "useful data" rather than objective reality, and mentally stepping outside a problem to advise himself the way he would advise a worried friend, before making any decision while anxious.
- **Treat problems as the actual raw material of the business, not an interruption to it.** An early mentor told him business is fundamentally the act of finding, embracing, and profitably solving problems; when a hostile short seller report crashed his stock more than 20 percent in a day, his team spent hours picking the report apart, concluded their own shares were now radically undervalued, and bought back roughly 2 billion dollars of stock that was worth about 6 billion a few years later.
- **Build your own exhaustive research file on an industry before committing a dollar to it.** His standard process before entering any new market runs through trade journals, employee reviews, SEC filings, sell side and buy side analysts, investment bankers, venture capitalists, industry vendors, and even skeptical journalists, all aimed at identifying the one real megatrend before betting on it.
- **When an entire industry runs on scattered, hard to get information, build or buy the system that centralizes it.** At his oil brokerage in 1979, before the internet existed, he built an internal database so brokers could share pricing intelligence in hours instead of days; at United Rentals he simply bought the software company whose platform the whole industry already ran on, which handed him aggregated market data none of his competitors had.
- **Accept a real loss immediately once the premise behind it is gone, rather than holding on to avoid admitting the mistake.** When a promised 600 billion dollar federal infrastructure bill mostly failed to materialize, he sold the road maintenance companies he had built around that bet at roughly a 500 million dollar loss rather than wait, a habit he calls "radical acceptance" of the world as it actually is.
- **Screen ruthlessly for talent and overpay for it without hesitation.** His hiring filter asks whether a candidate can argue multiple sides of an issue, change their own mind, and imagines his own reaction if that person quit without warning, treating underpaying a top performer to save money as a direct, measurable loss of profit rather than a saving.
- **Ask your own frontline people two blunt questions on a regular basis.** He sends every employee, individually rather than buried in a survey, the same two prompts: what is your single best idea to improve our company, and what is the stupidest thing we are doing as a company, on the theory that management layers soften bad news before it ever reaches the top.
- **When you have a real advantage, press it instead of slowing down to keep a rival comfortable.** After a competing CEO invited him to lunch essentially to ask him to ease off, Jacobs kept accelerating instead; years later his company was generating several times that rival's revenue, and he calls slowing down to be liked a losing strategy.
- **A once in a career move is justified by the math of the moment, not by whether anyone has done it before.** Advisers warned him no company had ever bought back such a large share of its own stock in a single move; he did it anyway because the price made no sense otherwise, and it created roughly 4 billion dollars in value.
