# The Eternal Pursuit of Unhappiness by David Ogilvy

> **Founder principle (David Ogilvy):** Stay in a permanent state of divine discontent: never let satisfaction with your last piece of work curdle into smugness about the next one. Build courage, idealism, curiosity, playfulness, candor, intuition, free-spiritedness, and persistence into daily habits, because each one has a matching vice, fear, small ambition, boredom, grimness, false politeness, over-reliance on pure logic, bureaucracy, and giving up, that will fill the vacuum if the virtue isn't drilled on purpose. A creative culture only survives its founder if these habits are actually practiced, not just remembered as things he used to say.

## Key lessons

- **Treat satisfaction with your own work as a warning sign, not a reward.** The handbook calls this divine discontent, a permanent, low-grade dissatisfaction with your last piece of work that keeps you from coasting, the same instinct David compares to Steve Jobs asking what you do the day after you make something wonderful.
- **Give the company a spine before you give it a strategy.** Courage is treated as the root habit that makes every other one possible, on the theory that a business only earns real trust once it is willing to state an honest opinion out loud instead of hedging it away.
- **Size your ambition to compete with the immortals, not to hit next quarter's number.** The habit of idealism argues that a company whose goals shrink to short-term targets has already limited how great it can ever become, since the size of the dream sets the ceiling on the result.
- **Go hunting for ideas instead of waiting for them to show up.** Curiosity is described as a habit of constantly looking, at old ads, magazines, art, anything in reach, because discovery is mostly just noticing what everyone has already seen and thinking something nobody else has.
- **Make the workplace fun on purpose, because grim people do worse creative work.** Ogilvy once told a reporter he didn't care if staff came to work in pajamas as long as the work got done, and the day that ran in the paper an entire department showed up in pajamas.
- **Treat false politeness as a real cost, not a courtesy.** The handbook warns against a tyranny of politeness and says candor works like flint on stone: "only dead fish go with the flow," and conflict, not agreement, is what actually produces the spark.
- **Hire for productive strangeness over smooth conformity.** The team argues real talent clusters among nonconformists and dissenters rather than the polished caretakers who tend to rise inside large, founderless competitors that got that way by losing their edge.
- **Replace rulebooks with atmosphere.** Free-spiritedness is framed as a near-absence of formal process, on the theory that heavy bureaucracy produces meeting material, not real ideas, and that people do their best work when trusted with judgment instead of treated as servants to a system.
- **Expect the creative process to feel like a roller coaster, and keep going anyway.** Persistence is framed as tolerating dead ends, blind spots, and self-doubt without quitting, since the difference between a moderately creative person and a highly creative one is usually dogged repetition rather than raw genius.
