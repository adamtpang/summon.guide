# How To Lose A Few Billion Dollars: The Life Story of Samuel Insull

> **Founder principle (Samuel Insull):** Insull spent 53 years building the actual business model of the modern electric utility: cheap universal power funded by relentless expansion, real public relations, and total command of operating detail. That same relentless growth was financed almost entirely by debt that only worked as long as it could be perpetually refinanced; when the 1929 crash cut off that refinancing, decades of otherwise excellent decisions collapsed into what newspapers at the time called the largest business failure in history. He was never convicted of fraud, and called his own errors honest mistakes in judgment, but the debt was his choice, made repeatedly, long after the warning signs were visible.

## Key lessons

- **Earn a place next to a great mentor through excessive unpaid service before you ask for anything.** As a teenager, Insull volunteered nights and weekends taking dictation and later stringing wire for Thomas Edison's chief engineer for no pay; that service, not a request, is what got him recommended as Edison's own private secretary.
- **Learn a business by reading every scrap of paper in it, not just your assigned part.** At 19 he read every contract on file until he knew Edison's European telephone affairs better than his own boss did, and the same habit, total command of the underlying paperwork, became the core operating skill of his entire career.
- **To turn a luxury good into a mass necessity, price and expand toward monopoly scale rather than toward the highest margin.** While competitors treated electricity as a premium product for the few, Insull deliberately sold power at the lowest sustainable price, on the theory that a bigger customer base was what let him spread fixed costs and keep lowering price further; he guessed Chicago's market at a million customers when the industry's own optimists said 25,000, and his companies eventually supplied something like an eighth of the entire country.
- **When you acquire a smaller competitor, pay them more than the plant is worth.** Insull was consistently generous to the small stations he bought out, reasoning that overpaying does not buy loyalty but reliably keeps people from becoming enemies, the same logic Rockefeller used a generation earlier in oil.
- **Treat public relations as a core operating function, not an afterthought.** He built one of the first full scale corporate public relations departments, gave away a free consumer magazine by the tens of thousands, and used rate cuts rather than advertising as his main tool for winning public goodwill, on the theory that an educated customer is what protects a regulated business from its critics.
- **Reframe an alarming number into a conservative sounding structure if the size of the ask is what is scaring people off.** When his board balked at issuing 100 million dollars in bonds, he asked instead for a mortgage with no fixed limit, which sounded smaller and safer; he eventually drew five times that original amount through it.
- **Debt that only survives on perpetual refinancing is not survivable once credit actually stops flowing.** Insull's entire growth engine depended on rolling ever larger amounts of debt into new debt, which worked through several earlier financial panics and then failed completely once the 1929 crash made new credit disappear and old debts had to be paid in cash he did not have.
- **Surviving past downturns is not proof you are immune to the next one.** Insull explicitly compared the Great Depression to panics he had already lived through and kept borrowing and expanding well into 1930, spending roughly 200 million dollars on capital projects in a single year, almost entirely on debt, after the crash had already happened.
- **A fight to keep control of your own stock can force you into the exact concentrated, overcollateralized position that destroys you.** To defend against a raider quietly buying up shares in his operating companies, Insull built two heavily leveraged holding companies to hold control; as the market fell, each drop forced them to post more of their own stock as collateral, until every share they owned sat in the hands of the banks, and Insull was left, in one banker's description, "too broke to be bankrupt."
