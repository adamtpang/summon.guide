# The Autobiography of Andrew Carnegie

> **Founder principle (Andrew Carnegie):** Carnegie watched technological change destroy his father's handloom weaving business and resolved to always be on the early side of the next technological shift instead. He credited his success not to being the smartest person in a room but to finding and organizing people who knew more than he did, concentrating his entire attention and capital on one business rather than spreading either, and treating optimism and self-judgment as habits to be practiced on purpose.

*David Senra on Andrew Carnegie, reading from The Autobiography of Andrew Carnegie. Founders Podcast · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=flaB56wUuYM) · 52 min · published 2024-01-23.*

## Key lessons

- **Adapt to technological change before it forces you to.** Carnegie's father lost his livelihood when steam looms replaced handloom weaving and he failed to adapt in time, and Carnegie drew the opposite lesson from watching his family fall into poverty: get ahead of a technology shift rather than be run over by one, a habit he repeated later by moving early into iron bridges, then steel, then industrial chemistry.
- **Cultivate optimism as a deliberate practice, not a personality trait you either have or lack.** He described a sunny disposition as something that can be built the way a habit is built, and paired it with what he called the judge within, a private standard of self-approval that mattered more to him than outside praise or criticism.
- **Your first organizing success matters more than it looks.** As a boy, Carnegie got other children to gather food for his pet rabbits and pigeons simply by offering to name the rabbits after them, and he later credited that small trick, not any personal technical mastery, as the real root of his ability to run enormous businesses built on people who understood the actual machinery better than he ever would.
- **Seize an opening the moment it appears.** Offered a telegraph messenger job while admitting he did not know the city and might not be strong enough for it, Carnegie's only answer was that he could start immediately, reasoning that hesitation simply hands the opportunity to the next boy in line.
- **Treat free access to knowledge as a debt to be repaid.** A local colonel's decision to open his four-hundred-volume personal library to working boys on Saturdays gave a teenage Carnegie his only real education, and Carnegie later built free public libraries across the country as a direct, lifelong repayment of that specific debt.
- **Concentrate so completely that you remove your own distractions.** Once he committed to iron and steel, Carnegie sold off his outside stock holdings and refused to own any securities at all, reasoning that even glancing at a stock ticker each morning would pull his attention away from the one business he had decided to master.
- **Be willing to spend on what competitors call excessive before the payoff is obvious.** Carnegie hired chemists at his blast furnaces when rival manufacturers considered this an extravagant waste, and the resulting reduction in wasted material became one of his most profitable and longest lasting advantages, one competitors did not copy until it was too late to catch up.
- **Pay for the education you never got by helping the next person get theirs.** Carnegie's own rise depended on older, established men choosing to mentor a poor immigrant teenager for no clear return, and he treated that pattern as proof that people willing to help themselves rarely have to look far to find others willing to help them.
