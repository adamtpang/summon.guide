# Paul Graham (How To Do Great Work)

> **Founder principle (Paul Graham):** Choose work using only two filters, real aptitude and a level of interest that borders on excessive, and treat the question of whether it's important as unanswerable until much later. You cannot know what a field is actually like except by doing it, so guess, start, and expect to be wrong sometimes rather than waiting to plan correctly. Protect your morale and your taste on purpose: study the best existing work until you know what excellence looks like, and remove anyone, including a spouse, who treats your ambition as competition for their attention.

*David Senra on Paul Graham, reading from How to Do Great Work, an essay by Paul Graham. Founders Podcast · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=F3HZpu_YH64) · 58 min · published 2024-04-01.*

## Key lessons

- **Filter candidate work by natural aptitude and excessive interest, not by how important it looks.** Importance is nearly impossible to judge in the moment and is usually only visible in hindsight, so the reliable test is whether you're curious to a degree that would bore most other people. Work also has to be your own project; do not let work mean something other people assign to you.
- **You cannot know what a field is really like except by doing it, so guess, start, and expect to be wrong sometimes.** Years can pass before you know how much you like a kind of work or how good you are at it, and school-style planning fails here because education systems expect you to commit to a field long before you could possibly know what it's like.
- **At each stage, choose whatever is most interesting and preserves the most future options, rather than committing to one rigid long-term plan.** Graham calls this "staying upwind": the recipe for most people who've done great work was not a plan pursued from childhood but a sequence of choices for what seemed most alive at each step.
- **Catch avoidance disguised as busywork by periodically asking yourself directly whether you're working on what you most want to work on.** Per-project procrastination is more dangerous than daily procrastination because it hides behind the appearance of hard work on something else entirely, sometimes for years at a stretch.
- **Manage morale as a deliberate input to the work, not an afterthought.** "Husband your morale," expect setbacks as a normal part of solving hard problems rather than letting one deflate you all at once, keep a direct line to even a small, genuinely devoted audience instead of routing through intermediaries, and remove people, including a spouse, who treat your need to work as competition for their attention.
- **Deliberately cultivate taste by studying the best existing work in your field before trying to produce your own.** Consciously learning what's best and what makes it so gives you something to aim at; refusing to try to be the best is why so many people end up merely mediocre instead.
- **Have the confidence to scrap and redo work that no longer fits, no matter how much time is already sunk into it.** Status quo bias and laziness will otherwise talk you into keeping something you're only proud of because of the effort it cost, not because it's actually right.
- **Go to where the best people in your field physically cluster, and deliberately seek out the best colleagues you can find.** Great work happens in clusters throughout history because colleagues don't just improve your output, they shape who you become, so choose to work alongside people you actually want to turn into.
