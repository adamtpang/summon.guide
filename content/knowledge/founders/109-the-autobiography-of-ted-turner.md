# The Autobiography of Ted Turner

> **Founder principle (Ted Turner):** Combine the assets you already own into combinations competitors structurally cannot match, and bet on the medium that is growing fastest rather than the one that is biggest today. When you believe in an idea years before anyone else does, race to prove it before your capital runs out, funding the bet by selling your most valuable but least strategic asset rather than your core business. Match total commitment against rivals many times your size, because a giant with divided attention usually loses to a founder treating the fight as existential. Keep every deal simple enough that you never have to trade away control to survive it.

*David Senra on Ted Turner, reading from Call Me Ted by Ted Turner. Founders Podcast · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HD3AqauHMy0) · 83 min · published 2024-04-14.*

## Key lessons

- **Set goals so large you never run out of them.** Turner's father told him shortly before his death that he had made the mistake of setting his own goals too low and then had nothing left to aim for; Turner took the opposite approach, telling a stranger on a plane in 1976, more than a decade before it was true, "I'm going to be a billionaire."
- **Find the one point of leverage in a bad contract before you're forced to accept someone else's terms.** When a family friend held Turner's late father to a deathbed agreement to sell off the family's billboard assets, the 24-year-old Turner discovered the buyer had never included a non-compete clause, then had his own sales team quietly re-sign every landlord's lease at a higher rate, destroying the value of the assets he was contractually obligated to hand over until the buyer agreed to cancel the deal.
- **When you can't pay cash, structure the seller and a bank into the deal, and keep every acquisition as its own legal entity.** Turner bought a Chattanooga billboard company with no money down by getting the seller to finance most of the price and a bank to lend the rest for equity, then followed his father's practice of never folding an acquisition into the parent company, preserving his core equity and leaving room to restructure around tax exposure later.
- **Combine the assets you already own into a bundle competitors can't structurally copy.** Turner filled his billboard company's unsold ad inventory, worthless to him otherwise, with promotions for his own radio and then TV stations, giving him free advertising no single-medium competitor had access to and, station by station, the largest local audience in the market.
- **Bet on the medium that is growing fastest, not the one that is biggest today.** Turner never warmed to radio the way he later did to television, and kept moving up the chain from billboards to radio to local TV to cable and satellite specifically because each new medium had a higher ceiling than the one before it.
- **When the standard data provider won't measure you, build your own proof.** Nielsen refused to verify his superstation's audience outside Atlanta because doing so would help him compete with its broadcast-network customers, so Turner had his staff sort the postmarks on direct-response checks into an Atlanta pile and a non-Atlanta pile; the non-Atlanta pile dwarfed the local one and became his evidence for national advertisers.
- **Fund an unproven, bet-the-company idea by selling your most valuable but least strategic asset, and race to prove the concept before the money runs out.** With no lender willing to finance a 24-hour news network, Turner sold a Charlotte TV station for a $19 million profit to get CNN on air, comparing his plan to Rommel's desert tactic of capturing British fuel dumps: get moving first, and the resources to keep going follow.
- **Match total commitment against rivals many times your size.** Turner said his competitors typically had "less commitment and desire than I did," and it held twice: he outlasted RCA in a licensing fight and forced a joint ABC and Westinghouse news venture to withdraw and pay him $25 million, after which no competing 24-hour news channel appeared for 13 years.
- **Keep every deal simple enough that you never have to trade away control to survive it.** Turner's over-leveraged, overly complex acquisition of MGM's library from Kirk Kerkorian nearly cost him the company; John Malone's rescue package saved it but left Turner needing board approval above a certain deal size for the first time in his career, a loss of autonomy he named as the point his long run as sole operator began to end.
