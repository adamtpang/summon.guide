# Herb Kelleher: Founder of Southwest Airlines

> **Founder principle (Herb Kelleher):** Treat hardship as fuel rather than a burden, and build the entire business around the lowest possible cost structure so it survives downturns without debt. Know exactly what you compete with, not only who, and refuse to chase market share at the expense of profit. Lead from the front, keep the company inside its niche, and treat employees as missionaries on a crusade rather than hires doing a job.

*David Senra on Herb Kelleher, reading from Nuts! Southwest Airlines' Crazy Recipe for Business and Personal Success and Herb's Heroes. Founders Podcast · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=460sCtmGmKI) · 45 min · published 2024-04-09.*

## Key lessons

- **Treat adversity as an opportunity, not a burden, from the very start.** Raised by a widowed mother after his older brother was killed in the war and his father died the following year, he absorbed her lesson that stress and hardship were never a debit but a chance to excel; decades later, asked how he handled the constant stress of running an airline, his answer was direct: "I don't handle it, I like it."
- **Fight the legal and competitive battles yourself rather than folding when the numbers turn against you.** When three rival airlines sued to block Southwest's flying certificate and two courts ruled against the company, with the entire original investment consumed by legal fees, Kelleher told the board "let's do one more round with them" and offered to argue the case to the Texas Supreme Court personally, postponing his own fees and paying court costs out of pocket.
- **Build the cost structure to survive the next downturn, not just to win the current fight.** Southwest paid for roughly 80 percent of its planes out of earnings instead of debt and flew only the Boeing 737 to cut training and parts costs, which needed about half the staff per aircraft of the industry's high end; the airline stayed profitable for 47 consecutive years against an industry where the next-best streak was five.
- **Know exactly what you compete with, not only who.** When a shareholder pushed to raise a fifteen-dollar Dallas to San Antonio fare that a rival airline charged sixty-two dollars for, Kelleher refused, because Southwest's real competitor was the cost of driving, not another carrier; that discipline kept fares low enough to keep expanding the market instead of merely taking share from rivals.
- **Chase profit, not market share.** He told employees he would punch anyone in the nose who raised the subject of market share in a meeting, reasoning that four percent of a market while profitable beats ninety percent while losing money, a rule that kept Southwest out of jumbo jets, international routes, and head-to-head fights with the major carriers.
- **Turn a competitor's copy into a public argument you can win with the customer.** When a rival copied Southwest's new fare and rebranded it an introductory rate despite decades in the same market, Southwest held its price and threw in a free bottle of liquor instead, then told customers plainly why the incumbent was suddenly discounting; most business travelers paid the higher fare and took the gift, and the rival pulled out of the route two years later.
- **Prepare for the future with scenario questions instead of a rigid plan.** Southwest ran recurring "future scenario generation" meetings asking what it would do if a competitor opened new cities or an airport rebuilt, producing a menu of ready responses rather than a single fixed plan that reality would inevitably break.
- **Move at the speed the opportunity actually demands.** When a struggling competitor announced it was shutting down at midnight, Southwest's team was negotiating with city officials by nine the next morning, had a gate deal signed by early afternoon, and was already installing its own equipment before the press conference ended; a new marketing hire who proposed a nine-month campaign timeline was told the real deadline was next Wednesday.
- **Lead from the front and treat the mission as bigger than any one person, including yourself.** On the airline's busiest day of the year he could be found loading bags on the tarmac in a poncho, unrecognized as the founder and CEO, and he described the company not as employees doing a job but as missionaries on a crusade, a stance he backed with the plain advice to spend more time with your own people than with other CEOs.
