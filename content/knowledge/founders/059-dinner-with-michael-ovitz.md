# Dinner With Michael Ovitz

> **Founder principle (Michael Ovitz):** Long experience need not diminish ambition, but it should change how you sustain it. In Senra's account of their dinner, Ovitz combines urgency and expansive thinking with advice to choose aligned partners, welcome unwelcome truths, and reclaim some time once the business can support it. The practical goal is a pace and circle of people that let meaningful work continue for decades.

## Key lessons

- **Demonstrate the difference between adequate service and exceptional service.** Senra recalls Ovitz supplying a director with promising scripts before the director's own agent did, sustaining that effort for two years. The prospect could compare useful work rather than competing descriptions of quality. Choose a visible customer outcome where sustained attention will make the service gap unmistakable.
- **Treat the profession's customary boundaries as proposals you can test.** Ovitz moved from representing talent to packaging projects, advising on acquisitions, and helping Coca-Cola with advertising. Each step extended his ability to connect people and opportunities into a new commercial setting. When an adjacent customer problem fits a proven capability, investigate it even if competitors define the job more narrowly.
- **Choose partners for compatible ambition and keep trust proportional to experience.** Ovitz warned that partners with different aspirations may experience your next opportunity as an unwanted distraction. Yet he also resisted treating a few betrayals as evidence that future partnerships were impossible, comparing those experiences with a much larger set of relationships. Discuss the desired scale and pace before committing, and evaluate each new partner without making past hurt the sole decision rule.
- **Study the overlooked builders as well as the famous ones.** Ovitz encouraged Senra to keep introducing listeners to less familiar founders, since many people already know the major industrial fortunes. He also credited studying predecessors in his own industry. Build a reading list that broadens the available operating examples, rather than repeatedly confirming lessons from the same celebrated people.
- **Reassess stalled opportunities using a concrete path to completion.** Senra uses Shogun, Tootsie, and Rain Man to illustrate projects others had left undeveloped before Ovitz helped move them forward. Prior rejection alone did not tell him whether the ingredients could now be assembled. Look for the specific missing participant, agreement, or development step before accepting another person's conclusion that a project cannot work.
- **Make urgency explicit instead of assuming everyone shares your clock.** Ovitz described expecting work requested on a Thursday evening to arrive Friday morning, while a colleague understood a much slower timetable. Senra reinforces the point with a Herb Kelleher story about compressing an advertising schedule. State the actual deadline and remove unnecessary waiting so the team can act at the intended pace.
- **Reconsider the working hours after the business becomes established.** Asked about his earlier regret over excessive work, Ovitz distinguished the underfunded climb from the period after CAA had gained a strong position. He still believed he could have reduced his hours later without losing the result. Review whether each additional demand supports long-term effectiveness or merely preserves an old habit from a different stage.
- **Give candor a protected place in close relationships.** Ovitz valued a mutual friend because he spoke directly and truthfully, and he offered Senra criticism during their own conversation. Senra connects this with founders who seek information directly from employees and customers. Reward people for surfacing uncomfortable facts early, then create routes for those facts to reach you without excessive filtering.
- **Preserve a project that gives continued work a purpose.** Ovitz remained driven in his late seventies and described needing something to advance and improve. Senra relates this to other older founders who continued building after financial necessity had disappeared. Treat that observation as a prompt to choose meaningful ongoing work that suits you, rather than allowing a financial milestone to decide how your attention will be used afterward.
