# Sam Zell's Autobiography

> **Founder principle (Sam Zell):** Zell treated early rejection and scarcity as raw material rather than as pain to avoid, generalizing a teenage arbitrage trick and a summer of cold selling into a lifelong investment philosophy built on tenacity. He bet capital on character once trust was earned, defaulted to cutting obvious waste rather than betting on growth stories in troubled companies, and once he took a company public he treated the fiduciary duty to shareholders as absolute, selling even a business he loved the moment the price cleared his own bar.

*David Senra on Sam Zell, reading from Zell's own autobiography, Am I Being Too Subtle: Straight Talk from a Business Rebel. Founders Podcast · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1Oskijvz6No) · 68 min · published 2023-10-19.*

## Key lessons

- **Build tolerance for rejection deliberately, early, in a job that has nothing to do with your real ambition.** His first real job was door to door sales with constant no's, and he later called comfort with being told no a fundamental trait of an entrepreneur. He treated that summer as an investment in a skill, not just a low paying job he happened to take.
- **Recognize scarcity pricing power in a trivial personal experiment, then generalize it.** As a teenager he bought a single magazine unavailable in his suburb and resold it to friends at six times the price, drawing the lesson that "when there is scarcity, price is no object." He said that lesson became a governing principle of his entire investment philosophy, not a one off trade.
- **Assume there is always a way through an obstacle, then find it.** Assembling a full city block of student housing lots in Ann Arbor one holdout at a time taught him what he later called his most fundamental principle of entrepreneurship: tenacity, the assumption that a path exists paired with the discipline to keep looking until you find it.
- **Bet capital on the person, not the paperwork, once trust is earned through direct evidence.** He and his most important early partner, developer Jay Pritzker, closed a deal and later realized they had never even drafted a formal partnership agreement. Once character was proven, it mattered more to both of them than the contract.
- **When you buy into a poorly run company, default to cutting the obvious waste, not to growth projections.** One acquisition target ran executive offices with Persian rugs and Perrier in the drinking fountains and sent staff on a Caribbean cruise while struggling financially, and it went bankrupt within six years. The reliable return, he found, was removing that kind of excess, not betting on an optimistic story about future revenue.
- **Build an explicit arbitrage, not a vague instinct to buy low.** Between 1974 and 1977 he assembled billions in real estate using large amounts of fixed rate, non recourse debt in a period when inflation ran higher than his interest rate, generating a positive spread the moment each deal closed, before he improved a single asset.
- **Prefer limited competition to open bidding, and stay out of auctions you do not control.** He said he would rather hold a natural monopoly or oligopoly than compete, because competitive bidding inflates prices past what the underlying economics justify, and he built his acquisition strategy around markets and situations without a crowd.
- **Once you are a fiduciary, the obligation overrides your own attachment.** Zell called Equity Office his baby and said he probably would have never sold it had he kept the company private, but once it was public he treated his duty to shareholders as absolute. When an offer cleared the bar he had privately set for what he would pay for the same real estate himself, he sold, with no reported regret afterward.
- **Stay chairman of vision, not CEO of daily operations, and protect direct access to yourself as a retention tool.** He described his own role as "chairman of everything and CEO of nothing," picking capable operators and staying close to them without managing day to day. One senior manager who left for a better paying job with more authority came back specifically because he missed being able to walk into Zell's office and get an answer immediately.
