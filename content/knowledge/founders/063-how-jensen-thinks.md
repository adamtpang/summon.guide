# How Jensen Thinks

> **Founder principle (Jensen Huang):** An ambitious strategy depends on the organization's ability to learn and change direction. Huang developed Nvidia through painful product failures, then built routines that exposed technical reasoning, moved information directly to decision makers, and assigned clear responsibility for results. Those routines helped the company recognize new uses for its chips and commit resources to markets that were still taking shape.

## Key lessons

- **Make the product excel at the job buyers are actually choosing it for.** The first Nvidia card included capabilities customers did not particularly want, while more focused competitors delivered the graphics performance buyers sought. Huang later treated that failure as evidence that technical talent did not automatically confer product judgment. State who the product serves and the result it must deliver before adding another feature.
- **Challenge the sequence of work when time threatens survival.** During development of the Riva 128, Nvidia bought an expensive emulator despite limited remaining cash. It allowed software work and testing to advance without waiting for a physical prototype, shortening the path to a saleable product. Break a schedule into dependencies and distinguish unavoidable physical limits from waiting created by customary process.
- **Make reasoning visible enough to teach and challenge.** Huang's frequent whiteboard sessions required people to show how they reached an answer, making gaps easier to identify. The episode also describes his severe public criticism and the intention that a mistake become a lesson for the whole company. The transferable practice is to examine the reasoning and share the resulting learning widely, with the leader's own past failures subject to scrutiny as well.
- **Get observations from the edge before they become a consensus report.** Nvidia's top-five emails let employees surface current work, customer problems, market developments, and delays in concise bullets. Huang read them to detect weak signals that could disappear through layers of management. Establish a route for specific observations to reach someone able to connect them across the business, and make those messages easy to search.
- **Assign every mission a named person with clear accountability.** Huang designated a leader for each project and could draw talent from functional groups rather than treating existing divisions as fixed boundaries. That structure combined individual responsibility with the ability to redirect resources. For a priority that crosses teams, identify one accountable owner and make the intended outcome explicit.
- **Match development cadence to the customer's buying cycle.** Nvidia reorganized engineering into parallel teams for new architectures and derivatives, allowing more frequent releases than a single sequential development effort. Buyers could expect an improved part sooner, reducing their reason to switch suppliers for a temporary advantage. Study when customers replace or upgrade products, then design staffing and release work around that rhythm.
- **Recognize exceptional contributions while the connection is clear.** The episode describes special equity grants that named the employee's contribution and could occur outside an annual compensation review. Huang personally examined recommendations and sometimes reached into the organization to recognize work. Tie meaningful rewards to demonstrated results and explain the reason so people can see what the company values.
- **Attack the weak point in your own successful position.** Concerned that low-priced competitors could enter beneath Nvidia's premium products, Huang saw an opportunity in otherwise functional chips that could not meet top-tier performance requirements. Selling those parts in lower-performance products created revenue and protected a vulnerable market segment. Examine whether discarded output or an unserved price level could become the entry point for a competitor.
- **Create demand by making an unfamiliar capability easier to use and learn.** Researchers' non-graphics uses of GPUs helped reveal a larger opportunity, and CUDA reduced the effort required to program them. Nvidia supported university teaching, supplied equipment, helped develop a textbook, and gathered researchers for feedback while sustaining investment through a difficult period. When a platform's value is not yet obvious, product development, user education, and close observation of early users belong in the same investment plan.
