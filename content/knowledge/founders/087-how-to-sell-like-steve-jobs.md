# How To Sell Like Steve Jobs

> **Founder principle (Steve Jobs):** Sell the improvement a product makes in someone's life, not the product's specifications, and compress that improvement into one plain sentence repeated everywhere the audience will encounter it. Build every presentation around a small number of vivid, wordless slides and real rehearsal rather than dense data, and let genuine conviction, not borrowed enthusiasm, carry the room.

*David Senra on Steve Jobs, reading from The Presentation Secrets of Steve Jobs: How to Be Insanely Great in Front of Any Audience by Carmine Gallo. Founders Podcast · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=k72aCBm7Jpk) · 47 min · published 2024-07-03.*

## Key lessons

- **Sell the improvement, not the product.** Jobs opened the 1998 iMac launch by naming what customers actually wanted, getting online simply and fast, and described competitors' machines in blunt, plain language before ever showing his own product, because the question that matters most to any audience is why they should care.
- **Compress your entire pitch into one plain sentence, then repeat it everywhere.** The iPod launch reduced its whole pitch to a single vivid image of carrying a thousand songs in a pocket, and the MacBook Air launch reduced its pitch to being the thinnest notebook in the world, with both lines repeated across the keynote, the press materials, and the website alike, because repetition is what makes an idea stick and spread on its own.
- **Plan the structure on paper before you touch a slide.** Jobs built presentations around a strict rule of three: one main idea broken into three supporting points, each carried by a story, a fact, or a demo, the same structure behind his Stanford commencement address, which moved through three named stories before its close.
- **Keep slides nearly wordless so you are forced to know the message cold.** Jobs favored a single image over blocks of text specifically so he could not lean on the slide as a script, which meant he had to speak directly to the audience instead of reading at them.
- **Make abstract numbers concrete by anchoring them to something familiar.** When Apple's personal computer market share sat at a discouraging 5 percent, Jobs reframed it against BMW and Mercedes, both low-share but highly desirable brands, and when announcing 4 million iPhones sold in 200 days, he broke the figure down to 20,000 phones sold every single day so the audience could actually feel the scale.
- **Choose plain, unusual, human words over jargon.** Jobs described products in vivid, almost conversational language rather than technical specification language, which made each claim sound like an actual person talking rather than a script written by committee, and made the claims easier to remember afterward.
- **Let independent proof carry weight your own claims cannot.** Jobs routinely displayed or read customer and press testimonials on screen during presentations, using the same social proof logic that makes people trust what others have already approved before they will trust a claim standing alone.
- **Rehearse far past the point of comfort.** The presenters this book studied practiced roughly 90 hours for every hour they spent on stage, and one story in the book has Jobs arriving four hours late to an interview simply because he was still rehearsing a presentation; effortless delivery, the book argues, is manufactured in private, not improvised live.
- **Conviction has to be real, or the audience can tell.** A reporter who watched Jobs privately preview a new ad before its public release described him as genuinely moved by it, not performing for her benefit, and the lesson underneath every technique in the book is that real belief transfers to an audience while borrowed enthusiasm does not.
