# The Biography of George Lucas

> **Founder principle (George Lucas):** Never again let a financier hold the leverage over your own work. Trade a smaller upfront fee for the contract clauses everyone else treats as worthless, since those clauses can end up worth more than the project itself. Once you have any leverage at all, use your own profits rather than someone else's money to buy full ownership of your next bet, even if the whole company is the collateral.

*David Senra on George Lucas, reading from George Lucas: A Life by Brian Jay Jones. Founders Podcast · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=X1xSXDDzlcM) · 119 min · published 2024-05-02.*

## Key lessons

- **Let a real betrayal become your permanent operating rule.** Twentieth Century Fox nickel and dimed Lucas through the original Star Wars shoot, denying him money he needed and overriding decisions on set. He vowed he would never again cede control to studio executives, and that single vow shaped every contract he signed for the rest of his career.
- **Read history as a deliberate source of working material, not entertainment.** Lucas was "addicted" to a series of biography books for young readers as a kid, and later said that reading, more than film school itself, gave him the storylines he drew on for Star Wars. He treated the whole history of his field, down to a cartoon like Scrooge McDuck's mix of hard work and cleverness, as source material to study and reuse.
- **Build your career-long peer network before you have any leverage.** At USC film school and through Francis Ford Coppola, Lucas became part of a small circle of young directors, including Steven Spielberg, who hired, financed, and traded profit points with each other for the next five decades. None of them could get a studio to take them seriously alone; together they eventually controlled the industry that had locked them out.
- **Spend only on what the audience actually experiences.** Lucas's first company, American Zoetrope, broke apart in part because his co-founder spent their limited funding on an antique pool table, an espresso machine, and rentable equipment that mostly got stolen, none of which changed what ended up on screen. Lucas's own mantra was blunt: stay small, be the best, don't lose any money.
- **Treat your first real disaster as the founding event of your real company.** The studio recut his debut film, THX 1138, against his wishes and called in the loan that ended his first company. He described the experience as watching someone "cutting the fingers off my baby," and founded Lucasfilm on independent terms almost immediately afterward, determined never to depend on a studio's money again.
- **Force the part of the craft you hate through raw discipline.** Lucas hated writing and had already been burned once paying someone else to do it for him. To finish Star Wars he sat at his desk from nine to five every day whether or not he wrote anything, marking a big X on a wall calendar only on days he hit five pages, and allowing himself to leave early only when he did.
- **Negotiate for the clause the other side thinks is worthless.** When Fox was the only studio willing to finance Star Wars, Lucas took a reduced director's fee in exchange for merchandising and sequel rights, clauses the studio treated as minor boilerplate. Merchandise alone would go on to earn roughly three times what the films made at the box office.
- **Reframe someone else's financing as a service you can buy directly.** When a studio executive argued he had earned his cut by "providing the money," Lucas pointed out that a studio itself just takes a loan from a bank and marks it up. He used that exact insight to finance The Empire Strikes Back himself, personally guaranteeing a bank loan against his own Star Wars profits and taking 77.5 percent ownership instead of the 40 percent he had held on the first film.
- **Trust your own judgment over a room full of talented people who disagree with you.** At the first screening of the finished Star Wars, his wife was in tears, certain it was a disaster, and a close friend openly mocked the dialogue; only Spielberg told him he had a hit. Lucas shipped the film he believed in anyway, and it earned more than seventy times its production budget at the box office alone.
