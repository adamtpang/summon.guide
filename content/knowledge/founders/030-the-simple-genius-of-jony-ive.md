# The Simple Genius of Jony Ive

> **Founder principle (Jony Ive):** Begin every product with its story and with what it should be, ignoring how things currently are or what an engineer says is possible. Then reduce relentlessly, asking of every part whether it is needed and whether one part can do the work of four, and pour fanatical care into the details a user may never consciously notice but will feel. This only works inside a company that puts design ahead of engineering, refuses consensus, and has a leader willing to overrule 38 reasons not to.

*David Senra on Jony Ive, reading from Jony Ive: The Genius Behind Apple's Greatest Products by Leander Kahney. Founders Podcast · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AXYbyl-5-2U) · 53 min · published 2026-06-10.*

## Key lessons

- **Start with the story of the product and the first touch.** Handed the flawed Newton, Ive began not with the hardware but by asking what the product's story was, since the label PDA was too slippery to relate to anyone's life. He spent unusual effort on the lid because opening it is the first interaction a user has, before the machine is even on. Throughout his career the starting point was what something should be, never what it currently was.
- **Respect the work by prototyping in volume.** As a student he filled his apartment with more than a hundred foam models for one project where classmates built half a dozen; at Apple a single home button or volume switch might get fifty models examined for edge, protrusion, material, and proportion. His father, who shaped design education across the UK, taught him to make first and refine as he went, and Ive said what he most despises in a product is a sense of carelessness.
- **Simplify, remove, reduce.** Where most companies add features with each release, his method was to go back to the beginning again and again, asking whether each part was necessary and whether one could replace several. The iPod design effort was framed explicitly as reduction, and the book concludes that if there is a single secret to his work it is following the simplification philosophy with unusual ruthlessness.
- **Figure out what you are good at and go where it is protected.** At his own firm Tangerine he estimated up to 90 percent of time went to selling services, and clients would strip the soul out of designs he loved. He concluded he was only interested in design and neither interested in nor good at building a business, so at 27 he took a full-time role at Apple because it let a designer focus on craft rather than day-to-day business.
- **Consensus produces middle-of-the-road products.** Pre-Jobs Apple ran product development through steering committees with three separate requirement documents per proposal and executive review on top, so four years could pass between a design brief and a shipped product. Ive's boss quit after sitting eight hours in meetings that needed thirty minutes, and Ive himself was preparing to leave. The lesson from that era: a business trying to build something for everyone rarely produces a spark of genius.
- **Put design ahead of engineering, and have a leader who fights for it.** When Jobs returned he cut the lineup to a two-by-two grid, laid off thousands, saved hundreds of millions in inventory, and redefined design as how a thing works rather than how it looks. Ive's costly recessed handle on the iMac existed only to give nervous users permission to touch the machine; engineers produced 38 reasons it could not be done and Jobs overruled them as CEO. Development time fell from three years to nine months and industrial design got the final say.
- **Compete on the attributes that cannot be measured.** The computer industry competed on speed and capacity because eight is obviously better than six, which Ive called comfortable but inhuman and cold. Apple deliberately competed on emotive, hard-to-measure qualities, ran no focus groups because imagining tomorrow is the designer's job, and ignored a hostile press: the iMac sold 278,000 units in six weeks, and the iPod, mocked at launch for its $499 price, went on to sell 450 million.
- **Refuse me-too products by asking what yours is better than.** Jobs dismissed netbooks because they were not better than anything, just cheaper laptops, and Ive proposed the tablet as the answer; by 2011 iPads outsold the entire netbook category, 63 million to 30 million. Apple targeted markets full of second-rate products, and Ive held that in a company built to innovate the real risk is playing it safe.
- **Hold the work above your own likability.** The team recruited designers who impressed them to the point of intimidation and stayed tiny, about sixteen people against a rival's thousand. When Ive asked Jobs to soften his critiques out of care for the team, Jobs called it vanity and a wish to be liked rather than devotion to the work, and Ive admitted he was right. He took personal blame for any weak design, spent most of his time in factories making designs manufacturable, and told suppliers to imagine an unlimited bucket of money so no idea died on cost before it was tried.
