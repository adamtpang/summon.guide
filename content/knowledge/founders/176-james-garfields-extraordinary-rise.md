# James Garfield's Extraordinary Rise

> **Founder principle (James A. Garfield):** Born in a one-room log cabin in Ohio and fatherless before he was two, Garfield went from canal hand to school janitor, from janitor to assistant professor in a year, and from there to college president, Union general, congressman and president, carried by a love of learning rather than a hunger for office. He refused to seek any promotion and was made president against his will. His death, caused far more by his doctors' unsterilized fingers and probes than by the assassin's bullet, shows how long a proven idea, Joseph Lister's antiseptic method, can sit unused while experts dismiss it.

## Key lessons

- **Treat education as the way out of poverty, without romanticizing the poverty.** Garfield's father died in the log cabin he had built, leaving his mother with four children and debts, and James had no shoes until he was four. Millard writes that for Garfield, education was salvation. He refused to make a virtue of his childhood, saying "let us never praise poverty," and told his mother nobody would care later what kind of coat he had studied in.
- **Let a near miss redirect your life.** At 16 he left home to work on a canal, taking the lowest job among rough men. He nearly drowned, caught malaria, and came home barely able to walk. His mother and brother had saved $17 for his schooling; he took the money and their advice, and Senra marks this as the turning point.
- **Pay for your education with whatever work is available.** Unable to afford tuition, Garfield persuaded the school to let him work as its janitor, sweeping floors, hauling wood, lighting fires and leaving the first lecture early to ring the bell. He never hid his poverty from classmates.
- **Compete against the room, and hardest against yourself.** His days ran from 5 a.m. to nearly midnight. When his effort flagged, he pushed on by imagining a classmate mastering the subject first, but his fiercest rival was himself. Within a year the school promoted him from janitor to assistant professor teaching literature, mathematics and ancient languages. At Williams College, facing better-prepared classmates, he sized them up and resolved that 37 of the 42 in his class would stand behind him within two months. By 26 he led the institution where he had once swept floors.
- **When outnumbered, look bigger than you are.** Sent, inexperienced and outgunned, to hold eastern Kentucky for the Union, he studied a map all night and split his small regiment into three groups that struck the Confederate commander, Humphrey Marshall, from three directions in turn. Marshall believed he was surrounded by a large army and retreated, leaving Kentucky in Union hands and making Garfield a brigadier general. Walking the field afterward, he saw young men he first took for sleeping soldiers were dead, and said something left him that never came back.
- **Refuse to campaign for your own advancement.** Garfield made it a rule never to seek an appointment or promotion. He took his congressional seat only when Lincoln asked, because the administration needed votes from men who knew the army. After seventeen years in Congress watching presidential ambition ruin colleagues, he resolved it would not seize him.
- **Ability can pull you into leadership you never asked for.** At the 1880 convention, Garfield gave a speech nominating John Sherman. When he asked the delegates what they wanted, someone shouted his own name, and support kept growing. He objected from the floor that nobody had his consent to use his name, but finished with 399 votes, more than enough, without ever agreeing to run. He called the presidency a bleak mountain he was obliged to climb, and wrote that there was a tone of sadness in the triumph.
- **Be a poor hater and a good fighter.** Facing party bosses like Roscoe Conkling, Garfield never took the bait and couldn't hold a grudge, yet he never walked away from a fight. He detested war, he wrote, but anyone who brought it to his door would find him at home. The Librarian of Congress counted him among the most diligent researchers he had known, and his house was full of books.
- **A proven idea can still lose to experts who won't adopt it.** Joseph Lister had shown that antiseptic surgery cut deaths, but American doctors laughed at germ theory. Frank Hamilton, who later treated Garfield, sat in Lister's audience and preferred leaving wounds open to the air. After Garfield was shot in a train station, one doctor put an unwashed finger in the wound on the station floor, and Willard Bliss pushed an unsterilized probe in so far it got stuck between broken ribs, then probed again with his finger. The bullet had missed his spine and vital organs, and the body had walled it off. Millard argues that X-rays and antiseptic care fifteen years later would have saved him, and that even with no treatment at all he would likely have lived. Garfield had once written that great ideas travel slowly.
- **Keep a record of your own mind.** Garfield wrote in a diary every night for 28 years, which is why Millard can let him speak for himself. One line Senra returns to: Garfield said he never passed a ragged boy without feeling he might owe him a salute, since nobody knew what that boy might become. Senra compares it to Jay-Z saying he would sign the kid writing rhymes at the breakfast table, because he had been that kid.
