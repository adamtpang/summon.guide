# Todd Graves and His $10 Billion Chicken Finger Dream

> **Founder principle (Todd Graves):** A limited menu gives a restaurant fewer things to perfect and makes ordering and preparation faster. Graves ties that focus to active attention to ingredients, locations, marketing, and the people doing the work. His experience also shows that commitment to a concept must be accompanied by management depth and enough financial room to survive disruption.

## Key lessons

- **Judge a menu addition by its effect on the whole operation.** Graves resisted pressure to add variety because each extra item could slow ordering, complicate preparation, and dilute quality. Even the chicken sandwich reused the existing chicken fingers. Before expanding an offer, trace the consequences for the customer promise and the work required to fulfill it consistently.
- **Remove small delays where they recur at high volume.** Graves reconsidered a promotional poster featuring himself and Snoop Dogg because customers paused to look at it in the drive-through. A few extra seconds for one car affected everyone waiting behind it. Examine the ordering environment as closely as the kitchen when speed is part of what the customer buys.
- **Choose a business whose routine work you actually enjoy.** Graves describes himself as a fry cook and cashier and says he likes working both the line and the drive-through. His early partner left after the second restaurant, and Graves attributes the difference partly to their feelings about that daily work. Assess your attraction to the operating job itself, beyond the appeal of owning a successful business.
- **Build management depth before assuming a second location will work like the first.** Graves calls the move from one restaurant to two his hardest stage because he had lived inside the first operation and had not learned to develop enough leaders. His own presence could no longer cover both sites. Test which decisions and standards other people can reliably carry before using the first location's performance as an expansion forecast.
- **Treat survival after excessive borrowing as a warning to redesign financing.** Graves expanded using personally guaranteed debt and the interval between collecting sales and paying wages, rent, and suppliers. When Hurricane Katrina shut twenty-one of twenty-eight restaurants, that arrangement nearly ended the business. He explicitly discourages copying his earlier borrowing choices; evaluate how the company meets its obligations if sales stop across several locations at once.
- **Reuse existing assets when they shorten the path to a working location.** Graves converted vacant double drive-through burger restaurants, negotiating to use the equipment already inside. That reduced the work and money needed before opening and collecting revenue. Look for an existing physical setup that suits your concept rather than assuming every new site requires construction from scratch.
- **Make appreciation specific and frequent.** Graves contrasts the yelling he experienced in earlier restaurant jobs with thanking crew members for a well-prepared item or an extra task. Raising Cane's established a department to recognize and reward that work. Design leadership routines around helping the people who serve customers, including acknowledgment of unglamorous tasks that keep the operation functioning.
- **Invent marketing partnerships that preserve the operating model.** When Post Malone wanted a Cane's location, Graves developed a specially designed restaurant with profit sharing while keeping its operation inside the company. The menu stayed the same, and the partnership was treated as marketing. Adapt how customers encounter the brand without casually handing away the control needed to maintain its product standards.
- **Expand a proven offer through better placement and accumulated capability.** Graves personally reviewed locations and described the Times Square restaurant producing substantially more sales than an average site with the same underlying menu. Later expansion also benefited from the operating knowledge built over many years. Ask whether location, execution, or organizational capacity offers more growth than adding another product category.
