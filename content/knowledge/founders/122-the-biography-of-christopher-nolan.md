# The Biography of Christopher Nolan

> **Founder principle (Christopher Nolan):** Go all in, obsessively, on one project at a time, and have the next one ready before the current one lands so momentum never stalls. Treat tight budgets and deadlines as the mechanism that protects your creative control, not a limitation to fight, and distill the heart of the work onto one page before production swallows you, so you can find your way back to it. When a big institution's default process doesn't fit how you work, build your own version yourself and hand it over finished.

*David Senra on Christopher Nolan, reading from The Nolan Variations: The Movies, Mysteries, and Marvels of Christopher Nolan by Tom Shone. Founders Podcast · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BV8ho9Yw4H4) · 49 min · published 2024-03-31.*

## Key lessons

- **Go all in, obsessively, on one project at a time.** Nolan says the only way he knows how to work is to "burrow into it," believing on every film that he's making the best film ever made, because that total immersion is what lets a film affect an audience the way it affected him.
- **Have your next project ready before the current one lands.** Before his first film was even released, he had already finished the screenplay that would become Memento, so when critics and distributors responded well and asked what was next, he could hand them a completed script instead of starting from zero.
- **Treat tight constraints as the mechanism that protects your creative control, not a limitation to fight.** He deliberately works faster and cheaper than a studio expects specifically so nobody has a reason to interfere: efficiency is, in his own words, how he keeps control, and a real deadline makes his creative process accelerate sharply.
- **When a big institution's default process doesn't fit how you work, build your own version and hand it over finished.** Rather than let Warner Bros. staff Batman Begins the standard way, he and his team developed the script and set designs on their own before telling the studio, then invited the executives to view the finished work rather than asking permission along the way.
- **Distill the heart of the project onto one page before production swallows you, then return to it on purpose.** Before shooting, he types up a single page describing what the film needs at its core, sets it aside, and comes back to it periodically, because once deep in production, with budget and location and casting pulling at every decision, it becomes hard to remember what you were originally trying to do.
- **Design the work around what you deliberately leave out, not just what you put in.** For Dunkirk, he stripped away the genre's usual scaffolding, no generals, no politics, no backstory, to leave only present-tense survival, the same instinct behind his description of his longtime composer as "a minimalist with maximalist production value," a description he recognized fit his own films too.
- **Make "keep working regardless of conditions" an explicit, stated policy for the whole team, not just a personal habit.** Despite the unpredictable weather that comes with insisting on real locations, his rule is to shoot no matter what and say so out loud, so the crew isn't checking the window each morning wondering whether the day is canceled: "just keep going."
- **Make the work a family affair instead of choosing between the relationship and the obsession.** His wife has produced his films since they met on the first day of college, and he brings his kids onto sets as they grow, treating the business itself as the way to have both a demanding creative life and a family life at once.
- **Protect your workflow choices even after the industry moves past them, because the tools were never the actual source of the work's value.** He kept shooting and cutting on physical film long after studios pushed for a fully digital pipeline, a stance that cost him arguments with post-production teams but never cost him the commercial success that followed.
