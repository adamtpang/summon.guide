# How To Be Rich by J. Paul Getty

> **Founder principle (J. Paul Getty):** Reduce running a business to a short list of explicit rules and apply them without exception: know your field completely, keep costs and debt under tight control, and stay personally involved in every detail rather than hiding behind executives and paperwork. Reward the people who think like owners, meet inevitable crises with a calm head instead of panic, and treat conformity to what everyone else is doing as one of the biggest threats to building real wealth.

*David Senra on J. Paul Getty, reading from How to Be Rich by J. Paul Getty. Founders Podcast · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nVWnDft6KW0) · 64 min · published 2024-07-10.*

## Key lessons

- **Reduce the game to a short, explicit list of rules and repeat them without exception.** Getty offered a list of rules he said every successful businessman he knew followed: know your field, watch costs constantly, avoid forced or blind expansion, stay personally on top of the business, keep improving the product, repay debt promptly, and always resolve guarantees in the customer's favor.
- **When a bigger rival tries to squeeze you, stop the bleeding first, then go straight to the top.** When major oil buyers boycotted his crude to force him into selling his leases cheap, Getty rented storage tanks to hold the oil rather than panic-sell, then got a personal meeting with Shell's company president and left with a commitment to buy 1.7 million barrels and start building him a pipeline the very next day.
- **Reward the employee who starts thinking like an owner, and watch for who reveals that trait.** When a site manager admitted he only caught cost savings because he personally owned the outcome, Getty moved him from a fixed salary to a share of the profits; a follow-up inspection sixty days later found nothing left to fix.
- **Small, unglamorous savings compound into large ones, so take them seriously.** Getty described a junior executive who found a half-cent-per-unit production shortcut worth more than $25,000 a year, twice the man's own salary, and used the story to argue that noticing costs at that scale is what actually separates real business talent from the rest.
- **Manage people with calm, applied psychology instead of threats or firing.** When several executives began arriving late, Getty did not issue warnings; he simply scheduled a daily meeting 45 minutes before the workday and the tardiness stopped on its own. When a valued employee took company lumber for a personal project, Getty billed him for its exact value instead of firing him, and word of the small charge traveled through the company by itself.
- **Get out from behind the desk and go to where the work actually happens.** Getty argued that an executive who never puts on overalls and stands in the plant, or a purchasing manager who has never touched the material he buys, cannot do the job as well as one who has, and he held up "no work about work, just work" as a standing complaint against paper-driven bureaucracy.
- **Stay calm when trouble hits, because trouble is the normal condition of business.** Getty passed along an older mentor's advice to treat setbacks the way a man overboard treats open water, keep your head and swim, because panic is what actually sinks you, and he added his own conclusion that a perfect business with no problems at all simply does not exist.
- **Own assets for the long term instead of trading them for a quick markup.** Getty told the story of a man who flipped a $4,000 oil lease for $8,000 in a single day and thought he had won, against his own decision to hold a similar lease and earn roughly $800,000 from it over twelve years, using the contrast to argue that the real money sits in ownership, not turnover.
- **Treat conformity as the actual danger to building wealth, not a safety net.** Getty warned that copying the herd only produces a mediocre, interchangeable competitor, and that a comfortable groove "may be safe" but wears down over time into a rut and then a grave; he told readers who said they just wanted security that the wish itself was a confession of weakness.
