# How Raymond Plank Made a Fortune in Big Oil's Scraps

> **Founder principle (Raymond Plank):** Find the edge that comes from what you already know, not what your industry considers standard expertise, and build the first product around it. Buy the assets, wells, and small companies that bigger players are too busy or too proud to want, watch every dollar of cost as a discipline, and when conditions change, change your entire strategy with them instead of staying loyal to what used to work.

*David Senra on Raymond Plank, reading from A Small Difference. Founders Podcast · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UpuinNtK6c4) · 38 min · published 2026-08-01.*

## Key lessons

- **Turn your outsider knowledge into the edge, not the industry's specialty.** Plank had almost no technical oil and gas knowledge when he founded Apache in 1954 with $250,000, but his financial and tax expertise, built from running a small accounting service for oil investors, became the company's first real product, a tax-efficient investment vehicle for wealthy clients.
- **Buy the scraps everyone else is too busy to want.** Apache built much of its later growth by purchasing older, partially depleted wells from major oil companies who were focused on larger discoveries and cost reduction, a strategy he described as being "like pigs following cows through a corn field."
- **Reverse a strategy completely, without nostalgia, when conditions change.** During a downturn from the late 1950s into the 1960s he diversified Apache into a roughly 58-company conglomerate spanning agriculture, plastics, telephone, and steel, then sold nearly everything once oil conditions improved and returned the company to being a focused energy business.
- **Control costs as a standing discipline, not a one-time cut.** From Apache's earliest days the culture was built around watching every dollar and avoiding redundant spending, captured in the internal line that the company's latest discovery was "better than it is," meaning results mattered more than appearances.
- **Overcommunicate through a crisis with a written daily record.** During a rough stretch fighting for control of the company, Plank wrote a daily report of his own activities for the board so it was never surprised, a practice he kept even after the crisis had passed.
- **Watch for an out-of-control ego in a partner before it costs you the company.** His cofounder's personal ambition for wealth and fame eventually led him to bug the company's own offices and boardroom, a conflict Plank later summarized as ego outstripping competence.
- **Buy the tool that multiplies your personal speed, but keep its cost honest.** Getting his own small plane was, in his words, a life-changing event that let him reach deals and meetings in hours instead of days, though he still charged the company only the equivalent of commercial airfare.
- **Stay in the game long enough for someone else's future technology to make your old assets valuable again.** Oil and gas leases Apache acquired between 1970 and 1977 were producing new, unexpectedly strong results decades later once outside drilling techniques improved, long after the company assumed those wells were fully tapped.
- **Study competitors directly and continuously as a discipline, not a one-time exercise.** He kept competitors' annual reports pinned to the wall behind his own desk specifically so he could study them.
