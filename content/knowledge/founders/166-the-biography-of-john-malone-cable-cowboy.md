# The Biography of John Malone (Cable Cowboy)

> **Founder principle (John Malone):** Malone took a large pay cut and a small salary in exchange for stock in a nearly bankrupt rural cable company, because the ownership stake, not the paycheck, was the real opportunity. He then built that company on leveraged cash flow and appreciating assets rather than reported profit, ran it with almost no overhead, and used its growing scale as leverage against suppliers and programmers alike, all while staying ready to walk away from the operating business the moment it stopped being the part of the job he actually wanted.

*David Senra on John Malone, reading from Cable Cowboy: John Malone and the Rise of the Modern Cable Business by Mark Robichaux. Founders Podcast · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iVeKq_lucNg) · 61 min · published 2023-10-17.*

## Key lessons

- **Take the pay cut when the ownership is real.** At 29, Malone turned down a job with a car and a much higher salary in New York to take roughly a third of that pay, plus stock bought partly with a personal loan, at a struggling rural cable operator carrying far more debt than revenue. The equity in a company he could actually help build mattered more to him than a bigger check from someone else's.
- **Build value through leveraged cash flow, and say so plainly to your own shareholders.** Malone ran TCI for years without reporting meaningful profit on purpose, using debt and depreciation to shelter cash flow and fund more acquisitions, and told investors who asked about quarterly earnings that they were "at the wrong meeting." He waited for a shareholder base that wanted appreciating assets over reported income, rather than changing his strategy to flatter the wrong audience.
- **Use your own scale as a weapon on both sides of the ledger.** As TCI grew, the same equipment parts that once cost eight cents fell to a fraction of a cent, and the per subscriber fees it paid for channels like CNN and HBO dropped far below what smaller operators paid for identical programming. Malone treated the compounding advantage of size as a deliberate strategy, not a side effect of growth.
- **Trade guaranteed distribution for equity in the thing you distribute.** When a founder needed money to launch a new cable channel, Malone would offer to fund part of it in exchange for an equity stake, closing some deals in under an hour, because TCI's existing reach into millions of homes let him give a new channel instant scale no one else could match. Controlling the pipe let him take a stake in the water flowing through it.
- **Stay frugal regardless of how large or wealthy the company gets.** TCI operated with minimal staff, saying flatly that "staff are people who second guess people," shared secretaries, no dedicated public relations function, and executives who doubled up in modest hotel rooms on the road, a deliberate contrast to the bigger East Coast cable operators it competed with. Malone treated overhead itself as a competitive weakness to avoid, not a sign of success.
- **Sit out the deal making at the top of a cycle.** Malone would deliberately abstain from acquisitions when he judged that competitors were overpaying, then wait for the same assets to come back to him at a steep discount once the market turned. Patience about when not to buy was as much a part of his strategy as the buying itself.
- **Decentralize operations, and keep capital and strategy at the center.** TCI ran as roughly six nearly autonomous divisions, each with its own accounting and engineering, while Malone concentrated the acquisition and capital decisions in his own hands. He compared a properly decentralized company to flying the most powerful fighter jet in the world, fast and responsive because authority sat where the information was.
- **Know whether you are a dealmaker or an operator, and refuse the wrong seat.** When IBM approached him about an executive role, Malone turned it down because he knew he had no interest in running a large corporation day to day. He restructured his own career, splitting TCI's cable systems from the equity stakes he actually wanted to keep, once he was honest with himself about which part of the job he loved and which part he did not.
