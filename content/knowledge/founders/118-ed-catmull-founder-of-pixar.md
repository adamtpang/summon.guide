# Ed Catmull (Founder of Pixar)

> **Founder principle (Ed Catmull):** Give people the resources and trust to take responsibility without asking permission, then protect their ideas with structures like the Brain Trust: candid, specific critique with zero authority to dictate the fix. Treat every early draft as expected to be bad and every setback as unavoidable R&D, not failure. When a decades-long mission is finally achieved, do not assume fulfillment follows automatically, assign yourself the next mission on purpose.

*David Senra on Ed Catmull, reading from Creativity, Inc. by Ed Catmull. Founders Podcast · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zlby8I0z4O4) · 66 min · published 2024-04-04.*

## Key lessons

- **Give people the authority to take responsibility without asking permission first.** Catmull borrowed this directly from W. Edwards Deming's manufacturing philosophy: any employee at any level who spots a problem should be expected to raise it, the way a factory worker could stop an assembly line. He built Pixar's management on this instead of walling quality control inside one "development department."
- **When you finally find where your real passion lives, bet the whole company on it.** Pixar spent years losing money trying to sell hardware, the Pixar Image Computer among it, before abandoning that path entirely for computer animation, the thing the team had wanted to do from the outset. The pivot cost more than a third of the staff in layoffs, and it was the only bet that ever worked.
- **Build a critique structure with full authority to surface problems and zero authority to dictate the fix.** Pixar's Brain Trust gathers five to thirty people to review a film in progress and give blunt, specific notes, but the director is never obligated to adopt any of them. The point is to force the true cause of a problem into the open; the remedy has to come from the filmmaker who owns the work.
- **Separate the person from the idea, on both sides of a critique.** Catmull's rule, "you are not your idea," only works because Pixar also assumes every movie is bad in its first version, "all of our movies suck" at first, so a hard note about the current draft isn't an attack on the person who made it, it's the expected first step of the process.
- **When you're stuck choosing between two options, get to the wrong answer fast rather than deliberate.** Filmmaker Andrew Stanton's rule: if you're unsure which of two hills to attack, hurry up and pick one; if it's the wrong hill, turn around and take the other. The only unacceptable move is running back and forth between them.
- **Protect your people from their own willingness to destroy themselves for the work.** During the crunch to fix Toy Story 2, an exhausted employee nearly left his baby in the car at daycare drop off. Catmull vowed never to run a production that way again and made it his explicit job to guard ambitious, workaholic employees from their own pursuit of excellence, even when they wanted to keep pushing.
- **Refuse to trade quality for a faster, more profitable version of the product.** Disney wanted Toy Story 2 to skip theaters and go straight to video, a lower bar that would have made money faster on VHS and DVD sales alone. Catmull fought to hold it to full theatrical quality instead, on the principle that "quality is the best business plan" and that anything carrying the company's name has to be excellent.
- **A finished mission leaves a void; assign yourself the next one on purpose instead of expecting satisfaction to follow success.** After Toy Story became a hit and Pixar's IPO made him and Steve Jobs wealthy, achieving a goal Catmull had chased for over twenty years, he felt hollow rather than fulfilled. His deliberate fix was to name a new mission for himself: building a sustainable creative culture that could survive Pixar's own success.
