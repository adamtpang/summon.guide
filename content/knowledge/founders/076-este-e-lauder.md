# Estée Lauder

> **Founder principle (Estée Lauder):** Treat persistence as the one non-negotiable trait of a founder, ahead of talent or intelligence, and never accept a first no as final. Sell through direct, hands-on demonstration and free samples rather than advertising, because a woman who tries a product and sees the result becomes a customer for decades, not a single sale. Build the obsession into a family business so the same drive that built the company also protects it.

*David Senra on Estée Lauder, reading from Estée: A Success Story by Estée Lauder. Founders Podcast · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PLWjOO_1SSU) · 55 min · published 2024-09-04.*

## Key lessons

- **Define success as persistence, not talent.** Estée Lauder names persistence as the real ingredient behind a successful entrepreneur, ahead of talent or intelligence, and proved it by ignoring her own accountant and lawyer when they took her to dinner specifically to beg her not to commit her savings to cosmetics full time.
- **Turn a lifelong private obsession into the business rather than manufacturing one.** She was giving free makeovers and touching faces at eight years old, apprenticed under her Uncle John's homemade cream formulas as a girl, and ran the business as an unpaid hobby for roughly a decade and a half before formally founding the company at 38.
- **Sell through demonstration, never through the pitch alone.** When a salon owner asked to simply keep her samples and try them later, Lauder refused to leave without a live demonstration on the spot, and that insistence won her the small counter concession that became her first real business.
- **Never let a customer leave empty-handed.** She built the business around giving a sample gift with every visit, even without a purchase, reasoning that a woman who tried a product at home and saw the result would be faithful forever, which she treated as more valuable than any single sale.
- **Never underestimate a customer by appearance.** At a Texas counter opening a staff member urged her not to bother with a barefoot customer who seemed unable to buy, so Lauder gave her the full demonstration anyway; the woman bought two of everything used on her face, and her relatives returned the next day.
- **Push past rejection with outside proof, not repeated asking alone.** Turned away by the Harrods buyer on her first visit to London, she spent months getting beauty editors to write about her products before she had any UK counter at all, then returned across three trips over about a year and a half until the same buyer finally granted her a small spot.
- **Make word of mouth the advertising budget you don't have yet.** With no advertising budget in the early years, she relied on what she called 'tell a woman,' turning individual customers into unpaid evangelists so effectively that she says she was still receiving letters from women she had personally made up decades earlier.
- **Reframe the product so the customer can buy it for herself, guilt-free.** Noticing that perfume was traditionally a gift a man bought a woman, she launched a bath oil, Youth Dew, that doubled as a skin perfume a woman could buy for herself the way she bought a lipstick, and it grew from fifty thousand dollars of business in 1953 to more than 150 million dollars by 1984.
- **Bring family into the business so the same obsession that built it doesn't destroy it.** After her single-minded focus on the business contributed to a divorce from her husband Joe in 1939, she remarried him, brought him into the company, and sent her son Leonard copies of every business letter and financial statement while he was still in college so he would be ready to help run it.
