# How Henry Singleton Worked

> **Founder principle — Henry Singleton:** Treat the CEO role as capital allocation. Give excellent operators autonomy, measure the cash economics honestly, and keep headquarters tiny so the person at the top can move capital wherever it creates the most durable value per share. A tactic is never sacred: issue richly valued stock to acquire businesses, stop when that currency becomes cheap, then repurchase aggressively when the market offers your own company at a discount.

## Key lessons

- **The chief executive has two jobs: run operations and allocate capital.** Most leaders rise through an operating specialty and keep spending their attention there. Singleton built Teledyne so strong divisional leaders handled operations while he concentrated on the rarer job: deciding whether the next dollar belonged in an acquisition, an existing business, a security, debt reduction, or the company's own shares.
- **Optimize durable value per share, not the appearance of smooth earnings.** Teledyne emphasized cash generation and asset stewardship over quarterly cosmetics. Its internal return measure blended profit and cash flow, making it harder for a unit to manufacture attractive accounting results by tying up more working capital.
- **Price determines the right tactic.** During the conglomerate boom, Singleton used expensive Teledyne shares to buy more than a hundred businesses. When that currency stopped being advantageous, he stopped issuing it. Later, when Teledyne was undervalued, he reversed direction and bought back more than 90 percent of its outstanding shares. The principle stayed fixed; the mechanism changed with price.
- **Decentralize decisions, centralize standards and capital.** A headquarters of fewer than fifty people sat above tens of thousands of employees. Operating authority lived close to the work, while Singleton selected leaders, taught the economic philosophy personally, and expected managers to know the exact numbers of their businesses.
- **Protect the CEO's unstructured attention.** Singleton avoided a fixed calendar of recurring operational duties. That freedom let him study the whole system and work on the highest-value problem of the moment instead of allowing meetings and routines to define the job.
- **Use the market offering the better bargain.** When entire companies became too expensive, Teledyne bought minority stakes in public businesses instead. Singleton concentrated in a few companies he understood, paid conservative prices, and avoided paying a control premium merely for the status of owning everything.
- **Prefer permanent gains to temporary applause.** He rejected transactions that could flatter near-term results while weakening the long-term whole. Patient capital allocation, willingness to cut a failed operation, and indifference to conventional fashion gave compounding room to work.
- **Independent thinking is a system, not a pose.** Singleton combined first-principles economics, numerical command, secrecy about strategy, and the flexibility to reverse himself. His edge was not reflexive contrarianism; it was refusing to outsource judgment to whatever other chief executives happened to be doing.
