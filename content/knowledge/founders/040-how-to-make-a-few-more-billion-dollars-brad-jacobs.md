# How To Make A Few MORE Billion Dollars: Brad Jacobs

> **Founder principle (Brad Jacobs):** Treat capital allocation as a discipline of matching the right investor to the right stage, integrate every acquisition from the day the deal is signed instead of the day it closes, and give every line item a single accountable owner. Underneath all of it, treat your own mind the way you treat the business: something you actively manage with named, repeatable tools, because a founder's inner state is a real competitive asset, not a private problem to hide.

*David Senra on Brad Jacobs, reading from How to Make a Few More Billion Dollars by Brad Jacobs. Founders Podcast · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=2c4LWQpuw9s) · 43 min · published 2025-12-29.*

## Key lessons

- **Match your funding source to the behavior it will actually show under pressure, not its pitch.** He ranks family offices as fast and founder friendly, private equity as fair weather and self interested despite the loyalty they promise, and sovereign wealth funds and pension plans as slow to commit but sticky once they do, while hedge funds flip the stock as soon as the price moves regardless of what they said about being long term. Choose investors for how they behave when things get hard, not for how they describe themselves beforehand.
- **Being a public company is a discipline mechanism, not just a source of funding.** He argues the quarterly earnings cycle forces a management team to stay sharp because analysts model and challenge the numbers in public every ninety days, and a strong public profile has itself brought him acquisitions and shareholders he never went looking for. Treat outside scrutiny as a forcing function that improves decisions rather than only as a cost to minimize.
- **Negotiate access to an acquired company before the deal even closes.** Most acquirers wait until closing to start integration; he negotiates unrestricted access to employees the moment an agreement is signed, then runs town halls and front line interviews immediately so the work starts on day one instead of months later. Ask direct operating questions right away: what should be stopped immediately, where are we underinvesting, and what annoys our customers.
- **Give every line item in an integration a single named, accountable owner.** He calls this a throat to choke: one person personally responsible for one outcome by one firm completion date, applied across the thousands of specific items a single acquisition can generate. Diffuse ownership is where integration plans quietly die; concentrated ownership is how they actually finish.
- **Design the org chart around roles and decision rights, not the people you happened to inherit.** His test for a good chart is that an outsider can understand the structure at a glance, and he will leave a seat empty rather than fill it with the wrong person, because an empty seat does less damage than a poor fit. He also cuts the "nice to have" layer of management first, since it is the largest source of unnecessary headcount and problems most often start at the top, not on the front line.
- **Ask three simple questions after every acquisition instead of a long survey.** What's working really well, what needs fixing, and what's your single best idea to improve the company, sent immediately after a deal closes to signal that leadership wants the truth and intends to act on it. Simplicity produces faster, more honest answers than an exhaustive questionnaire does.
- **Treat your own mindset as a trained skill, not a fixed trait.** He meditates twice daily without exception and keeps a rotating set of five specific practices, rational emotive behavioral therapy, cognitive behavioral therapy, dialectical behavioral therapy, positive psychology, and mindfulness, for getting back to center whenever he is knocked off it. Build a repeatable, named process for recovering your own composure instead of hoping you simply handle the next crisis well.
- **Reframe a perfectionist or irrational thought into a rational one in the moment.** He learned this from Albert Ellis's work: substitute a rigid demand for approval with a preference you can still be at peace without, and do this deliberately each time a distorted thought appears rather than letting it run unchallenged. Catching and rewriting one sentence in your head is a repeatable skill, not a fixed personality trait.
- **A generative motivation compounds; a negative one runs out.** He describes moving from a fuel source built on avoiding poverty or proving doubters wrong, which depletes once the goal is met, to one where solving problems and building things generates its own energy for whatever comes next. Check whether your own drive gets replenished by the work itself or only by hitting external, exhaustible targets.
