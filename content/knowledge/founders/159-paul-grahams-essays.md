# Paul Graham's Essays

> **Founder principle (Paul Graham):** Test whether you love a kind of work by whether you keep producing it even when nobody is making you, not by how it feels in any given moment. Once you're building something, determination (willfulness balanced by discipline and aimed by ambition) predicts survival better than intelligence does, and the state of the economy is a rounding error next to the quality of the founders. Startups do not take off on their own; founders have to make them take off, one unscalable, manual action at a time.

## Key lessons

- **Test whether you actually love a kind of work by whether you keep producing it.** If you claim you want to write novels but keep a safe day job, ask whether you are actually writing pages, however bad, in your off hours; always producing pushes you toward what you truly want to work on and away from what you merely think you're supposed to want.
- **A task that feels like work to everyone else but doesn't feel like work to you is a direct signal of where you're well suited.** Programmers who tolerate, even enjoy, debugging tend to like programming generally, given how much of the job debugging actually is; the stranger your tastes look to other people, the stronger the evidence that they point at your real aptitude.
- **Startups rarely die from one dramatic event, they die from demoralization, so learn to recognize the tell.** Any sentence where a founder explains that a co-founder is drifting off to something else but that they personally plan to keep the startup going means the decision to quit has already been made; expect that almost nothing works on the first try and treat that as normal rather than proof of failure.
- **Judge your company's survival odds by the founders in the mirror, not by headlines about the economy.** The state of the economy is a rounding error next to the quality of the people running the company; make the business recession-proof by doing what you should always do anyway, run it as cheaply as possible, since the cheaper it is to operate, the harder it is to kill.
- **Be relentlessly resourceful rather than merely relentless.** The opposite of a good founder is someone who lets the world have its way with them instead of finding a new way through; because obstacles in any genuinely interesting field are novel, you cannot just push through them by force, you have to keep inventing new approaches.
- **Determination outpredicts intelligence, and it breaks down into willfulness balanced by discipline and aimed by ambition.** Willfulness without discipline is just self-indulgence, and the stronger your will, the more dangerous a lack of discipline becomes; deliberately put yourself near other ambitious people, since most ambitious people are starved for ambitious peers and grow measurably once they find them.
- **Choose co-founders on character and commitment, not raw ability, because that relationship will become more intense than a marriage and everything else is built on it.** Persistence then dissolves obstacles that looked entirely outside your control more reliably than raw intelligence does, so treat your original idea as a hypothesis to keep testing and revising, never as a fixed blueprint.
- **A founder who is hard to talk to and a founder whose startup is quietly failing usually share the same underlying defect: a shortage of resourcefulness.** It shows up as failing to chase down the implications of a lead, a piece of user feedback, or a warning, so measure yourself by whether you follow every thread all the way out, not by how articulate you sound.
- **Startups take off because founders physically make them take off, so do the unscalable thing first.** Recruit your first users one at a time by hand, even building the product for them yourself if that's what it takes, and consider deliberately narrowing your first market so a small population can reach critical mass rather than betting everything on a big public launch.
