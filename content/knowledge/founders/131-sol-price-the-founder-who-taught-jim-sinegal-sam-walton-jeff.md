# Sol Price: The Founder Who Taught Jim Sinegal, Sam Walton, Jeff Bezos, and Bernie Marcus

> **Founder principle (Sol Price):** Build the business as a fiduciary for the customer rather than a markup machine. Make your margin from a flat membership fee, stock fewer items at sharper prices, teach employees the reasoning behind their work instead of handing them a manual, and defend the customer's interest even when it costs you money, a lawsuit, or your own standing in your industry.

*David Senra on Sol Price, reading from Sol Price: Retail Revolutionary and Social Innovator by Robert Price. Founders Podcast · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1ENZ3Mq2NXw) · 60 min · published 2024-03-22.*

## Key lessons

- **Teach the reasoning behind the job instead of writing a manual for it.** Price distrusted procedure manuals as a substitute for real thinking and liked to say "you train an animal, you teach a person," building what he called the alter ego concept: since no owner can personally do every job, he has to teach each employee to understand why the work matters so they act as he would. Jim Sinegal, who started at FedMart at 18 and went on to found Costco, said he did not just learn a lot from Price, he learned everything he knows.
- **Sell fewer items, not more, once you understand what Price called the intelligent loss of sales.** Conventional retailers stocked every size of a product to capture every possible customer, but Price found that "customer demand is most sensitive to price, not selection," so stocking only the best value size of an item, like the large can of a household oil sold in three sizes, raised total sales even though a few customers walked away. Fewer items also cut labor across the entire supply chain, since payroll was roughly 80 percent of a retailer's cost, and in his own accounting, handling 4,500 items cost far less than handling 50,000.
- **Make your money on a flat membership fee, not on marking up goods, so your incentives align with the customer's instead of against it.** Price marked goods up by roughly 12 percent over cost, far below the 36 to 50 percent common elsewhere, and said "our first duty is to our customers," ranking employees second and stockholders third. Price Club's own hot dog and soda combo, priced at a dollar fifty to prove the point, had still barely moved in price decades later.
- **Set one fair wage policy and apply it everywhere, without recalculating for what the local market would tolerate.** FedMart paid a dollar an hour in San Diego and Phoenix, twice the fifty cents competitors were paying, simply because Price thought it was the right thing to do, and when the company opened in the lower cost San Antonio market, he applied the same dollar wage there too, reasoning that those employees worked just as hard as anyone else. The payoff was durable: decades later, the same philosophy at Costco produced 22,000 applications for 200 open positions.
- **Do the right thing even when it costs you professionally, whether that means breaking an unfair rule or refusing a profitable but unethical term.** Price built an entire pharmacy specifically to discount prescription drugs below the price floors set by so called fair trade laws, and the pharmacist he hired for it was expelled from his professional associations and received death threats for it. When a lender's mortgage agreement required FedMart to maintain segregated bathrooms by race, Price simply refused to sign until the clause was removed, and the lender backed down.
- **Know your own weakness honestly, and hand execution to whoever is actually best at it.** Price considered himself a strong idea generator but a weak operator, and when it came time to sell Price Club he specifically steered the deal toward Jim Sinegal at Costco over other bidders because he judged Sinegal to be the best operator he had encountered, a decision that produced the merged company operating today.
- **Cap your downside before you know whether a new idea will actually work.** When Price opened his first store with 50,000 dollars raised from a handful of investors, he negotiated a ten year lease but insisted on the right to cancel it after just one year if the concept failed to catch on, protecting the fledgling business from a long term commitment to an unproven idea.
- **A partner's true character shows up over years, not during a friendly negotiation.** A European retailer who wined, dined, and charmed Price's team during merger talks later bought a controlling stake in FedMart, and revealed his real temperament for the first time only after the deal closed, launching into a ninety minute tirade at the very first board meeting that ended with Price fired from the company he had founded.
- **Protect the focus that made you successful, especially after competitors copy your idea.** Price shared his retail concepts openly, and both Sam Walton and Bernie Marcus have credited him directly for ideas behind Walmart, Sam's Club, and Home Depot, but Price Club squandered its seven year head start by diverting money and attention into real estate development instead of its core warehouse business, letting faster, more focused operators at Costco and Sam's Club overtake it.
