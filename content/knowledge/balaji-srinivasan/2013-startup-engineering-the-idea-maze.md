## Key lessons

- Situation: these are his written notes for the Market Research, Wireframing, and Design lecture of Startup Engineering, the online Stanford course he taught in 2013.
- He treats an idea as a state machine: idea, mockup, prototype, program, product, business, profits. Most ideas die at an early stage, and he suggests keeping one document listing every idea by stage.
- He walks through three views of what matters most (the idea, the execution, the market) and concludes all of them matter; the point of saying execution beats ideas is to stop novices overvaluing a napkin sketch or a patent.
- The idea maze: users only see the one path a successful company took, not the paths it rejected or the dead companies in the pits. A good founder knows the history of the industry, the players, the past casualties and the technologies that could move the walls.
- His definition of a good idea is "a detailed path through the maze", and the test is whether you can diagram the decision tree and explain why your plan beats the ten past companies that failed and the twenty competitors still in the maze.
- A rule of thumb from the notes: if someone can steal your idea just by hearing it, you probably do not have something defensible yet.
- Transferable lesson: before building, write down who tried this before, why each one failed, and what has changed (a new technology, a competitor's blind spot) that opens a door for you now.
