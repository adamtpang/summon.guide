# How To Price For B2B | Startup School

> **Key principle:** Most first-time B2B founders freeze when asked for a price and default to a consumer-software number ($19-$49/month) that badly undervalues what they're selling. Instead, build a written "value equation" with your champion inside the customer's company (what cost, time, or revenue impact the product delivers, quantified in real dollars) and price at roughly a third of that value.

*Tom (YC group partner) on how to price for B2B, Y Combinator Startup School · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4hjiRmgmHiU) · 18 min.*

## Key lessons

- The core method is the "value equation": sit down with your champion (the internal advocate at the customer) and write out, step by step, exactly what cost savings, time savings, or revenue increase your product will deliver, backed by real numbers they can defend to their own CFO. A worked example: a 100-person customer service team costing $10M fully loaded, where a tool eliminating 20% of query time represents $2M of value.
- Price at roughly 25-50% of the quantified value delivered, letting the customer keep the majority (in the example, the customer nets about $1.3M of the $2M while the vendor charges around $700K); this framing alone is described as capturing 80-90% of the pricing decision.
- The value equation doubles as your pilot's success criteria: run a scoped trial with a subset of the customer's team, measure whether the promised savings materialize, and adjust pricing based on the real result rather than the original guess.
- Never start from cost-plus pricing; cost should only ever function as a floor, not an anchor. Aim for 80-90% software gross margins, and treat any AI/cloud infrastructure credits as a real future cash cost rather than assuming they'll last forever, since building margin assumptions on temporary free credits sets up a painful correction later.
- Facing a competitor who undercuts on price is a trap best avoided by differentiating on functionality or vertical focus rather than racing to the bottom; the airline industry (commodity seats, ~2.7% average net margin) is cited as the cautionary example of what happens to an entire industry once price becomes the only lever.
- Practical structuring tips: mirror how the customer's industry is already used to paying (flat fee, per-seat, usage-based) rather than inventing a novel structure, favor committed monthly/annual recurring revenue over pure usage-based pricing (which can fall off a cliff during a downturn), and use the champion's individual signing authority (e.g., "can approve up to $15K without escalation") as a practical ceiling for smaller pilot deals.
- Gate advanced compliance and security features (SOC 2 reports, SSO, audit logs, data residency) behind a separate "Enterprise" tier rather than picking one universal price; this lets a company avoid simultaneously overpricing small customers and underpricing large ones with a single public number.
- A useful rule of thumb ties pricing to your sales model: expect roughly a 5:1 ratio between a salesperson's total compensation and the new annual recurring revenue they close, meaning contract size dictates whether you need a true outbound account executive (closing a few large deals) or something closer to an inbound order-taker (closing dozens of small deals monthly). If you truly can't determine a price, start with a comparable number, then raise it by roughly 50% with each new prospect until you start losing more than about 25% of deals on price alone, that's your real ceiling.
