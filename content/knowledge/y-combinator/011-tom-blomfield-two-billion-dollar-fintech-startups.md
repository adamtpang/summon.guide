# Tom Blomfield: How I Created Two Billion-Dollar Fintech Startups

> **Key principle:** Blomfield's first YC company was a classic tar-pit idea that got reset into GoCardless after a humbling YC batch; his second, Monzo, grew to a million customers on word of mouth alone by following YC fundamentals (launch fast, talk to users, do things that don't scale). Both stories show that surviving a company at real scale costs something personal, and recognizing that cost is part of the job.

*Tom Blomfield (GoCardless and Monzo co-founder, now a YC group partner) on building two billion-dollar fintech startups, Y Combinator · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QKPgBAnbc10) · 14 min.*

## Key lessons

- Blomfield's first YC company was a UK bill-splitting app, a classic "tar pit" idea; getting into the 2011 YC batch was a humbling reset (he describes his London team as having been "play acting" at running a startup) that forced a pivot into a stronger B2B payments idea, which became GoCardless.
- GoCardless grew slowly for its first year or two through sheer persistence. Blomfield ultimately left after about three years, not because it was failing, but because the problem never became personally compelling enough to commit another five to ten years to it, a distinction he draws between loving a team and loving the problem.
- Monzo (started 2015) shipped a working prototype on a prepaid debit card in three to four months, explicitly applying earlier YC lessons, and grew from zero to one million customers in about two and a half years on word of mouth alone, with no paid advertising, helped by a distinctive card color that prompted strangers to ask about it in public.
- Choosing an unusually ambitious mission (rebuilding a retail bank from scratch) became a real recruiting and press advantage: the scale of the vision attracted more ambitious talent and disproportionate media attention, a counterintuitive benefit of picking a harder problem rather than a safer one.
- Real infrastructure stakes brought real personal cost: as Monzo processed tens of billions of pounds a year, brief outages disrupted customers' actual paychecks, anti-fraud account closures triggered serious backlash including personal threats, and growth eventually flipped the press from favorable to openly hostile.
- In March 2020, a roughly nine-figure funding round that had taken nine months to arrange collapsed 72 hours before closing as COVID lockdowns hit and revenue dropped by half, forcing the company to seriously prepare a wind-down while simultaneously fighting to survive.
- Blomfield stepped down as CEO after reaching what he describes as genuinely "not functioning anymore," a decision he frames as necessary for personal survival even though letting go of a company entangled with his identity was extremely difficult; under new leadership Monzo later reached roughly 8 million UK customers and became profitable.
- After a recovery year, Blomfield found he loved advising and investing in founders (eventually making around 75 angel investments in nine months), which led directly to rejoining YC as a partner, closing the loop from being a funded founder himself.
