# Kat Mañalac - How to Launch (Again and Again)

> **Key principle:** Most founders treat launching as a single, high-stakes moment they get one shot at, spending months perfecting every detail before shipping. The better model is to launch continuously and repeatedly across many small channels, since each launch is really a cheap experiment that tells you whether you've found the right users.

*Kat Mañalac (YC partner, head of outreach) on launching again and again, Y Combinator Startup School · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3xU050kMbHM) · 20 min.*

## Key lessons

- Launching is reframed as a continuous, repeatable activity, not a single moment: a team that spent six months perfecting every pixel before its first launch risks running out of runway before it gets a second chance, whereas founders who launch early and often get to practice their pitch and see real reactions long before the product is finished.
- A "silent launch" (a simple landing page with a name, one-line description, and a call to action like "subscribe for updates") requires almost no effort and is often skipped; tools like Product Hunt's "Ship" feature exist specifically to make this trivially easy to set up in a weekend.
- Do a friends-and-family launch quickly once you have an MVP, but don't linger there: family and friends aren't your target user and their feedback is often too kind to be useful (early Reddit was first shared only among a small group of fellow YC founders before going wider).
- "Launching to strangers" in person can validate a problem before any product exists: Lugg's founders staked out an IKEA parking lot, approached struggling shoppers directly with the pitch, and manually fulfilled the "app" by personally driving a truck over, which confirmed the problem was a genuine "hair on fire" need before they built out the backend.
- Online communities (Hacker News, Product Hunt, niche subreddits, and YC's internal community forums) are a favorite launch channel; the key advice is to write like a human, not a marketing robot, share genuinely interesting insights from talking to users, and explicitly invite questions rather than posting polished corporate copy, since community moderators report that unglamorous B2B and even niche personal-mission products (like a formerly-incarcerated jobs platform) often outperform flashy consumer apps when the founder engages authentically.
- Build viral mechanics into "request for access" launches: Magic's early text-based concierge service went from a basic friends-and-family link to 40,000 signups over one weekend after a friend shared it on Reddit and Hacker News, and used a waitlist with line-skipping incentives (tweet about it to jump the queue), a technique also used by Superhuman via referral-based access.
- New feature and product launches deserve the same repeated-launch treatment as a company's first launch: Stripe engages its community directly on Hacker News every time it ships something new, and Glossier runs a disciplined six-to-eight-week product cadence where every release hits every launch channel (community, social, press, advertising) again, creating a constant public drumbeat.
- Start building your own audience before you have a product: one founder built a personal email list from every person he'd ever discussed his startup idea with, however casually, and when a major press hit landed, that list (plus asks to investors and friends to share it themselves) produced outsized reach, proof that pre-built personal distribution compounds over time.
