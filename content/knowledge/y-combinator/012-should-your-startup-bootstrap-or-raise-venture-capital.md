# Should Your Startup Bootstrap or Raise Venture Capital?

> **Key principle:** Well under 1% of businesses started each year are venture-funded, and VC as a product only makes sense for the rare business that could plausibly return 100x to 1000x. The bootstrap-vs-VC "debate" is treated as largely manufactured online engagement, not a real values disagreement: raise if your business's growth math genuinely requires and supports it, otherwise don't.

*Dalton Caldwell and Michael Seibel on bootstrapping versus raising venture capital, Y Combinator · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=D81y-kh11oI) · 14 min.*

## Key lessons

- The vast majority of businesses started every year, by their estimate well under 1%, are venture-funded; venture capital as a product only makes sense for a company that could plausibly return 100x to 1000x on the investment, so forcing VC funding into a business that can't grow that large is bad for everyone involved.
- There is no moral dimension to the bootstrap-vs-VC choice; it's purely a fit question. The framing of it as an identity-driven online debate is described as largely manufactured engagement bait rather than a genuine disagreement inside the startup industry.
- Concrete counterexample to "you must raise to win": a friend's non-VC-backed software product generated $30,000-$50,000 a month in revenue on roughly 7-10 hours a month of maintenance work while its founder traveled, married, and had kids, a comparison used to needle the panelists' own much harder VC-funded grind at the time.
- No trillion-dollar software company has ever been built entirely without venture capital, so VC is realistically the only mechanism for an idea (Google is used as the example) that mathematically requires tens of millions of dollars of upfront infrastructure spend with no other viable financing path.
- Venture capital is explicitly a plain business transaction, not a values statement: an investor gives money now for a credible shot at much more money later, and a "no" from an investor is a comment on that specific business's return math, not a judgment on the founder's character or the users being served.
- Chasing VC funding as a status marker, because it's the "default" path or gets attention online, without genuinely wanting to build a company at that scale, is called out as a bad reason that predicts a bad experience either way, on both the bootstrap and VC-funded sides.
- The decision is explicitly reversible: a founder can bootstrap first and still raise venture money later once the product is working and clearly shows the growth profile investors look for; starting bootstrapped forecloses nothing about future fundraising.
- Their advice on viral "VCs funded something dumb" stories: don't let a single, deliberately cherry-picked bad funding decision convince you the entire funding system, or your own rejected pitch, is fundamentally broken or unfair.
