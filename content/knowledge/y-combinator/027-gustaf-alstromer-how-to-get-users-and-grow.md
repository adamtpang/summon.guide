# Gustaf Alstromer - How to Get Users and Grow

> **Key principle:** Applying growth tactics before you have real product-market fit is actively dangerous: it produces a graph that spikes and then crashes back to zero once the artificial fuel runs out. First prove retention with a simple cohort chart (does usage of your core value metric flatten out over time, the way Netflix retains 70% of payers after a year, versus a company that decays toward zero), and only then build a real growth function.

*Gustaf Alströmer (early Airbnb growth team member, YC group partner) on how to get users and grow, Y Combinator Startup School · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=T9ikpoF2GH0) · 64 min.*

## Key lessons

- Measure product-market fit with a two-step method: pick the one metric that represents real value delivered (bookings for Airbnb, payroll runs for Gusto, transactions for Stripe), then chart repeat usage of that metric in a cohort retention curve. A curve that keeps declining toward zero (a real example: 50% retention at one month, 10% by 24 months) is not clear product-market fit; a curve that flattens out at a real floor (Shopify's or Netflix's) is.
- Facebook's growth history is used to show how intentional, data-driven growth work compounds: early data scientists forecasted roughly 400 million users by 2015 based on then-current trends, and the company blew past that by deliberately solving new bottlenecks as they emerged, translating the product into hundreds of languages once English-speaking growth saturated, rebuilding around mobile once desktop usage capped out, and partnering with carriers to get more of the world online at all, eventually reaching over 2 billion users.
- Growth work splits into two legitimate categories for an early-stage company: product growth/growth engineering (funnel and conversion-rate optimization done by engineers, PMs, and data scientists working directly on the product) and performance marketing (Google, Facebook, Instagram, YouTube ad spend); brand marketing is explicitly discouraged for early-stage startups because its impact is nearly impossible to measure.
- Every product is a funnel: authentication, onboarding, and purchase/conversion are named as the highest-leverage areas for improvement, and even something as "solved" as sign-up flow deserves constant experimentation (Airbnb spent years optimizing just login and account creation).
- Identify which of five growth-channel patterns fits your product before investing: infrequent, high-consideration purchases point to SEO/paid search (people go to Google); frequent daily-use products point toward direct/organic habit instead; products that get more valuable with more users point toward virality/referrals; products with an enumerable target list (e.g., "every dentist's office in California") point toward direct sales; and only products with proven high lifetime value per customer should use paid acquisition (Facebook/Google ads), since paying to acquire customers before you have real revenue is called out as a common founder mistake.
- Referral programs are "engineered word of mouth": Airbnb's referral emails were the product of dozens of iterated experiments (personalizing the subject line with the referrer's name, adding urgency with a deadline, framing signup as an exclusive "accept invitation" rather than a generic signup, and including the referrer's photo and tenure as social proof), and even small wording changes (one experiment: renaming a button from a generic label to a personalized invitation phrase) produced double-digit percentage lifts in conversion.
- SEO still matters because people still start on Google; the two levers are on-page optimization (writing in indexable, non-JavaScript-hidden text, targeting a deliberate list of researched keywords by search volume and competition) and off-page optimization (earning links from authoritative sites, which Airbnb sourced heavily through press coverage rather than manual link-building).
- A/B testing is the antidote to gut-feel decision-making at any real scale: Alströmer runs the audience through several real Airbnb experiments (a redesigned share sheet that lifted shares by 40%, a button-copy change that lifted signups by 14%) where a majority of the room guesses wrong every time, illustrating that intuition alone reliably loses to structured experimentation once a company is past its earliest, most obvious decisions.
