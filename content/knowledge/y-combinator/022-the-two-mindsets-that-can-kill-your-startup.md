# The Two Mindsets That Can KILL Your Startup

> **Key principle:** Founders fail by drifting to one of two extremes: giving up after a single bad signal (too pessimistic) or ignoring a mounting pile of negative evidence through "magical thinking" (too optimistic). The healthiest founders can hold both realistic hope and realistic doubt at once without letting either one take over.

*Michael Seibel and Dalton Caldwell on the two mindsets that can kill your startup, Y Combinator "Dalton + Michael" series · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lJausFj_Dto) · 25 min.*

## Key lessons

- A good leader must be an optimist (a pessimistic leader who doesn't believe things can work is disqualifying), but an excessively optimistic leader's plans start to sound disconnected from reality, which destroys employee trust just as fast, since staff start wondering what else the leader is failing to see.
- Common signs of excess pessimism: quitting or wanting to pivot after one bad experience (a poorly-written cold email that got few replies, one rejected pitch), treating a single negative opinion from a friend, investor, or customer as decisive when that person may simply be the wrong judge, and treating an unmet "demo day goal" as proof the company is failing when the real purpose of that goal was just to create forward momentum.
- Pessimism is described as the more curable of the two conditions, since it usually responds to simply resetting expectations (this is normal, it takes longer than you think, mostly you'll hear "no"), whereas overcoming excess optimism is harder because a founder's confidence is also, correctly, part of what makes them effective.
- "Magical thinking," a founder's term borrowed loosely from psychology, describes believing in causes and effects untethered from reality: assuming a single lucky encounter with an investor at an airport could make a company, or thinking a dramatic, movie-like gesture will magically produce success rather than the mundane, repeatable process (like simply applying through the normal channel) that actually works.
- Magical thinking shows up as chronic externalized excuse-making (blaming other people or circumstances for a lack of growth rather than examining the product or approach), and as believing that raising money or hiring a single senior person will automatically solve a deep, poorly-understood problem, rather than actually doing the underlying work.
- Chasing a trending category specifically because it's currently fashionable (a rush of video-conferencing and remote-work startups right as COVID hit, or the wave of chatbot startups a few years earlier) is flagged as a lagging, over-optimistic signal: the founders positioned to benefit from a trend, like Zoom, had already built for years before the moment arrived, so joining after a trend has already "jumped the shark" usually means arriving too late.
- The psychological core of the talk is "cognitive dissonance": most people are uncomfortable holding two conflicting realities simultaneously (a key employee just quit and the situation looks dire, and there's also still a sales call worth showing up fully for), but the best founders learn to hold both without collapsing into either denial or despair.
- Self-diagnostic tip: notice how conversations with you leave other people feeling. If people consistently feel drained and deflated after talking to you about the company, that's a signal you're too pessimistic; if your own excitement outpaces any actual evidence you can point to, that's a signal of magical thinking.
