# How to Apply And Succeed at Y Combinator | Startup School

> **Key principle:** Reviewers read an application looking for a clear story: who the founders are, what they're building, what exists today, and whether anyone actually wants it. If a reader can't reconstruct that story from your answers, "the odds of convincing someone is basically zero," so clarity and honesty matter more than sounding impressive.

*Dalton Caldwell on applying to and succeeding at Y Combinator, Y Combinator Startup School · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=B5tU2447OK8) · 25 min.*

## Key lessons

- Applying is framed as "luck creation": a fast, low-cost application with uncapped upside, where avoiding rejection by simply not applying reduces your surface area for something good to happen. This same logic is offered as general advice for finding co-founders, ideas, or customers.
- Common excuses for not applying don't hold up: "too early" (many accepted companies pivoted after getting in, some hadn't even quit their day jobs) and "too far along" (companies with $500K ARR or having already raised a million dollars still apply and get accepted).
- Genuine reasons not to apply: you only want to work on the idea briefly (a summer, then get a job), you're philosophically opposed to venture capital as a model, or the business has no technology component at all.
- Concrete application tips: fill out every field including founder bios (skipping them signals low effort), follow the founder-video instructions exactly (a scored proxy for detail-orientation), and write short, direct answers, since longer responses don't earn bonus points.
- GitLab's actual early YC application description ("100,000 organizations use it... programmers at Apple use it") is held up as the model for clarity: concrete, specific, and immediately understandable in a few sentences.
- Honesty is strictly enforced: misrepresenting revenue, traction, or credentials (including subtly presenting annual revenue as monthly) is treated as an automatic disqualifier, and any striking claim, like a big-name customer, needs to be backable with real evidence.
- The single biggest lever on interview-selection odds is the technical strength of the founding team (hireable-at-a-top-company skill level, internships included); teams without a technical co-founder working on a known "tar pit" idea have the lowest odds, which is the explicit reason YC built co-founder matching.
- In interviews, YC partners ask basic, context-dependent questions, not trick questions, and the biggest failure mode is treating the process as adversarial: reciting memorized pitches, ignoring the actual question asked, or stretching the truth all score poorly, since the interviewers are literally the people a founder would work with if accepted.
