# Design for Startups by Garry Tan (Part 1)

> **Key principle:** Design is not "how it looks," it's making things that work well and delight users, and the two can conflict: Apple's screen notch is described as form winning over function since it makes video content categorically worse to look at. Good design starts with genuine empathy for a specific user, expressed through direct command-voice writing, borrowed interaction patterns, and a visual toolkit built almost entirely from contrast and proximity.

*Garry Tan (Posterous co-founder, former YC partner, Initialized Capital founder) on design for startups, Y Combinator Startup School · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9urYWGx2uNk) · 67 min.*

## Key lessons

- Design splits into three practical layers for a startup: product design (what's the problem and who's it for), interaction design (how does the flow actually work, wireframes before pixels), and visual design (the last-mile pixel-level execution); most solo founders end up doing all three themselves before ever hiring a specialist.
- Empathy is the real starting skill, not aesthetic taste: Tan explicitly recommends Dale Carnegie's "How to Win Friends and Influence People" as required founder reading, since design fundamentally requires becoming genuinely interested in another person's point of view before you can build for them.
- Build concrete user personas, not an abstract "target market": Posterous's own early personas included "David the Dad" (an average-comfort iPhone user), "Grace the Grandma" (low-comfort, uses Hotmail on a netbook), and a power user chasing the then-uncertain social media landscape, each pulling product priorities in different, sometimes conflicting directions.
- Prioritize features with a simple P0/P1/P2/P3 system tied explicitly back to which persona needs them; Tan frames this as the real lever against scope creep, since a team without clear priorities doesn't know what to cut when a sprint runs long, and unprioritized "someday" features are how bloated, confusing settings menus happen in shipped products.
- Interaction design is fundamentally about writing commands, not neutral descriptions: Tan cites Stanford research (Clifford Nass, Byron Reeves, BJ Fogg) showing people unconsciously treat computers like social actors, and argues large companies can get away with vague, passive-voice copy because of brand and sales-force scale, but a startup cannot; homepage and onboarding copy needs direct, obvious calls to action.
- Two concrete friction-reduction techniques: remove unnecessary steps (Paul Graham's real feedback to Posterous, dropping the "confirm password" field, reportedly lifted signup conversion by as much as 50%) and, when a flow is unavoidably complex, break it into a wizard-style sequence rather than one overwhelming form.
- Don't invent new interaction patterns, steal proven ones: conventions like pull-to-refresh or swipe-to-archive exist because they're already familiar and satisfying to users, and reinventing basic navigation (the example given: dot-based pagination borrowed from mobile swipe UI misapplied to a desktop trackpad context) usually just confuses people rather than impressing them.
- Visual design reduces to three practical tools: contrast (bold, color, or size to signal importance, checkable with a literal "squint test" where a good layout still reads as a clear shape when blurred), proximity (grouping related elements, illustrated by the common mistake of visually separating a login link from the signup flow it belongs to), and a grid system (Bootstrap's original CSS grid is cited as the tool that made basic visual hierarchy accessible to non-designers).
- Feedback loops don't end at ship: usability testing (walking a real person through a printed wireframe before any code exists, asking open questions like "what would you click next") and customer support (treated as a design research channel, not a chore to minimize) are described as the most underrated parts of the process, since the shipped product's failures live disproportionately in unglamorous long-tail edge cases that a spec never anticipated, and a founder who personally fixes a user's reported bug creates "an evangelist for life" in a way large companies, who treat users as anonymous, structurally cannot.
