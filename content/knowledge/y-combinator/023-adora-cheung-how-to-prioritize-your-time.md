# Adora Cheung - How to Prioritize Your Time

> **Key principle:** "Real" startup progress is narrowly defined as anything that directly grows your primary KPI, almost always talking to users or building product; everything else (conferences, awards, optimizing vanity metrics) is "fake progress" that feels productive but doesn't move the needle. Grade every task on impact and complexity, and always do high-impact, low-complexity work first.

*Adora Cheung (YC partner, Homejoy co-founder) on how to prioritize your time, Y Combinator Startup School · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XcCmMOWuAF4) · 17 min.*

## Key lessons

- Draw a hard line between real and fake startup progress: real progress is anything that grows your primary KPI (almost always revenue or active users), and in practice that reduces to two activities, talking to users and building/iterating product. Attending conferences, chasing awards, or optimizing the wrong metric are classic examples of fake progress that feel productive but don't count.
- Low-value work creeps into a schedule unnoticed because it's usually the easiest thing to do and gives the satisfying feeling of checking something off a list; Cheung recommends an hour-by-hour journaling exercise for a full week to expose how much of your actual time went to genuinely high-leverage work versus busywork.
- Maintain a running spreadsheet of task ideas, but don't act on a new idea the moment it occurs to you; jumping straight to "the thing you just thought of" causes constant whiplash and is a primary cause of a week with no visible progress.
- Grade every task on two independent dimensions: impact (high/medium/low probability of hitting this week's KPI goal) and complexity (easy under a day, medium one to two days, hard multiple days that may not finish this week); a common technical-founder mistake is defaulting to "build first, talk to users later," which this framework exposes as usually the least impactful use of the week.
- Always prioritize the high-impact, low-complexity combination first, then high-impact/medium-complexity; low-impact, high-complexity tasks should essentially never be worked on, since there's no path where they're worth the time even if completed.
- Avoid working on too many things simultaneously; picking a small number of tasks you can fully complete and show real progress on beats spreading effort across many half-finished ones.
- Use YC's weekly-update discipline (goal, whether it was hit, biggest blocker, predicted vs. actual impact, key learning) as a feedback loop, and periodically re-read your own update history to check whether you're learning fast enough, correctly predicting task impact, letting fake progress creep back in, or stuck hitting the same blocker week after week without realizing it.
- If a task consistently runs over time, either break it into smaller easy/medium pieces, or restructure your schedule using a "maker's/manager's" split (borrowed from Paul Graham) that dedicates whole days, or at least large uninterrupted blocks, to a single task type (coding vs. meetings) rather than fragmenting the day into small context-switching chunks, which is especially critical for solo founders who can't divide labor across a team.
