# Michael Seibel - Building Product

> **Key principle:** Before writing code, a founder needs a one-sentence problem statement, a specific first customer, and proof that customer is desperate enough to use something bad. From there, product development is a disciplined cycle: brainstorm openly, rank ideas by real difficulty rather than whoever argues loudest, write the spec down, ship on a fixed cadence, and track events-based usage or revenue metrics from the very first release.

*Michael Seibel (Justin.tv/Twitch, Socialcam co-founder; former YC CEO) on building product, Y Combinator Startup School · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=C27RVio2rOs) · 59 min.*

## Key lessons

- If a founder can't state the problem they're solving in one or two sentences, they don't actually know the problem yet; the diagnostic questions Seibel runs through are: have you personally experienced this problem, have you defined it narrowly enough to actually address (not "cure cancer," but the specific first slice you can serve), and is it even solvable given real-world constraints (Poppy, a babysitting marketplace, discovered that the caregivers skilled enough to earn parents' trust with infants already had well-paid nanny jobs, making the "on-demand infant sitter" version of the problem structurally hard).
- Evaluate a candidate customer by frequency and intensity of their pain, not by how big or impressive they sound: a five-year car-purchase decision is low-frequency even though it feels important, while Uber's underlying problem (needing to get somewhere) is both frequent and intense, which is what actually predicts a large market.
- Look for desperate customers, not friendly ones: talking to founder-friends, YC peers, or investors about a product is close to useless for feedback because they almost never have the underlying problem themselves and will lead a founder astray "out of good intentions."
- Charging money, even a high price, is a better early signal than giving a product away for free; a free product mostly attracts casual users who aren't the desperate, real-problem customers a founder needs to learn from, and starting with a higher price is described as "almost always better" than starting free.
- Replace Google Analytics with an events-based product analytics tool (Mixpanel, Amplitude, Heap) from the very first release, track five to ten simple, well-named actions (not 150), and treat instrumentation as a required line item in every product spec, not an afterthought bolted on later.
- The team's actual product-development cycle: run an open brainstorm where every idea gets written on the board regardless of who proposed it, rate each idea "easy / medium / hard" based on real build cost, tackle high-impact hard ideas first then fill in with mediums and easys, write the spec down in detail, then hold the line on scope for a fixed cycle (roughly one to two weeks) so no new "brilliant idea" derails the sprint mid-flight.
- Define iterate (changing the solution while keeping the same customer and problem) as the normal, expected mode of progress, and pivot (changing the customer or the problem itself) as something that should be rare; teams that treat two months of slow progress as grounds to pivot are moving far too fast, since finding real product-market fit realistically takes closer to two years of sustained iteration.
- The "fake Steve Jobs" myth, a founder who supposedly conjures a perfect product from pure vision, is dangerous: the real first iPhone had no 3G, no App Store, and constant battery problems, and Apple's actual edge was rapid, humble iteration driven by real user feedback, not innate genius that skipped the feedback loop entirely.
