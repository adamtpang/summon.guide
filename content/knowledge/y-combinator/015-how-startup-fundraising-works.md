# How Startup Fundraising Works | Startup School

> **Key principle:** Fundraising is not glamorous or fast like Shark Tank; it's a grind of repeated one-on-one meetings. A real YC company's fundraising map shows 160 investor meetings, 39 yeses, checks from $5K to $200K, and 4.5 months to close $1.6M, and that grind is normal, not a sign something is wrong.

*Brad Flora (YC group partner) on how startup fundraising actually works, Y Combinator Startup School · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zBUhQPPS9AY) · 28 min.*

## Key lessons

- Fundraising isn't glamorous: in reality it's a series of quiet one-on-one coffee chats and Zoom calls, not a Shark Tank-style pitch competition, which Flora notes exist mostly as marketing events rather than real investment sources.
- You don't need money to start: the best founders build a rough first version and get real users before ever raising, since cheap hosting and audience-finding tools (Product Hunt, Hacker News, social media) make this affordable. Solugen built a desk-sized chemical reactor, sold hydrogen peroxide to hot tub supply stores for $10K/month, and only then raised its first $4M, rather than pitching a $20M facility off a deck alone.
- Startups don't need to be "impressive" to raise money, they need to convince investors: nearly every legendary company sounded bad on paper at the start (Airbnb was originally "renting an air mattress on someone's floor"), and investors are described as bored by founders who try to wow them rather than simply show the real product and explain honestly why it might get big.
- Fundraising isn't necessarily slow, complicated, or expensive: the giant, months-long, lawyer-heavy rounds covered in the press are Series A/B rounds, not seed rounds. YC's SAFE (a five-page document with only two real negotiated terms, valuation cap and amount) lets founders close a seed round in days without lawyers.
- Raising on SAFEs preserves founder control: they carry no board seats, no shares change hands until a future priced round, and there are no information rights, which is how Zapier's founders raised once, kept the majority of their company, and ran it entirely their own way (including going fully remote a decade before it was common) without ever raising again.
- Bootstrapping forever is reframed as the same pain as fundraising, just stretched across a company's entire life: constant fear of running out of money, no real founder salary, and frequent detours into consulting work just to stay afloat. Flora's suggested alternative is to raise once on a SAFE, then never raise again if you don't want to.
- A fancy network is not required to raise money: Podium's two Utah-based founders with no Silicon Valley connections raised successfully once they had real revenue from tire shops, because investors respond to evidence of a working business far more than pedigree or introductions.
- Rejection is universal and not a verdict on the startup: a medical-device founder was turned down more than 50 times before landing a $25K check (that company later sold for $275M), and a collectibles marketplace that struggled to raise its seed round later reached a $3.7B valuation; you only need a handful of believing investors, not everyone's approval.
