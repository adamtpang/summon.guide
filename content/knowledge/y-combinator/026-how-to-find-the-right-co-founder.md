# How to Find the Right Co-founder

> **Key principle:** The most important thing to know about a potential co-founder isn't their skill set, it's how they handle stress, since that's what determines whether the relationship survives the years of pressure a startup actually requires. Find co-founders by doing small, low-commitment projects together long before you need to found anything, not by cold-matching on an idea.

*Harj Taggar (YC group partner) on finding the right co-founder, Y Combinator Startup School · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=prKi3-rUPHc) · 29 min.*

## Key lessons

- Three reasons to have a co-founder: productivity (dividing work, better idea generation through debate), moral support (the best co-founder pairs naturally balance each other's highs and lows, something a hired employee, who is fundamentally less invested, can't provide), and pattern-matching to success, since Apple, Facebook, Google, and Microsoft all started with co-founders even though history later collapses them into a single celebrity figurehead.
- Solo founding can work, but only under two specific conditions: extremely high personal conviction in the idea (often from living the problem or deep domain expertise) and the ability to make real progress alone, usually meaning being technical enough to build a first version without anyone else. Dropbox's Drew Houston was initially rejected by YC for having no co-founder, kept building alone, found his eventual CTO Arash, and was funded on the second application.
- The single most important trait to evaluate before starting a company with someone is how they handle stress, and second, how well they help you handle yours, which is why the strongest co-founder relationships come from people you already have real shared history with (a close friend, a prior working relationship under pressure), not people you've only socialized with casually.
- Explicitly discuss goals and values before committing: a founder who wants a high-growth, high-risk, venture-backed company aiming for an eventual IPO is not compatible with a co-founder who wants a smaller, steady, comfortably profitable business, even though both goals are individually legitimate; surfacing this mismatch early prevents a slow-building structural conflict.
- Don't over-index on matching skills to your current specific idea; a trusted co-founder willing to learn an unfamiliar skill (like a non-iOS-developer learning iOS development because the startup needs it) is a better bet than a stranger who happens to already have the exact technical skill but no track record with you.
- Find co-founders by first working on small, low-stakes side projects with people you know, in school, at work, in hobby communities, rather than starting from "I have an idea, now I need a co-founder." Projects lower the ask (much easier to say yes to "want to build a game together" than "want to start a company"), and let you build a real read on who you work well with before anything is at stake.
- Once you've found a candidate, de-risk the decision with a defined trial period (weeks to a few months) working together toward a concrete milestone (an MVP, or pitching real customers), followed by an honest conversation about whether to formally commit or part ways cleanly.
- On equity and title: default to a 50/50 split unless there's a clear reason to deviate, and resist the temptation to grab extra equity just because you made some early solo progress, since long-term full commitment from your co-founder matters more than a short-term ownership advantage. On the CEO question, the title matters little day-to-day (everyone still does unglamorous grunt work early on) but matters externally to investors, who want a clear final decision-maker; if both co-founders want the CEO title for its own sake, Taggar calls that itself a red flag worth taking seriously before starting the company.
