# Should You Start A Startup? | Startup School

> **Key principle:** After working with roughly a thousand YC founders, Taggar found no reliable test for who will succeed; school pedigree and even interview confidence are both poor predictors. The trait that actually matters is resilience: the ability to absorb sustained, personal-feeling rejection from users and investors without quitting.

*Harj Taggar (YC group partner) on whether you should start a startup, Y Combinator Startup School · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BUE-icVYRFU) · 17 min.*

## Key lessons

- Neither school/work pedigree nor interview confidence reliably predicts founder success. Benchling's Sagi, a quietly spoken engineer, was initially doubted for enterprise sales and had no revenue for nearly two years after graduating YC, yet went on to build a multi-billion-dollar company.
- Resilience, not brilliance or charisma, is the trait Taggar identifies as most predictive of founder success. It shows up specifically as the ability to withstand repeated, personal-feeling rejection from users and investors without giving up.
- Initial motivation for starting a company (wanting to get rich, or just being curious what founding feels like) matters far less than what that motivation evolves into over time; the durable motivations Taggar has observed are genuine interest in the problem and love for the people you're building with.
- Practical self-test for whether to start now: define the real worst case (roughly a year with little or no salary before knowing if an idea is promising) and honestly ask if you can live with it. A recent graduate risks little by trying; someone deep into a lucrative corporate career risks more, and unresolved anxiety about the downside will itself sabotage the effort.
- The value of the startup experience persists through failure: companies like Rippling explicitly recruit and promote ex-founders into general-manager roles, and a founder whose first company failed later became Airbnb's first employee before founding his own multi-billion-dollar company.
- To get founder-ready without an idea or co-founder yet, don't treat idea-finding and co-founder-finding as separate problems: seek out smart people you enjoy debating ideas with (often found at a startup job, since that environment selects for less risk-averse people than a big-company job).
- Once ideas start forming, ship small, real side projects rather than waiting for a fully-formed plan. Borrowing advice from YC's Paul Buchheit, it's better to build something a few people genuinely love than something many people are merely indifferent to; one passionate user of a rough prototype outweighs a thousand waitlist signups.
- The signal that it's time to quit and commit isn't explosive traction, which can't be relied on to appear on schedule, but a felt contrast: energized by the side project versus drained by the day job, especially paired with finding a collaborator you genuinely enjoy working with who also wants to found a company.
