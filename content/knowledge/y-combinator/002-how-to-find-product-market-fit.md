# David Rusenko - How To Find Product Market Fit

> **Key principle:** Weebly took about 18 months of flat or declining growth, and roughly four years overall, before product-market fit arrived, despite multiple press spikes on TechCrunch, Newsweek, and Time that each faded back to baseline. PMF shows up as retained, returning usage that pulls customers back on its own, not a launch story.

*David Rusenko (Weebly co-founder, sold to Square) on finding product-market fit, Y Combinator Startup School · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0LNQxT9LvM0) · 59 min.*

## Key lessons

- Only two things at a startup are genuinely hard: finding product-market fit and building a great team. Making money is a distant third and is usually much easier once real usage already exists, which is why Rusenko advises against optimizing for monetization before people are hooked.
- Press spikes are not product-market fit: every major feature (TechCrunch, Newsweek, Time) produced a burst of new signups at Weebly that decayed back to the prior baseline for a year and a half, and the real inflection only showed up when daily new users stopped decaying and started compounding.
- The core toolkit for finding fit is small and qualitative: 5-10 in-depth customer interviews, 3-5 unmoderated UX testing sessions (watch someone attempt a real task without touching their keyboard or coaching them, however painful the silence gets), and a handful of tracked metrics, not large-sample market research.
- The concrete PMF signals to track are returning usage by day 1/3/7/30, an NPS score above roughly 50, and cohort-based paying-customer renewal rates; Rusenko explicitly distinguishes renewal rate from "churn," which he calls misleading because its denominator shifts as a company grows.
- Products that create a new market often look feature-poor next to entrenched competitors at launch, the way the early iPhone lacked apps and 3G, because the goal is pulling in a wholly new set of users rather than out-featuring incumbents on their existing turf.
- Before product-market fit, keep the team under roughly 20-25 people (past that, flat structure breaks down and slows iteration) and prioritize whatever task teaches you the most about your single biggest unknown, not whatever scores highest on a simple effort-versus-impact list.
- After product-market fit, the advice flips to scaling aggressively, since market-leader advantages compound (faster hiring, more revenue, easier financing), though Rusenko still caps headcount growth at roughly doubling per year to avoid building a company on an unstable foundation.
- Listen to what customers say their problem is, not the specific solution they propose (echoing Henry Ford's "faster horse" line); ship cheap, disposable prototypes to test ideas instead of fully building things out, because it typically takes far more iterations to reach fit than founders expect going in.
