# Adora Cheung - How to Set KPIs and Goals

> **Key principle:** A startup should track exactly one primary metric, ideally revenue, that quantifies real value delivered, reflects recurring rather than one-time value, and lags behind (rather than predicts) actual usage. Set a weekly growth target (5-10% a week is typical for YC-stage companies) and use hitting or missing it as the mechanism for deciding what to work on next.

*Adora Cheung (YC partner, Homejoy co-founder) on setting KPIs and goals, Y Combinator Startup School · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lL6GdUHIBsM) · 24 min.*

## Key lessons

- A good primary metric has four properties: it quantifies real value delivered to the customer (ideally through money, since paying is the strongest signal), it captures recurring rather than one-time value (monthly recurring revenue for SaaS, daily active users for a daily-use product), it is a lagging indicator (revenue already received, not email signups that might convert someday), and it's usable as a fast feedback loop, which is why monthly active users is discouraged: it lags too much to inform weekly decisions.
- Revenue is the recommended default primary metric for the large majority of startups, because nothing signals real value like a customer handing over money; a common trap is delaying charging "to get feedback first," but free users give qualitatively different, less serious feedback than paying customers.
- Active users is a legitimate primary metric only in specific cases: advertising-based businesses (where revenue is essentially a multiple of active users), or businesses with very strong network effects that need to prove the flywheel before monetizing. Even then, "user" must be defined precisely (an email signup is not the same as a daily active user).
- Two-sided marketplaces need a metric that captures value for both sides at once, such as nights booked for Airbnb (guests and hosts) or weekly trips for Uber (riders and drivers), rather than picking one side's user count alone.
- Biotech and hard-tech companies facing regulatory approval before any sales are possible should track technical milestones (proof that the underlying science or technology works) rather than revenue or users, since a quantitative business metric doesn't yet apply to their stage.
- Set growth targets in weekly, not monthly, increments, since startups need fast feedback loops; an analysis of recent YC demo-day companies found growth clustering around 5-10% week over week (roughly 20-50% month over month), which Paul Graham's essay "Startup = Growth" cites as the range that separates startups with billion-dollar potential from good small businesses growing around 1% a week.
- Track 3-5 secondary metrics (not more) alongside the primary metric to avoid tunnel vision. A common failure mode is only tracking growth while ignoring retention, since new-user acquisition and churn are equally important to the same underlying health picture.
- Use the metric operationally, not just for reporting: print a forward-looking growth chart and post it somewhere visible (Airbnb's founders famously did this early on), stack-rank ideas each week by expected impact on the metric, and treat a missed weekly target as the trigger to go talk to more users rather than to guess internally at what's wrong.
