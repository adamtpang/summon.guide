# Enterprise Sales | Startup School

> **Key principle:** Founders, including technical ones, are almost always the right people to sell a pre-product-market-fit product, since early sales requires vision, product expertise, and a tight feedback loop that a hired salesperson can't provide yet. Sales is not adversarial or a "dark art"; it's fundamentally about deeply understanding one real person's problem and helping them solve it.

*Pete Koomen (Optimizely co-founder, YC group partner) on enterprise sales, Y Combinator Startup School · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0fKYVl12VTA) · 23 min.*

## Key lessons

- Sales before product-market fit is fundamentally different from sales after it: it requires vision, credibility, and a tight feedback loop with the people building the product, which is why it's a founder's job, and technical founders often turn out to be great at it because expertise and genuine conviction matter more in selling than social polish.
- Start prospecting from a clear, specific sales hypothesis ("customer X has problem Y, our product solves it"), not a vague target market; Optimizely's early hypothesis named a specific persona (marketers at small/medium tech, media, and e-commerce companies who wanted to A/B test without writing code) and used a tool (BuiltWith) to filter for prospects likely to fit it.
- A major founder anti-pattern: talking mostly to whoever is easiest to reach, rather than to real qualified buyers. This creates an illusion of progress, since easy-to-reach contacts give plenty of "feedback" without ever being a real customer, whether that's selling enterprise-only software to startups, or trying a bottom-up motion (individual users self-adopting) for a product that structurally requires top-down buy-in from a CIO or CFO.
- The first call is for qualification, not pitching: the goal is to learn whether the prospect actually has the problem, has budget and decision-making authority to buy, and to schedule a demo, using open questions about the cost of the problem, prior attempts to solve it, and how their organization actually buys software.
- A great demo is not a feature tour, it's a personalized story: recap the specific person's problem from the first call, walk through exactly how the product solves it (with the prospect's own logo, website, or team names where possible), and build toward one or more "magic moments." Optimizely's founders built a live-editing demo directly on a prospect's own website (rather than a generic demo site) specifically because it made the value visceral.
- On pricing: don't publish enterprise pricing upfront, treat each quote as a live experiment, and don't fear pricing too high. Koomen's co-founder once quoted $10,000/month, got negotiated down to $2,000/month, and the deal still closed, proof that a too-high price rarely scares away a customer who genuinely needs the product, while a too-low price signals the founders aren't confident in the value delivered.
- Closing is a multi-step process, not one conversation: ask upfront how the prospect's company actually buys software and who needs to sign off, so procurement steps (security review, legal redlining, compliance signoff) don't become surprises; keep contracts simple by borrowing YC-backed Common Paper's open-source templates and moving scope/timeline details into a separate order form.
- The single biggest post-close mistake is treating implementation as "the customer's job": Optimizely closed six-figure deals where customers never actually ran a single test because no one on the vendor side helped get it installed. The fix was to build a shared implementation roadmap and project-manage adoption as seriously as an internal high-priority project, since a sales funnel only really ends once the customer is using the product habitually.
