# How to Get and Evaluate Startup Ideas | Startup School

> **Key principle:** Good startup ideas rarely come from sitting down and brainstorming; they come from working inside a fertile problem space until founder-market fit and a real, acute problem become obvious. Ideas that look unattractive on the surface (annoying to build, boring, or already crowded with competitors) are disproportionately the ones worth taking, because they scare off other founders.

*Jared Friedman on generating and evaluating startup ideas, Y Combinator Startup School · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Th8JoIan4dg) · 32 min.*

## Key lessons

- Most bad startup ideas fall into four traps: building a "solution in search of a problem" (starting from a technology like AI and hunting for something to apply it to), chasing a "tar pit" idea that has a hidden structural reason it has stayed unsolved for years (the classic example being yet another app to coordinate plans with friends), waiting for a mythical perfect idea instead of starting with a good-enough one that can morph, and picking something the founding team has no real edge in.
- Friedman's ten-question evaluation framework centers on founder-market fit (do you personally have an unfair advantage on this problem, the way Plangrid's founders combined construction industry experience with engineering skill), market size (big now, or small-but-fast-growing like early Coinbase), and how acute the problem is (the strongest signal is when the current alternative is literally nothing, as with pre-Brex startup credit cards).
- Counterintuitively, having competitors is often a good sign, not a red flag: Dropbox launched as roughly the 20th cloud storage company into a market where nobody had solved the actual UX problem, which is exactly the kind of opening a new insight can exploit.
- Picking a fertile "idea space" (one level of abstraction above a single idea, like vertical SaaS versus consumer hardware) matters because different spaces have wildly different historical hit rates; staying inside a good space lets a founder drift into the right specific idea even if the first guess is wrong, as Fivetran did by repeatedly pivoting products while staying anchored to "data tools for companies."
- Three signals that make an idea look bad but are often good: schlep blindness (unglamorous setup work, like Stripe's early bank-integration slog, keeps other founders away), being in a "boring" space (Gusto's payroll software beat flashier consumer ideas precisely because founders avoid boring problems), and existing competition (usually proof of real demand that nobody has fully met).
- Around 70% of YC's top 100 companies had their idea occur organically rather than through deliberate brainstorming, which is why becoming a genuine expert in something valuable, or just building things you find interesting, is a more reliable idea-generation strategy than sitting down to think one up.
- The most concrete idea-generation method for founders without deep domain expertise: pick a plausible idea space, then do the manual legwork to become an expert in it, the way the AtoB founders physically visited truck stops to interview drivers until a real problem (fuel cards for truckers) surfaced.
- When a founder still can't tell if an idea is good after running it through the framework, the only remaining test is to launch it and see what real users do.
