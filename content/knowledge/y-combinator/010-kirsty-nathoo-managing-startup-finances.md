# Kirsty Nathoo - Managing Startup Finances

> **Key principle:** Cash is a startup's lifeblood, and running out is terminal in a way other mistakes aren't. Track three numbers straight from your bank statements (balance, money in, money out), calculate burn, runway, and growth rate from them weekly, and never round the numbers in your own favor: it only delays the same crash date.

*Kirsty Nathoo (Y Combinator CFO) on managing startup finances, Y Combinator Startup School · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LBC16jhiwak) · 29 min.*

## Key lessons

- Three numbers, taken straight from bank statements with no bookkeeper or software required, tell you almost everything: current bank balance, money in, and money out. From these, derive burn (money out minus money in), runway (balance divided by burn), and period-over-period revenue growth rate.
- Use YC co-founder Trevor Blackwell's "default alive vs. default dead" calculator: given constant expenses and your current growth rate, will the company reach profitability before the cash runs out. Being profitable, or even one lever-pull away from it, gives founders real negotiating leverage because it removes desperation from fundraising conversations.
- Check these numbers weekly at minimum, daily if runway is tight. Runway is explicitly framed as a tool for a founder's own honesty, not a vanity metric to compare against other companies; quietly assuming a lower burn than reality just delays the same outcome while feeling better in the meantime.
- Expenses reliably rise over time in three underestimated ways: founders paying themselves near-minimum wage early on hides the true cost of the business, every new hire costs roughly 25-50% more than salary alone once equipment, benefits, and space are included, and paid acquisition costs typically climb as the cheapest, most eager early adopters get used up.
- Delegating bookkeeping to an outside bookkeeper does not transfer responsibility for understanding the numbers; a bookkeeper reconstructs your finances from bank statements at a remove and will make mistakes, so founders still have to review monthly reports and flag anything that looks off.
- Headcount is a seductive but misleading measure of progress ("how many employees are you at?"); revenue-per-employee is the better metric, every hire is an investment that should show a return, and founders should be willing to fire fast when a hire isn't generating one.
- Scaling headcount before product-market fit is called the single mistake with no coming back from: hiring more salespeople or engineers doesn't create product-market fit, and low sales or a thin feature set are usually product problems, not staffing problems.
- On fundraising timing: always assume the current round will be your last, and start raising with roughly 12 months of runway remaining, since the process itself typically takes about three months and a shrinking cash balance during that window steadily erodes a founder's leverage.
