# Startup Business Models and Pricing | Startup School

> **Key principle:** Nearly every billion-dollar YC company uses one of just nine proven business models (SaaS, transactional, marketplace, hard tech, usage-based, enterprise, advertising, e-commerce, bio); the innovation should be in the product, not a novel way of making money. Pricing is a second, equally underused lever: most startups underprice, and raising prices is often the fastest way to grow revenue.

*Aaron Epstein (YC group partner) on business models and pricing, Y Combinator Startup School · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=oWZbWzAyHAE) · 33 min.*

## Key lessons

- Three business models (SaaS, transactional, marketplace) account for 67% of YC's top 100 companies by value; marketplaces alone are only 14% of that list but create roughly 30% of its total value, because clearing the initial chicken-and-egg supply/demand problem tends to produce durable, winner-take-all network effects.
- Transactional businesses (Stripe, Coinbase, Brex) overperform because they sit directly in the flow of funds and can simply take a cut on every transaction; being that close to the money makes them "critical infrastructure" a customer never wants to rip out.
- SaaS wins on predictable recurring revenue, but only compounds with strong retention: a five-point difference in monthly retention (95% vs. 90%) is the gap between ending the year with 54% or just 28% of an original 100 customers, illustrating why churn quietly undermines even well-acquired customer bases.
- Business models conspicuously missing from YC's top 100: services/consulting (non-recurring, scales with headcount, low margin), affiliate businesses (too many steps removed from the actual transaction), hardware (capital-intensive, low margin), and businesses built entirely on someone else's platform (exposed to that platform simply shutting them out).
- Five pricing insights: charge from day one (refusing to charge destroys the signal of who actually wants the product); price on perceived value, not cost-plus (Stripe deliberately priced above competitors early to test how much value customers placed on fast integration and documentation); most startups underprice, and a lower price than a well-funded competitor is not a durable strategy since they can always underprice you into the ground; pricing isn't permanent (Netflix has repeatedly raised prices across 221 million subscribers as its main revenue-growth lever); and keep pricing pages simple (GitLab's three clear plans convert better than a cluttered five-tier page full of slashed prices).
- Segment's origin story: two engineers nervously started charging $10/month after giving their product away for free, only to have customers say they wished it cost more, worried a cheap vendor might not be trustworthy with their data; a sales advisor later pushed them to quote $120,000/year to an enterprise prospect, and even after the customer negotiated down, they landed 150x their original price, a pattern that scaled toward Twilio's $3B+ acquisition of Segment.
- Practical price-finding tactic: keep incrementally raising prices until customers start to complain but still pay; a price accepted instantly with zero pushback is itself a signal it was set too low.
- When customers won't pay more, it usually means one of two things: the product needs more real value built into it, or the problem being solved is a "nice to have" rather than a top-three priority worth real budget, in which case the fix is repositioning toward a bigger problem, not discounting further.
