# Understanding SAFEs and Priced Equity Rounds by Kirsty Nathoo

> **Key principle:** A SAFE isn't shares yet, so its dilution is easy to forget, but it's real the moment you sign it. Walking a real numeric example from incorporation through a Series A: founders who raised on SAFEs at a $4M and $8M post-money cap had already sold 15% of the company before a single share existed, and by the time the priced round, option pool increase, and new investor money all converted, their ownership had fallen from an apparent 92.5% to an actual 51.5%.

*Kirsty Nathoo (Y Combinator CFO) on understanding SAFEs and priced equity rounds, Y Combinator Startup School · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Dk6JNTDec9I) · 45 min.*

## Key lessons

- A SAFE (Simple Agreement for Future Equity) is not debt: it carries no interest rate and no maturity date, and it converts to actual shares only at a future priced round, acquisition, or dissolution. Only two terms are typically negotiated (amount invested and valuation cap), which is what makes SAFEs far faster to close than a full priced round.
- The core dilution formula, valid for both SAFEs and priced rounds, is: investor ownership = amount invested divided by post-money valuation (or valuation cap). In the walked example, a $200K investment on a $4M post-money cap buys 5%, and an $800K investment on an $8M cap buys 10%, meaning the founders have already sold 15% of the company before any shares are actually issued.
- Tracking dilution is explicitly the founder's own responsibility, not something to delegate entirely to lawyers; a simple spreadsheet is enough at the earliest stage, and dedicated cap-table tools (Carta, captable.io) become worth adopting as the structure gets more complex.
- Post-money SAFEs (YC's current standard) were introduced specifically to make dilution easier for founders to track in real time, compared to the older pre-money SAFE format where the true ownership impact of multiple stacked SAFEs was harder to calculate until conversion.
- Creating or expanding an employee option pool (a routine step before hiring and again as a standard part of most priced-round term sheets, often targeting roughly 10%) dilutes existing shareholders, including any outstanding SAFE holders, and should be factored into a founder's real ownership picture the same way SAFE dilution is.
- At a priced round, SAFE conversion, option pool increase, and new investor money all execute in a specific order (SAFEs convert first, then the option pool is topped up, then new money buys in at the calculated price per share), and each step further dilutes the founders; understanding this order explains why a cap table can look dramatically different than founders expect on close.
- If a company's priced round ends up valued below an outstanding SAFE's valuation cap, SAFE holders actually get a better deal (converting at the lower priced-round price instead of their cap), which further increases founder dilution beyond the original back-of-envelope estimate, a scenario Nathoo flags as rare but important to understand before negotiating an aggressively high cap.
- Don't over-optimize SAFE valuation caps in the fundraising process: in the worked numerical example, negotiating an $8M cap up to a $10M cap only moved final founder ownership from 51.5% to 52.7%, a difference judged not worth the negotiating friction and investor goodwill it costs, especially since fundraising itself is a means to an end, not a status competition to win on paper.
