# Do Technical Founders Need Business Co-Founders?

> **Key principle:** All the non-coding work a startup needs (incorporation, payroll, sales, hiring, fundraising, customer support) is general "smart human" work that a technical founder is just as capable of doing as a dedicated business co-founder. Google, Nvidia, Microsoft, Stripe, and Dropbox were all started by all-technical founding teams, proof that a business co-founder is helpful, not structurally required.

*Dalton Caldwell and Michael Seibel on whether technical founders need business co-founders, Y Combinator · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=43RhhwpiSk0) · 11 min.*

## Key lessons

- Every startup has necessary non-coding work: incorporation, bank setup, payroll, tax filings, hiring, customer support, sales, and fundraising. None of these require the ability to write code; they're general smart-human tasks any capable founder can pick up.
- The real question isn't "can" a technical founder do these tasks but whether someone on the team has genuine appetite to do them well, with real enthusiasm rather than grudging, low-effort compliance; a founder who says "sure, I could do sales" in a flat, reluctant tone is a warning sign, not a solved problem.
- If you're a technical founder solving your own (technical) problem, a business co-founder often isn't even well-positioned to help with the hardest parts, since they may lack the domain fluency to talk to your actual customers or explain your product; Nvidia is cited as a company that plausibly couldn't have existed with a non-technical founder driving its original vision.
- Counterexample test: ask whether large, valuable companies exist with only technical founders. Google, Nvidia, Microsoft, and (in the YC portfolio) Stripe and Dropbox all did, which is direct evidence against the idea that a business co-founder is essential, though it's still often genuinely helpful.
- Domain-specific business expertise (a lawyer selling legal software, a doctor selling to hospitals) can be a real, legitimate advantage since it comes with an existing understanding of the customer and industry language, but this is different from claiming a generic "business person" role is required.
- When an investor tells a technical founder "you need a business co-founder," that feedback is often really about a perceived weakness (poor sales appetite, thin go-to-market thinking, or a rough early pitch) rather than a literal structural gap; treating it as a fixed prescription rather than a symptom can lead a founder to search for the wrong fix.
- Real solutions to a founder disliking or being weak at a task like sales include several other than "add a co-founder": learn to do it and get better through repetition, hire an employee (not necessarily a co-founder) who's good at it, or, in Caldwell and Seibel's framing, simply choose to do the hard-but-necessary thing anyway.
- The deeper argument for co-founders in general isn't division of business-versus-technical labor at all, it's that building a company is hard enough that having another committed person to go through the experience with (including for emotional support through inevitable low points) matters more than any specific skill they bring on day one.
