# Co-Founder Mistakes That Kill Companies & How To Avoid Them

> **Key principle:** Reviewing real YC founder "rookie mistakes," Seibel and Caldwell find that co-founder breakups almost never come from a skill mismatch; they come from unresolved conflict. Founders with genuine skill gaps but real trust in each other rarely break up, while founders who never pressure-tested their relationship before the business got hard often do.

*Michael Seibel and Dalton Caldwell on co-founder mistakes that kill companies, Y Combinator "Rookie Mistakes" series · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dlfjs_eEEzs) · 9 min.*

## Key lessons

- Put equity split and founder vesting in writing at the very beginning, before any disagreement happens; without a written record, people genuinely misremember events differently later, and unclear terms turn an ordinary disagreement into a fight over the company's ownership itself.
- Founders overweight skill match and underweight trust when picking a co-founder: Seibel notes he's never seen a company die because "my co-founder is excellent but doesn't have the right skills," while he's seen many die from "my co-founder is a nightmare and I can't take it anymore." Skills you don't have yet are usually learnable on the job; trust and rapport are not.
- Take the first real argument seriously rather than treating it as an aberration; relationships that have never been "pressure tested" before the business gets hard tend to shatter completely at the first real disagreement, while relationships with some history of working through friction hold up.
- Not every disagreement needs to resolve immediately; it's fine to pause an unproductive fight rather than force a resolution in the moment, and understanding how your co-founder handles stress (some attack, some withdraw) helps you correctly interpret their behavior during conflict instead of taking it personally.
- A slow, silent breakdown is a warning sign, not a stable equilibrium: founders who go weeks or months without a real conversation despite spending 8-12 hours a day together are usually already past the point where repair is realistic, and the CEO's job shifts from fixing the relationship to separating as cleanly as possible before things get worse.
- The longer an unhealthy co-founder relationship persists before a breakup, the worse the breakup tends to be, more likely to involve litigation, and more likely to leave a departed founder with disproportionate vested equity still on the cap table.
- Structural tip for equity splits: get as close to equal as possible to avoid ownership-driven resentment, but avoid a literal 50/50 split, which can produce fatal deadlocks; instead give the CEO one extra tiebreaker share, agreed to in writing in advance, specifically for deadlock situations.
- Tactical advice for founders still searching for a co-founder: find the right person first, then look for the idea together, rather than developing an idea alone and adding a co-founder afterward. Co-founders who join later often unconsciously feel like hired help on someone else's company, which quietly undermines their long-term commitment and ownership during hard times.
