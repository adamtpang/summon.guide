# How to Sell by Tyler Bosmeny

> **Key principle:** Sales has a mystique (the charming, golf-playing Don Draper archetype) that scares off technical founders, but real early-stage sales looks like a co-founder on a broken chair in a cramped call room. It's a core founder responsibility, not something to defer until "the product is finished," and a founder's own passion and industry expertise are genuine competitive advantages over any hired salesperson.

*Tyler Bosmeny (Clever CEO/co-founder) on how to sell, Y Combinator Startup School · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xZi4kTJG-LE) · 53 min.*

## Key lessons

- Sales should be owned by a specific founder, treated as close to a full-time job; at Clever, one of three co-founders explicitly peeled off to sell almost 100% of his time starting before the product was even built, while the other two focused on building and implementing it.
- Everett Rogers' technology adoption curve puts "innovators," the segment willing to buy from an unproven startup with no track record, at only about 2.5% of any market; reframing rejection through this lens turns constant "no"s from discouraging into simply expected math (you need to reach roughly 100 companies to find just a couple of real buyers).
- Three real prospecting channels: personal network (don't underestimate how far even a small network extends through friends-of-friends), industry conferences (described as the most underrated sales channel, worth the effort of researching every relevant conference, buying tickets in advance, obtaining the attendee list, and pre-booking meetings so the actual event day is filled in 30-minute increments), and cold email (short, personalized, and actionable, with a real early Clever cold email shown as a template: a few sentences, no jargon, one clear ask for time).
- Sales calls are dominated by listening, not pitching: Bosmeny reports the best salespeople he's shadowed talk roughly 30% of the time and listen 70%, using tools like a call-recording service that reports talk-time ratios after each call to self-check whether a "great call" was actually one-sided.
- Enterprise sales cycles are long and unglamorous: a real early Clever deal chain (repeated unanswered emails, silence, a late response, more silence) took about two months to close a $100,000/year contract, illustrating that persistent, respectful follow-up (roughly weekly) is normal and not "pestering," as long as messages stay personalized rather than templated.
- Three closing traps to avoid: dragging out contract redlining over minor terms with an early customer (get a first agreement in front of them fast, using YC's open-source sales contract template as a starting point, and don't quibble over indemnity/warranty language that doesn't matter yet); building one-off custom features for a single prospect who says they'll buy "if you just add X" (often a soft pass in disguise, better handled by only building it after they commit, or waiting to hear the same request from multiple prospects); and offering long free trials, which Bosmeny says he's never used at any company, preferring annual contracts with a 30-day no-penalty opt-out instead, since a free trial secures neither commitment nor revenue.
- Product-market fit signal comes only from yeses, not from analyzing the reasons behind no's, since people are rarely fully honest about why they're passing (the same way VCs often give indirect reasons for rejecting a pitch); the practical response to any no is simply to move on and keep filling the pipeline.
- Pricing in the earliest days is mostly informed guessing: Clever's original $100K contract price came from an initial guess, tested and iterated as real deals closed; the guidance is to guess a defensible number, and if a sale closes easily, try roughly doubling the price on the next prospect, since founders systematically underprice. Also frame the "how many customers do I need" question through the price-point/sales-motion tradeoff: high-touch enterprise sales pairs with large contract values, while low-price products (tens or low hundreds of dollars) require scalable self-service and marketing motions instead, since a high-touch process can't be repeated millions of times profitably.
