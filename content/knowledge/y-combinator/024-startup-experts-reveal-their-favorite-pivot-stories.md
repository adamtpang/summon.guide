# Startup Experts Reveal Their Favorite Pivot Stories

> **Key principle:** A huge share of the best-known startups pivoted, often dramatically, before finding product-market fit. What separates a healthy pivot from "pivot hell" (switching ideas every week without ever committing) is a clear primary KPI: without one, a founder can wander for years without even realizing their current idea isn't working.

*YC partners share real pivot stories from Brex, Goat, GoCardless, Clipboard Health, Escher Reality, and Creative Market, Y Combinator office hours roundtable · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DmehFuCMtvc) · 21 min.*

## Key lessons

- Brex applied to YC with a virtual reality headset idea; its founders (who had previously built a Brazilian payments company) abandoned it after three to four weeks once conversations with real hardware experts made clear they weren't equipped to build it, then returned to the payments expertise they already had, which became Brex.
- Goat (originally "Grubwithus," a group-dinner social startup) took about two years post-YC to pivot into a sneaker marketplace after its founders realized their own passion for limited-edition sneaker collecting pointed at a real, underserved market, a pivot that looked deeply unconventional in 2015 but is described as being "years ahead of consensus."
- Tom Blomfield's first company (a college bill-splitting app, a classic fintech "tar pit") pivoted into GoCardless after Paul Graham told the founders to stop building features and just go get users; two weeks of cold-calling UK sports club treasurers at 3-4am produced only one new user, which was the clear signal it was time to redirect the same payments infrastructure toward small-business recurring payments instead.
- Clipboard Health (originally an "Indeed for nurses" hiring product) took years in the "idea maze" before its founder discovered, through direct hospital selling, that the real acute pain was last-minute nurse absences forcing hospitals to expensive staffing agencies; she deliberately avoided writing much software until she had manually solved the matching problem herself.
- "Pivot hell" describes companies that change their entire idea every week rather than genuinely iterating; it's linked to perfectionism (wanting to see the whole path mapped out before committing) rather than accepting that real learning only comes from pushing one idea forward and hitting real obstacles. One company deferred its own demo day for lack of anything to present, then found a strong idea (productizing a regulatory change) roughly six months later, built on the market knowledge accumulated during the earlier failed attempts.
- Escher Reality (an AR SDK company later acquired by Niantic) initially mistook enthusiasm from marketers for real demand; the signal that exposed the mistake was asking directly whether they would pay, since polite excitement evaporated the moment money entered the conversation, and the company found real traction only after shifting toward selling to game developers instead.
- Creative Market (originally "Color Lovers," a design community with over a million members) spent about a year and a half after YC trying unrepeatable, non-scalable monetization experiments (software sales, ads, contests) without ever noticing they weren't working, because they had no single primary KPI to track; once they launched a clear marketplace with an obvious daily number to watch, the founder became "obsessed," checking it every 15 minutes, and the clarity itself accelerated the path to product-market fit.
- The shared framing across every story: founders, not ideas or locations, are the fixed point in a pivot, comparable to "location, location, location" in real estate but restated as "founders, founders, founders." The same team can rebuild a company from the studs into something that looks completely different, and often becomes more formidable once working on an idea they're genuinely well-suited for and excited about.
