# Fundraising Fundamentals By Geoff Ralston

> **Key principle:** Fundraising is a strange, irrational, seldom-fair market where you will hear far more "no"s than "yes"s, even for the startups that eventually succeed. The best time to raise is when you don't actually need the money, since investors can smell desperation, and the whole process is just one step toward the real goal: building something people want.

*Geoff Ralston (former YC president) on fundraising fundamentals, Y Combinator Startup School · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gcevHkNGrWQ) · 53 min.*

## Key lessons

- Venture capital exists because building a high-growth company almost always requires capital for hiring, growth, and runway; even Hewlett-Packard, often cited as the archetypal bootstrap story, started with just $538 and grew slowly by comparison to what venture-backed growth later made possible.
- Raise when you don't need the money, not when you're desperate for it: investors want to back big opportunities with momentum, and a founder who visibly needs the check loses leverage immediately; having money and options is itself a competitive advantage even before it's spent.
- A rule of thumb for sizing a raise: assume this may be the last money you're ever able to raise, and size it to reach either profitability or a milestone persuasive enough to raise the next round, typically budgeting around 18 months of runway at seed stage, based on real headcount and salary math (roughly $15,000/month cited as a sample engineer cost at the time).
- Investors are betting on two things: the founders themselves (are you formidable enough to turn an idea into a large company) and the story (a large opportunity, a compelling product/traction, and a credible teller); traction is the easiest case to sell, but the best investors specifically want to get in before traction exists, when the company is cheapest and least proven, since that's when returns are highest.
- Choosing too high a valuation/cap is a real, sometimes fatal risk: an investor who's interested at one price often walks when told a lower price later ("you're cheaper, so you're not as good"), so overreaching on valuation can quietly kill a fundraise rather than helping it.
- SAFEs (specifically YC's post-money SAFE) are strange but useful: they represent a promise of future equity rather than actual shares yet, are typically just 3-5 pages versus the many-page legal apparatus of a priced equity round, cost hundreds of dollars instead of requiring extensive lawyering, and, in their post-money form, make a founder's real dilution mathematically clear and calculable at the moment of signing rather than ambiguous until conversion.
- Practical pitch mechanics: capture attention in the first minute, bring a demo or at least a prototype (Steve Jobs reportedly demanded wooden mockups of the first iPad before anything worked), listen at least as much as you talk, and treat every investor meeting as practice, since pitching quality compounds with repetition the same way a golf swing improves only with feedback.
- On negotiation: understand what different investor types actually care about (an angel doesn't want to feel foolish, a VC cares about ownership percentage), keep multiple live conversations going for real leverage, and use delay ("let me check with my co-founder") rather than trying to out-negotiate professionals who do this full time.
- Don't exaggerate facts or hide problems, since investors are specifically skilled at detecting this and a damaged reputation follows a founder into future fundraises; a bold, ambitious vision of the future is fine and expected, but it must stay credible, not exaggerated data.
- On "default alive vs. default dead" framing for traction: growth rate matters more than absolute revenue (a VC would rather back $100K in revenue growing fast than $1M flat for five years), and long-term, multi-year financial projections from a pre-traction company are treated as a red flag about the investor asking for them, not a sign of founder rigor, since realistic error bars at that stage are enormous.
