# Kevin Hale - How to Pitch Your Startup

> **Key principle:** A pitch's job is not to sell the investor; a good investor already imagines the optimistic path to a billion-dollar outcome on their own once they understand what you're building. A founder's only job is to describe the company clearly enough (in concrete nouns, without jargon or hedging) that the investor can do that imagining accurately.

*Kevin Hale (Wufoo co-founder, YC partner) on pitching your startup, Y Combinator Startup School · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=17XZGUX_9iM) · 28 min.*

## Key lessons

- Investors evaluate a pitch against three simple questions: do I understand it, am I excited by it, and do I like the team. Everything else in a pitch is in service of making those three answers easy to reach quickly.
- Word of mouth, not marketing spend, is what actually grows the best companies, and word of mouth depends entirely on legibility: a description has to be understood before anyone can remember or repeat it to someone else.
- Five things make a pitch illegible and should be cut: ambiguity, complexity (mixing multiple ideas into one description), mystery or vagueness, jargon and buzzwords that get mentally filtered out, and any padded, defensive language a founder adds to preempt objections.
- Make the idea reproducible by naming three concrete nouns: what you're making, the problem, and the customer. Airbnb's actual YC application description, "the first online marketplace that lets travelers book rooms with locals instead of hotels," is held up as the model: concise, concrete, and immediately picturable.
- "X for Y" framing (Uber for X, Airbnb for X) only works under three conditions: X must be a household-name, unambiguously successful company; the target market Y must clearly want that same model; and the resulting Y segment must still be a large enough market on its own. "Buffer for Snapchat" is cited as a failure because Buffer is smaller and less iconic than Snapchat.
- The recommended editing process: strip a description down to its bare nouns first (product, problem, customer), then only add back modifiers, adjectives, or technology terms like "AI" or "IoT" if they meaningfully change how exciting or differentiated the idea sounds; unnecessary buzzwords often signal to an investor that the founder started from a solution rather than a real problem.
- Concision is itself a signal of competence: a founder who can describe their company in very few, well-chosen words demonstrates they've thought deeply about the idea and are likely just as efficient in execution as they are in explanation.
- Lead with "what," not "why" or "how": origin stories, mission statements, and narrative preambles slow an investor down and get in the way of the clarity that actually earns their attention inside a stack of hundreds of applications.
