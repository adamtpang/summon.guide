# Top Ways Startups Waste Money

> **Key principle:** Nearly every early-stage money mistake, chasing a big-company hire, running paid ads too early, hiring a PR agency, customizing legal contracts, or taking on equity-hungry advisors, follows the same pattern: it looks like the "professional" move that bigger companies make, but it's premature before real product-market fit. Earn the right to spend by testing the cheap version yourself first.

*Michael Seibel, Harj Taggar, and Brad Flora on where early founders waste money, Y Combinator group partner roundtable · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BtzUo6vL3Iw) · 28 min.*

## Key lessons

- Chasing a "dream" big-company hire (FAANG-caliber engineer or salesperson) is a common trap the partners call "Sebastianism," after a Portuguese myth of a lost king returning to save everything: that person's productivity at a huge company depended on infrastructure and process your startup doesn't have, even though you're paying near their old salary.
- Replacing one real hire with an army of contractors rarely works, since contractors lack skin in the game and have different incentives; founders should expect to personally learn unfamiliar skills (like building a first iOS app) rather than assume someone will always be available to hire for it.
- Paid ads are dangerously easy to over-invest in: one panelist describes ramping Triple Byte to nearly $1M/month in Facebook ad spend, profitable for years, until the channel stopped working all at once and a new product had to replace it entirely, a transition he says should have happened years earlier.
- Event sponsorships and brand marketing are usually "cargo culting" bigger companies pre-brand; cheaper, scrappier stunts often outperform paid booths, illustrated by the classic story of dumping a block of ice with money frozen inside outside a PayPal conference to dramatize "PayPal freezes your funds."
- PR agency retainers (examples cited run $10K-$50K/month) are called out as an almost universal early mistake, often producing generic pitches from junior staff who don't understand the product; journalists are said to actually want a direct relationship with the founder building the thing, not an intermediary.
- On legal spend: don't customize standard agreements (one host describes burning $50K having a first-year associate "learn" employment law on his dime), always get an upfront quote before work begins, and use large firms' interest-free payment plans to smooth spiky legal bills and preserve runway.
- Paid or equity-compensated advisors rarely move the needle; the best advice usually comes free from people who volunteer it. Founders are encouraged to push back on advisors demanding equity, including asking them to invest cash instead, one anecdote describes a professor's equity ask dropping from 30% to 1% simply by being challenged.
- The unifying rule: don't spend real money on hiring, marketing, PR, or events until product-market fit is confirmed and customers are already trying to pull the product out of your hands; before that point, the cheapest do-it-yourself version is nearly always the right test.
