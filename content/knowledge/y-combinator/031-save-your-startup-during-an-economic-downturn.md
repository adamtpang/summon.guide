# Save Your Startup During an Economic Downturn

> **Key principle:** Paul Graham's "default alive vs. default dead" test is binary: if not one more dollar of outside funding ever arrives, does your current growth rate reach profitability before the bank account hits zero? Being "default alive" is not the same as being profitable today, and founders resist running this math because admitting the answer might be no feels like admitting weakness to their team and investors.

*Michael Seibel and Dalton Caldwell on surviving an economic downturn using Paul Graham's default alive/dead framework, Y Combinator "Dalton + Michael" series · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0OVSTWozvfY) · 36 min.*

## Key lessons

- "Default alive" means a company's current growth rate will carry it to profitability before its cash runs out, even if zero additional outside money ever arrives; "default dead" is the only alternative, there's no third category, which is exactly why founders avoid running the calculation honestly.
- Founders systematically overestimate how easy the next fundraise will be, unconsciously assuming later rounds work like the last one, when in reality each subsequent stage (seed to Series A to Series B) has fewer available investors and higher bars, and macro conditions (interest rates, market sentiment) are entirely outside a founder's control and can shift fast.
- Media coverage badly distorts founders' sense of base rates: TechCrunch and similar outlets only cover successful raises, so founders systematically underestimate how often fundraises fail, and equally underestimate how rarely a dying, cash-poor company gets acquired, since buyers have little incentive to acquire a company that will soon be bankrupt anyway (a real example: a well-known startup's team was reportedly acquired for effectively nothing once it ran out of runway).
- Default-alive companies have dramatically more leverage in every fundraising conversation: not needing the money makes a pitch more convincing, and experienced investors can sense whether a company is fundraising from strength or from desperation, sometimes responding to visible desperation with worse terms rather than better ones.
- Investor and founder incentives are structurally different, not aligned by default: from a portfolio perspective, a VC benefits from encouraging many companies to burn fast and grow explosively, since either the company breaks out spectacularly or it dies quickly and frees up the VC's limited board-seat capacity, whereas the founder is the one who has to personally live with a fatal, avoidable burn decision.
- The "fatal pinch" (from a related Paul Graham essay) describes the common trap of increasing burn specifically to hit a growth number a founder believes investors need to see, even as each dollar of that spend becomes less effective and payback periods stretch out; by the time a founder finally seeks help, they are usually already down to a few months of runway, past the point where a graceful correction is still possible.
- The three concrete levers to fix a default-dead position, roughly in order of impact: cut headcount (the single biggest lever, since people costs dominate most burn spreadsheets, not office perks), cut acquisition/ad spend (even if it means missing a growth target, since scaling unprofitable ad spend to "prove" growth just accelerates the crash), and raise prices (even if unit economics were designed around underpricing, since selling at a loss to hit volume numbers is structurally unsustainable).
- Justin.tv/Twitch's own near-death story: with roughly $500K left in the bank, $750K/month in revenue against $1M/month in expenses (a $250K/month burn), the founders held a company-wide meeting to admit the company might die, then spent weeks cutting costs and adding revenue (including letting people go and adding pre-roll ads across the platform) until the company reached breakeven within about two months and generated $1.2M in profit within about four months, the same period from which the Twitch pivot idea eventually emerged.
- Taking a deliberate short-term hit to growth (or revenue) to reach default-alive status is compared favorably to declaring personal bankruptcy: painful in the moment, but startups are judged on a rolling recent window (roughly the last 6-18 months), not on the fact that the business was once badly mismanaged, so a founder who corrects course early gets to be judged on the healthier company that follows.
