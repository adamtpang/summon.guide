# Eric Migicovsky - How to Talk to Users

> **Key principle:** Talking to users is, alongside building the product, one of only two things YC insists a founder cannot outsource. Run interviews using "The Mom Test" discipline: never pitch, never ask hypotheticals, and mostly listen, extracting specific facts about problems the person has already lived through rather than opinions about a future feature.

*Eric Migicovsky (Pebble founder, YC partner) on talking to users, Y Combinator Startup School · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MT4Ig2uqjTc) · 32 min.*

## Key lessons

- The three classic interview mistakes, drawn from the book "The Mom Test": pitching your idea instead of extracting information, asking hypothetical questions ("would you use this feature?") instead of asking about specifics that already happened, and talking too much instead of listening and taking notes.
- Five reliable interview questions: what's the hardest part about doing the thing you're trying to solve, tell me about the last time you ran into this problem, why was that hard, what have you already tried to solve it, and what don't you love about the solutions you've already tried; each is designed to surface facts, not opinions.
- To find the best first customer, score each candidate numerically on three axes: how much the problem costs them today, how frequently they encounter it, and how much budget or authority they have to solve it. The founder is warned off targets who feel the pain often but can't afford or authorize a fix, versus a customer who scores high on all three.
- At the idea stage, start by interviewing yourself, then move to warm introductions among friends and co-workers; a YC company selling to firefighters found more success simply showing up unannounced at fire stations than cold emailing, and walked away with dozens of 10-15 minute meetings.
- Keep early interviews short (10-15 minutes is often enough), take detailed notes or bring a co-founder to record, and don't wait to start until you have a "proper" process. The first five to ten interviews teach far more than trying to batch a hundred at once.
- Once iterating toward product-market fit, borrow Superhuman's technique: ask users weekly how they'd feel if they could no longer use the product (very disappointed / somewhat disappointed / not disappointed), and treat roughly 40% or more answering "very disappointed" as a real, quantitative product-market-fit signal instead of a vague retrospective feeling.
- Collect phone numbers at signup so that when the aggregate data is confusing, you can always call a specific real user and ask what happened directly, rather than only guessing from dashboards.
- Discard "bad data" in interviews: generic compliments ("I love this!") and enthusiastic hypotheticals about future features are not actionable; only specific facts about a real, already-encountered problem should change the roadmap.
