# Chapter 11: The Holy Family

> **Key principle:** Away from the boardroom, Rockefeller built an insular, God-fearing family life centered on Forest Hill and the Euclid Avenue Baptist Church, doting on his fragile only son while his family also absorbed the strains of a bigamist father and an openly hostile younger brother.

*Synthesized from Chapter 11 of Titan: The Life of John D. Rockefeller, Sr. by Ron Chernow.*

## Key lessons

- In 1873 Rockefeller bought 79 wooded acres east of Cleveland called Forest Hill; after a failed plan to build a homeopathic sanatorium there collapsed in the 1870s depression, he repurchased the property and, starting in 1877, made it the family's summer home, eventually expanding it to more than 700 acres with up to 136 employees; the family moved there full-time as their Cleveland residence after relocating to New York in late 1883.
- Rockefeller preferred plain comfort over show; he stripped Forest Hill of heavy curtains, avoided "fancy tiling" and other extravagances, and spent his energy landscaping (twenty miles of roadway, artificial lakes for boating and swimming, a private trotting track, a quarry) rather than decorating the interior.
- Their first son, John D. Rockefeller Jr. ("Junior"), was born January 29, 1874, delivered by Dr. Myra Herrick; he was a sickly child, raised almost entirely among women (three older sisters, his mother Cettie, and grandmother), and later described his boyhood self as "shy, ill-adjusted and frail." His father was patient and rarely showed anger, but was stingy with praise and used everyday moments (like a Sunday-school giving plan) to teach lessons about interest and thrift.
- Cettie ran the household with a strict, moralizing hand, banning square dancing as "promiscuous" and having ten-year-old Junior sign a pledge to abstain from tobacco, profanity, and alcohol; she once told a neighbor she was glad her son had named a Christmas wish "so now it can be denied him."
- The family remained devoted to the working-class Euclid Avenue Baptist Church (formerly the Erie Street Baptist Mission) rather than a fashionable congregation; by the early 1880s Rockefeller covered roughly half its annual budget and required his children to earn their own 20-cent weekly church contributions through chores. He served as Sunday school superintendent from 1872 to 1905 and forbade any talk of business or stock tips during class.
- Rockefeller and Cettie were zealous in the temperance movement: Cettie's parents, the Spelmans, campaigned against Brooklyn's roughly 2,500 "rum shops," and Cettie herself joined Women's Christian Temperance Union raids into Cleveland saloons, praying over drinkers; John financed the Central Friendly Inn, an early settlement-house-style temperance outpost.
- Rockefeller's estranged father, "Big Bill" (living under the alias "Dr. William Levingston" with his second, bigamous family in Illinois), made occasional unannounced visits to Forest Hill, delighting the grandchildren with fiddle music, marksmanship lessons, and gifts, while Rockefeller himself remained coldly civil and never publicly acknowledged his father's double life.
- Rockefeller's younger brother Frank became an open enemy: after selling his refinery interest in the 1872 "Cleveland Massacre," Frank mismanaged a fleet of lake boats for Standard Oil, gambled heavily, married into the family of rival refiner William Scofield, and in 1876 testified before Congress accusing John of threatening competitors that refusing to sell would leave their property "valueless." Frank later co-founded a rival Cleveland refinery, the Pioneer Oil Works (1878), and conspired with independent refiners trying to sue Standard Oil.
- Despite the public feud, John forced a secret 1876 deal binding Scofield's new firm (Scofield, Shurmer and Teagle) to a Standard Oil-set production quota, communicated via a secret post office box; when the firm exceeded its quota, Standard Oil sued, and in 1880 a Cleveland judge ruled the quota arrangement an illegal restraint of trade.
- The chapter also sketches Rockefeller's sisters: Lucy died in 1878 at 40; Mary Ann became a reclusive, penny-pinching hermit despite her husband's wealth; and their contrasting temperaments underscored how unevenly the family absorbed both Eliza's piety and Big Bill's restlessness, with John the most like his mother and Frank most like his father.
