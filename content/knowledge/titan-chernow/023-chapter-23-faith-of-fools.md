# Chapter 23: Faith of Fools

> **Key principle:** Rockefeller saw himself as a steward of a God-given fortune, not a sinner buying absolution, and Frederick Gates steered that money into medical research because it was popular, apolitical, and nearly guaranteed not to embarrass its founder.

*Synthesized from Chapter 23 of Titan: The Life of John D. Rockefeller, Sr. by Ron Chernow.*

## Key lessons

- Chernow frames Rockefeller as two seemingly irreconcilable men at once: the demonized monopolist of the Tarbell exposes and the increasingly devoted philanthropist, and argues Rockefeller himself felt no sense of atonement in the shift, believing his fortune was a divine trust to be dispensed responsibly.
- Politics as much as piety drove the timing: as public attacks intensified in the early 1900s, Rockefeller needed to prove, on a much larger scale than before, that a rich businessman could dispense wealth honorably, which pushed his giving toward broad, uncontroversial causes and away from narrow denominational or partisan groups.
- Rockefeller was haunted by fear of fostering dependency, telling associates he did not want to "encourage a horde of irresponsible, adventuresome fellows," and for that reason avoided direct relief (alms, libraries, recreation halls in the Carnegie mold) in favor of funding research and education meant to strengthen people to compete.
- The Rockefeller Institute for Medical Research (RIMR) originated with Frederick Gates's obsessive 1897 summer reading of William Osler's textbook Principles and Practice of Medicine, which convinced him American medicine was mired in guesswork; he pitched Rockefeller a research institute modeled on Paris's Pasteur Institute and Berlin's Koch Institute.
- Founded in June 1901 with a modest initial pledge of $200,000 over ten years and no fanfare, the RIMR was deliberately launched small, in a rented Lexington Avenue loft, to avoid repeating the runaway expectations and budget battles Rockefeller had endured with the University of Chicago.
- Simon Flexner, recruited by his mentor Dr. William Welch of Johns Hopkins, became the Institute's founding director; when Flexner asked Gates why he was sure the venture would yield discoveries, Gates answered that he had "the faith of fools," the line that gives the chapter its title.
- Rockefeller stayed personally remote from the institute for years, once sitting outside its new York Avenue building (built on a 13-acre tract bought for $660,000) and telling his son, "No, I can see the outside," yet Gates reported he wept with joy over its breakthroughs and tracked its work closely from a distance.
- The institute delivered real triumphs that built public goodwill during Standard Oil's antitrust fight: Simon Flexner's serum for cerebrospinal meningitis, developed after a 1904-05 epidemic killed roughly 3,000 New Yorkers, and Alexis Carrel's 1912 Nobel Prize, the first ever awarded to research done in America, for pioneering vascular suturing after saving a premature infant's life via a father-to-child blood transfusion in 1909.
- Even as he bankrolled cutting-edge scientific medicine, Rockefeller privately clung to homeopathy through his golf partner Dr. Hamilton Biggar, nearly withholding a $500,000 gift to a fire-damaged Johns Hopkins Medical School over the issue, while Gates worked behind the scenes to discredit homeopathy entirely, an irony Chernow underscores explicitly.
- Rockefeller ultimately gave $61 million to the RIMR, which became Rockefeller University in 1965 and housed sixteen Nobel laureates by the 1970s; Winston Churchill later called Rockefeller's endowment of research a milestone comparable to Renaissance patronage of the arts.
