# Chapter 28: Benevolent Trust

> **Key principle:** Pressed by advisor Frederick Gates's warning that his fortune was "rolling up like an avalanche," Rockefeller moved from ad hoc gifts to a permanent, professionally staffed foundation designed to give away money on the same industrial scale he had once used to make it.

*Synthesized from Chapter 28 of Titan: The Life of John D. Rockefeller, Sr. by Ron Chernow.*

## Key lessons

- In retirement Rockefeller filled his days with ritualized pleasures: daily 50-mile drives in a maroon 1918 Crane-Simplex touring car (chauffeur "Phillips"), golf, and orchestrated birthday parties for Pocantico village children with ice cream and a brass band. He still tracked drive times like race records.
- Security fears shadowed the idyll: in 1912 the Black Hand (a Sicilian/Italian-American extortion society) threatened him, prompting a hidden alarm system at Kykuit with a button under his pillow linked to lights in the trees for the night watchman.
- Gates's June 1906 letter warned that if Rockefeller didn't distribute his fortune faster than it grew, "it will crush you and your children and your children's children," and urged permanent "corporate philanthropies" so large that trusteeship itself would be a public office.
- Junior and Gates pursued a federal charter for the Rockefeller Foundation starting in 1908, but the effort collided with the concurrent federal antitrust suit against Standard Oil; Attorney General George Wickersham formally protested to President Taft in February 1911 that chartering the foundation while prosecuting Standard Oil was contradictory.
- A secret April 25, 1911 White House lunch, arranged by Senator Nelson Aldrich and entered through a side door to avoid the press, discussed the charter with Taft; Gates even proposed letting the President, the Chief Justice, and Ivy League presidents veto foundation board appointments to blunt criticism.
- After three stalled years in Congress, the Rockefellers abandoned the federal route and secured a New York State charter in 1913 instead, following the precedent of the 1911 Carnegie Corporation charter.
- On June 29, 1909, Rockefeller had already transferred 73,000 shares of Standard Oil of New Jersey (worth $50 million) to trustees Junior, Gates, and Harold McCormick as a first installment; he ultimately gave the Foundation $100 million in its first year and $182.8 million by 1919, making his lifetime giving (about $530 million) far exceed Andrew Carnegie's roughly $350 million.
- The Foundation's first trustee meeting was held May 19, 1913 at 26 Broadway with Junior as president; Rockefeller, though a nominal trustee for ten years, never attended a single meeting, ceding day-to-day control while retaining an informal veto.
- The Foundation focused overwhelmingly on public health and medicine to avoid controversy: it funded Wickliffe Rose's International Health Commission (hookworm and yellow fever eradication across 52 countries), public-health schools at Johns Hopkins (1918) and Harvard (1921), and the Peking Union Medical College in China (opened 1921), the era's largest single medical philanthropy project.
- Chernow frames this as Rockefeller's second great innovation: just as he had industrialized oil refining, he now industrialized philanthropy, shifting giving away from naming buildings toward funding the creation of scientific and medical knowledge itself.
