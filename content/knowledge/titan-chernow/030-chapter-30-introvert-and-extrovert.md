# Chapter 30: Introvert and Extrovert

> **Key principle:** While Rockefeller mourned his wife Laura "Cettie" Spelman with genuine tenderness, his daughter Edith spent nearly a decade abroad under Carl Jung's influence trying, and largely failing, to break free of the same emotional walls Jung diagnosed in her father.

*Synthesized from Chapter 30 of Titan: The Life of John D. Rockefeller, Sr. by Ron Chernow.*

## Key lessons

- Cettie had been chronically ill for years (pneumonia, shingles, pernicious anemia, sciatica, lumbago, congestive heart failure) and largely refused to consult doctors at the Rockefeller Institute; she died at Pocantico on March 12, 1915, with Rockefeller in Ormond Beach, Florida when he got the telegrams. Junior and Abby said they had never before seen Senior openly weeping.
- A bitter, unrelated Cleveland tax dispute (Cuyahoga County assessed Rockefeller $1.5 million after Cettie's illness forced him to overstay the February residency deadline) meant he could not risk crossing into Ohio to bury her; Cettie's casket was held for four and a half months in the Archbold family mausoleum at Sleepy Hollow Cemetery, then smuggled to Cleveland in a covert, decoy-casket operation orchestrated with an undertaker named Vanderbilt.
- Cettie's estate inventory revealed extreme personal frugality despite the family's billions: her most expensive item of clothing was a $150 seal coat, and she had never replaced her original 1864 gold wedding ring, appraised at $3.
- In 1918 Rockefeller endowed the Laura Spelman Rockefeller Memorial with $74 million in his wife's memory; under Beardsley Ruml it poured nearly $50 million into social-science research starting in 1922 before being absorbed into the Rockefeller Foundation in 1929.
- Daughter Edith Rockefeller McCormick, married to Harold McCormick of International Harvester, suffered severe agoraphobia and a "crisis of religious faith" and in 1912 began analysis with Carl Jung in Zurich, moving there in April 1913 and remaining largely for the next eight years while her children were parceled into Swiss schools and sanatoriums.
- Jung diagnosed Edith with "latent schizophrenia" and privately loathed Rockefeller, telling associates that "thousands of decent human beings have been destroyed by the Standard Oil Trust"; he also classified Edith as an introvert like her father and Harold as an extrovert.
- Edith poured huge sums into Jungian causes: roughly $120,000 to found Zurich's Psychological Club, funding for English translations of Jung's work, and a monthly stipend to James Joyce (from February 1918) that ended after about eighteen months, reportedly after Joyce declined her push that he undergo analysis with Jung.
- Edith's finances spiraled: a failed 1919 wood-hardening investment cost her $340,000, she gave away a $300,000 property for a Cook County zoo without consulting family, and by 1920 her debts reached $812,000, forcing Rockefeller to bail her out with Standard Oil of New Jersey stock.
- Harold McCormick, left alone in Chicago, took up with opera singer Ganna Walska; Edith's own relationship with a much younger Austrian, Edwin Krenn, contributed to their 1921 divorce, in which Edith received no alimony and had to pay Harold $2.7 million.
- Edith and a new partner, Dato, launched a real-estate venture called Krenn and Dato, including a planned 1,500-acre millionaires' resort on Lake Michigan called "Edithon"; it collapsed in the 1929 crash, and Edith never recouped the losses. She and her father exchanged affectionate letters for the rest of her life but saw each other only rarely after 1913, illustrating Chernow's point that even psychoanalysis could not fully unseat the emotional reserve she'd inherited from Rockefeller himself.
