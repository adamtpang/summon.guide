# Chapter 17: Captains of Erudition

> **Key principle:** Buried under an avalanche of personal appeals for money, Rockefeller used the founding of the University of Chicago to work out the principles of large-scale, professionally managed philanthropy, hiring Frederick T. Gates as his first full-time advisor and learning, through years of conflict with university president William Rainey Harper's overspending, that even generosity required the same discipline he applied to Standard Oil.

*Synthesized from Chapter 17 of Titan: The Life of John D. Rockefeller, Sr. by Ron Chernow.*

## Key lessons

- By the late 1880s, Rockefeller was so besieged by begging letters (one educational-gift announcement drew fifteen thousand letters in a week and fifty thousand within a month) that he described the pressure of vetting appeals personally as more taxing than running Standard Oil itself, and it pushed him toward a systematic, "wholesale" approach to giving.
- Reverend Augustus H. Strong, president of Rochester Theological Seminary, spent years pressing Rockefeller to fund a $20 million elite Baptist university on Morningside Heights in New York; the bond was reinforced when Rockefeller's daughter Bessie married Strong's son Charles on March 22, 1889, though Rockefeller ultimately rejected the New York plan.
- Thomas W. Goodspeed and thirty-year-old Bible scholar William Rainey Harper (a prodigy who earned his Ph.D. at eighteen) steered Rockefeller instead toward reviving a defunct University of Chicago, while Frederick T. Gates, executive secretary of the new American Baptist Education Society, made the decisive case in an October 15, 1888 report on the need for a Baptist university in the fast-growing Midwest.
- At a May 1889 meeting of Baptist leaders in Boston's Tremont Temple, Gates announced Rockefeller's pledge of $600,000 (about $9.5 million today) toward the Chicago school, contingent on another $400,000 being raised elsewhere, triggering an outburst of cheering and hymn-singing among the assembled ministers.
- Marshall Field donated a ten-acre parcel on Chicago's South Side in January 1890, near the future site of the 1893 World's Columbian Exposition, and Harper accepted the university presidency in February 1891 after Rockefeller agreed to fund an additional $1 million to relocate the Morgan Park Theological Seminary onto the new campus.
- The university opened October 1, 1892, with 750 students and a faculty Harper had assembled in barely a year through aggressive Ivy League raids, hiring figures such as John Dewey, George Herbert Mead, and Thorstein Veblen (who later mocked Harper in print as a "captain of erudition," a phrase Chernow borrows for the chapter title) and creating America's first graduate sociology department under Albion Small.
- Harper's chronic overspending strained the relationship for years: Rockefeller had to cover a budget shortfall after the Panic of 1893 with an extra $500,000 that October, and in October 1895 he pledged a further $3 million, news that coach Amos Alonzo Stagg had announced to the football team at halftime, reportedly inspiring a 22-12 comeback win over Wisconsin.
- The strain of philanthropy and business combined to wreck Rockefeller's health around 1890-91: plagued by digestive trouble and exhaustion, he spent eight months recuperating at Forest Hill in 1891, and in March of that year he hired Frederick T. Gates as his first full-time philanthropic advisor, a turning point that professionalized Rockefeller giving (his annual donations jumped from about $124,000 in 1889 to $1.35 million in 1892).
- By early 1897, with the university $200,000 in debt, Rockefeller cut off Harper's direct financial appeals, installed Gates and his son John D. Rockefeller Jr. on the board for oversight, and required advance notice of major expenditures, formalizing the fiscal discipline Harper had resisted for years.
- Rockefeller visited the campus in person only rarely, most notably for the July 1897 first-class quinquennial celebration, where he told the crowd the university was "the best investment I ever made in my life"; true to his preference for anonymous giving, he declined to have any campus building named after himself during his lifetime.
