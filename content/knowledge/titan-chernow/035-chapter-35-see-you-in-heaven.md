# Chapter 35: See You in Heaven

> **Key principle:** Rockefeller died in his sleep on May 23, 1937, six weeks short of 98, having given away roughly $530 million in his lifetime; Chernow's final verdict is that he was neither pure villain nor pure saint but a single man in whom ruthless monopoly-building and history's greatest philanthropy were two faces of the same relentless character.

*Synthesized from Chapter 35 of Titan: The Life of John D. Rockefeller, Sr. by Ron Chernow, the book's closing chapter.*

## Key lessons

- Rockefeller's frugality never faded: he once had his butler cut firewood from 14 inches to 12 to save money, and in 1930 he took legal action to stop a promoter from moving his dismantled Richford, New York birth house to Coney Island as a paid tourist attraction.
- He kept speculating in the stock market into his nineties, borrowing money from Junior for trades and once joking to him "do you think you are old enough to use it wisely?" when Junior teased him back; after the October 1929 crash, Ivy Lee scripted a public reassurance statement ("Believing that the fundamental conditions of the country are sound, my son and I have been purchasing sound common stocks") that prompted Eddie Cantor's quip, "Sure, who else had any money left?"
- Junior's finances were also hit hard: his net worth fell from nearly $1 billion in 1929 to under $500 million by 1934, forcing him to borrow roughly $8 million to meet prior charitable pledges. His riskiest venture, a Manhattan real-estate project originally tied to a new Metropolitan Opera House, collapsed into a solo undertaking after the Opera withdrew; Junior personally financed and leased it out, eventually naming it Rockefeller Center.
- Rockefeller Center opened its RCA Building in 1933 (family offices moved to the 56th floor, "Room 5600"), employed 75,000 union construction workers during the Depression, and became profitable by 1938 under Nelson's leadership as president; the 1933 controversy over Diego Rivera's mural (destroyed after Rivera refused to remove a portrait of Lenin) became a major public flashpoint.
- Edith McCormick died of cancer on August 25, 1932 at Chicago's Drake Hotel, still financially ruined; her will left five-twelfths of her estate to Edwin Krenn (more than to any of her three children), a bequest the Rockefeller lawyers fought down to a $24,000 annuity.
- In 1934, Junior placed $102 million into trusts for Abby and his six children (Abby getting the largest share, $18.3 million, with freedom to spend on modern art) ahead of a steep new gift-tax increase, describing it as continuing "the policy which your Grandfather Rockefeller adopted."
- Rockefeller's health declined steadily in his final years: he abandoned Kykuit permanently for The Casements in Florida around 1934, stopped golfing after a 1932 illness, and turned 96 on July 8, 1935, collecting a $5 million payout from his own life insurance policy under an old actuarial-longevity clause.
- He died at The Casements at 4:05 a.m. on May 23, 1937, of sclerotic myocarditis, after a final exchange of banter with Fanny Evans and paying off the mortgage of the Euclid Avenue Baptist Church that same day. He was buried at Lake View Cemetery in Cleveland between Eliza and Cettie, his casket sealed in a bombproof tomb against vandalism.
- He left an estate of $26.4 million (having recouped much of his 1929 losses), and his death drew laudatory obituaries across the political spectrum, including from attorney Samuel Untermyer, who had once cross-examined him, calling him "World Citizen No. 1."
- Chernow's closing judgment: Rockefeller embodied an unresolved American contradiction, Puritan thrift twisted into monopoly and ruthless competitive tactics, redeemed by philanthropy on a scale ($530 million lifetime, with descendants giving hundreds of millions more) that reshaped medicine, education, and science; Chernow calls him simultaneously "the fiercest robber baron" and "the foremost philanthropist," arguing his story proves that free markets need government-set rules and that organized, professional philanthropy, which he essentially invented, could turn even ill-gotten wealth toward genuine public good.
