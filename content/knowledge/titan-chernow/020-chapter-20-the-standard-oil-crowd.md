# Chapter 20: The Standard Oil Crowd

> **Key principle:** Rockefeller's retirement-era fortune was shaped by two very different forces: the disciplined, minister-turned-financier Frederick Gates, who exposed and untangled years of fraudulent investments and built the fortune anew through the Mesabi iron-ore venture, and the flamboyant "Standard Oil Crowd" of Henry Rogers, James Stillman, and brother William, whose Wall Street speculations (culminating in the Amalgamated Copper scandal) were falsely attributed to John D. himself.

*Synthesized from Chapter 20 of Titan: The Life of John D. Rockefeller, Sr. by Ron Chernow.*

## Key lessons

- Frederick T. Gates, a former Baptist minister who had lost his young wife and his faith in conventional ministry, joined Rockefeller in 1891 (starting at $4,000 a year, rising to $32,000 by 1902) and became his indispensable philanthropic and investment adviser, later called by Rockefeller "the greatest businessman he had ever known" ahead of Flagler, Archbold, Ford, or Carnegie.
- Gates discovered that Rockefeller's personal fortune outside Standard Oil had been badly mismanaged by two trusted Baptist churchgoers, Colgate Hoyt and Charles Colby, who had steered him into worthless iron-ore "flyers" in Alabama and Wisconsin (real-estate scams disguised as mining ventures) and a phantom Colorado gold mine, San Miguel Consolidated; Gates ultimately uncovered some twenty failing companies, all "showing a balance sheet in red ink."
- Once installed at 26 Broadway with unrestricted access to Rockefeller's outside files, Gates became president of thirteen distressed companies Rockefeller was forced to take over, and George Rogers pushed Rockefeller to formalize an executive committee (Gates on investments, Starr Murphy on legal matters, Rogers on office affairs) so he would never again be "robbed" through passive trust in friends.
- Rockefeller was an eccentric but disciplined investor: he kept a minimum $10 million cash balance, bought on an eighth-point decline and sold on an eighth-point rise, used a maze of secondary brokers to hide his identity, refused to give stock tips after being sued twice for bad advice, and was famously forgiving as a lender, "never known to call a private loan, foreclose a private mortgage, or oppress a debtor," per Gates.
- The so-called "Standard Oil Crowd" on Wall Street, popularly but wrongly assumed to be run by John D. himself, was really the domain of his brother William Rockefeller, Henry H. Rogers, and James Stillman of National City Bank; in 1899 the trio secretly bought Anaconda Copper with a $39 million loan and relaunched it as Amalgamated Copper, unloading it on the public for $75 million and pocketing a $36 million profit, a scheme John D. never invested in and privately resented.
- Boston broker Thomas W. Lawson's serialized exposé "Frenzied Finance" (1904-05) blurred the Amalgamated affair with Rockefeller's name, cementing a public belief that John D. was behind it even though Lawson himself footnoted that Rockefeller "never put a dime" into the flotation; the episode permanently strained relations between John D. and Rogers, whom he confronted and effectively forced out of Standard Oil stock in a 1907 boardroom showdown over the Virginia Railway debts.
- Henry H. Rogers, nicknamed the "Savage Old Tiger" (or "Hell Hound Rogers" on Wall Street), had a dual reputation: ruthless in business but a devoted friend, famously rescuing Mark Twain from bankruptcy in 1893 with an $8,000 check and shrewd management of his royalties, and quietly funding much of Helen Keller's education at Radcliffe, later set up with a lifetime annuity.
- Almost by accident, through the failed Colby/Hoyt investments, Rockefeller became the dominant owner of Minnesota's Mesabi iron-ore range in the 1890s, rescuing the debt-ridden Merritt family ("the seven men of iron") during the 1893 panic; the relationship soured into lawsuits, settled in 1896-97 for $525,000, while Gates (through recruit LaMont Montgomery Bowers) built the Bessemer Steamship Company into the largest ore fleet on the Great Lakes, cutting shipping costs from $4.20 to 80 cents a ton.
- Andrew Carnegie initially mocked Rockefeller's ore investment as worthless, but by 1896 was forced into a landmark deal: Carnegie agreed to buy a minimum 600,000 tons of Rockefeller's Mesabi ore annually at 25 cents a ton and ship it (plus his own ore) on Rockefeller's railroads and vessels, while Rockefeller agreed not to build a competing steel mill.
- When J. P. Morgan assembled U.S. Steel in 1901, he had to buy out Rockefeller's Mesabi and shipping holdings; Rockefeller deliberately sent his 27-year-old son Junior to negotiate in his place, and Junior held his own against Morgan's blunt "what's your price?" opening, a moment his parents celebrated as proof he had finally come into his own. The Rockefellers received roughly $88.5 million (about $55 million of it pure profit), pushing Rockefeller's net worth past $200 million and making him America's second-richest man, behind only Carnegie.
- The chapter closes on Rockefeller's permanently ruptured relationship with his younger brother Frank, worsened by a bitter dispute over Frank's friend James Corrigan's Standard Oil stock (sold to Rockefeller at market price in 1895, then contested when the stock later rose); after a final confrontation around 1898, the brothers never spoke again, and Frank had his two deceased children's caskets removed from the family cemetery plot rather than let them "rest upon land controlled by that monster, John D. Rockefeller," later becoming a reliably quotable source for muckraking reporters.
