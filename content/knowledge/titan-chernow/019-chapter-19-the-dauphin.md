# Chapter 19: The Dauphin

> **Key principle:** Junior's Brown years and early apprenticeship at 26 Broadway trace a painfully shy heir learning, in fits and starts, to separate himself from his parents' suffocating moral perfectionism, with Abby Aldrich and a forgiven near-disaster in the stock market marking his two real turning points.

*Synthesized from Chapter 19 of Titan: The Life of John D. Rockefeller, Sr. by Ron Chernow.*

## Key lessons

- Junior chose Brown over Yale in 1893 largely out of social anxiety (fear of Yale's "fast set" and of being "lost in the crowd"), enrolling under Baptist college president E. Benjamin Andrews, who taught him practical ethics and a proto-Progressive sympathy for profit-sharing; Andrews was later forced out partly, Henry Demarest Lloyd claimed, because trustees feared Rockefeller would withhold university gifts if Andrews (a Bryan/free-silver supporter) stayed.
- Junior's frugality became campus legend: he pressed his own trousers, sewed his own buttons, soaked apart stuck two-cent stamps, and recorded every expense (including sodas bought for dates) in a little account book, prompting classmates to nickname his priggishness ("Here comes Johnny Rock, reeking with virtue and without one redeeming vice!").
- At Brown he cautiously tasted forbidden pleasures for the first time: theater in London (Shakespeare, Charley's Aunt), dancing (which his mother Cettie never fully accepted), and managing the football team, where his father Senior, attending his first-ever football game, got so excited he ran onto the sidelines in a silk hat.
- He met Abby Aldrich, daughter of powerful Rhode Island senator Nelson Aldrich (a protectionist dubbed "the political boss of the United States" by Lincoln Steffens, who amassed roughly $16 million partly via a $5 million sugar-trust loan), at a sophomore-year dance; her confidence and ease gave Junior, for the first time, "faith in myself that his parents couldn't foster."
- Junior started work at 26 Broadway on October 1, 1897, at an oak rolltop desk on the ninth floor working alongside Frederick T. Gates, drawing a $6,000 salary from his father and doing menial tasks (filling inkwells, picking wallpaper, arranging a granite obelisk for the family burial plot) since Senior deliberately gave him no instructions and let him "make his own way."
- In 1899, con man David Lamar manipulated Senior's secretary George Rogers into feeding Junior a fake tip that trader James Keene was buying U.S. Leather stock; Junior bought heavily and lost nearly $1 million (over $17 million in today's dollars) of his father's money. When he confessed, Rockefeller Sr. calmly investigated the facts, offered no reproach, and simply said "All right, I'll take care of it, John," a response that bound Junior's loyalty more than any punishment could have.
- Frederick T. Gates became the mentoring father-figure Junior lacked at home, touring iron ranges and timberland with him and getting him named to the University of Chicago board just three months into his job; by his twenties Junior also sat on the boards of U.S. Steel, National City Bank, and the Delaware, Lackawanna and Western Railroad.
- Junior courted Abby for four years with agonized indecision, undergoing a mother-imposed six-month separation to test his feelings in 1901; he finally proposed by moonlight at the Aldrich estate at Narragansett Bay after hearing what he described as God's voice affirming the choice, and they married at Warwick on October 9, 1901, with Senator Aldrich hosting a lavish, wine-soaked wedding that scandalized the temperance-minded Cettie, who took to her bed and skipped the ceremony.
- Muckrakers mocked the union as an alliance of exploiters: David Graham Phillips wrote that "the chief exploiter of the American people is closely allied by marriage with the chief schemer in the service of their exploiters," but the press generally treated Abby and Junior themselves as more sympathetic, progressive figures than their fathers.
- Cettie's letters to Junior reveal the near-religious pressure placed on him, at one point comparing Senior to God and Junior to the Christ child ("you are a prince, the Son of the King of kings"), reflecting how the family, under mounting public attack, had built an alternate moral universe in which Rockefeller was a saint rather than a robber baron.
