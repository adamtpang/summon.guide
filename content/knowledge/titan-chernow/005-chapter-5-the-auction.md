# Chapter 5: The Auction

> **Key principle:** Rockefeller entered oil refining almost by accident in 1863 through his partnership with chemist Samuel Andrews, then, unwilling to be constrained by his more cautious partners, staged a rigged-in-his-favor 1865 auction that won him sole control of Cleveland's largest refinery for $72,500, a moment he called the day that "determined my career."

*Synthesized from Chapter 5 of Titan: The Life of John D. Rockefeller, Sr. by Ron Chernow.*

## Key lessons

- Edwin Drake struck oil at Titusville, Pennsylvania on August 28, 1859, after Yale chemist Benjamin Silliman Jr. had confirmed in an 1855 report that western Pennsylvania's "rock oil" could be refined into a superior illuminant; the discovery triggered a chaotic land and drilling rush along Oil Creek that Rockefeller later called "a sort of gold-field rush."
- Rockefeller entered the oil business through his produce partner Maurice Clark's connection to Samuel Andrews, an English self-taught chemist who had distilled Cleveland's first oil-based kerosene around 1860; when Andrews pitched a refining venture, Clark initially refused but reversed himself when Rockefeller said he thought well of it. In 1863 Rockefeller and Clark committed $4,000 for half the capital of the new refinery, Andrews, Clark and Co. (the Excelsior Works on Kingsbury Run).
- Rockefeller made at least one direct trip to the Oil Regions around Titusville, Oil City, and Franklin, describing the boomtowns as lawless, filthy, and drink-soaked ("Sodden Gomorrah"), and came away convinced that refining, not the unpredictable business of drilling wells, was the safest and most strategically powerful position in the new industry.
- Relations soured badly with his partners: Rockefeller privately called Maurice Clark "an ignorant, conceited Englishman," clashed repeatedly over his aggressive borrowing to expand (at one point owing $100,000, which alarmed the more cautious Clark), and physically stood down an intimidation attempt by Maurice's brother James, a former prizefighter, telling him flatly, "you can knock my head off but you might as well understand that you can't scare me."
- In January 1865, spurred partly by news of a new gusher at Pithole Creek (which proved there was more oil to be found), Rockefeller secretly arranged financing and lined up Sam Andrews's loyalty, then deliberately provoked the Clark brothers into voting to dissolve the partnership, as required unanimously under their agreement, catching them by surprise when he immediately placed a public dissolution notice in the Cleveland Leader.
- The firm was auctioned to the highest bidder with the Clarks' lawyer serving as auctioneer; Rockefeller represented himself. Bidding climbed from $500 past $70,000, and when the Clarks' final bid reached $72,000, Rockefeller topped it at $72,500, prompting Maurice Clark to concede, "I'll go no higher, John; the business is yours." Rockefeller called it "the day that determined my career."
- The $72,500 purchase (about $652,000 in 1996 dollars) gave the twenty-five-year-old Rockefeller sole control of a refinery processing 500 barrels of crude daily, twice the capacity of its nearest Cleveland rival; the new firm was renamed Rockefeller and Andrews, and Rockefeller permanently excised the three Clark brothers from his life and largely from his later retellings.
- Interwoven with the business narrative is Rockefeller's courtship of Laura Celestia "Cettie" Spelman, a former Central High School classmate from a prominent, reform-minded Akron and Cleveland family (her parents Harvey and Lucy Spelman were committed abolitionists whose home served as an Underground Railroad station); Cettie graduated valedictorian and attended the pioneering Oread Collegiate Institute for women in Massachusetts before teaching school in Cleveland.
- Rockefeller proposed to Cettie in early 1864 in what a family friend described as "a business-like way," bought a $118 diamond engagement ring (a striking extravagance for him), and married her on September 8, 1864, in a small private ceremony at the Spelman home, with pastors from both her Congregational church and his Baptist mission church officiating; Cettie thereafter became a Baptist.
- By the close of the Civil War, Rockefeller had simultaneously secured personal stability through his marriage and professional supremacy over Cleveland's largest oil refinery, setting up, in Chernow's framing, the single-minded, unbroken focus that would drive the creation of Standard Oil.
