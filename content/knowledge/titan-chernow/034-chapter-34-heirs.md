# Chapter 34: Heirs

> **Key principle:** Rockefeller's grandchildren split sharply by lineage: Edith McCormick's children were battered by her chaos and phobias into troubled marriages and estrangement, while Junior's six children, though scarred by his rigidity, matured into a governor, a Chase Manhattan chairman, art patrons, and other figures who defined the "Rockefeller" name for the twentieth century.

*Synthesized from Chapter 34 of Titan: The Life of John D. Rockefeller, Sr. by Ron Chernow.*

## Key lessons

- Edith's son Fowler McCormick, deeply devoted to Carl Jung, married Anne "Fifi" Stillman in the 1920s, a divorcee eighteen years his senior with four children from a scandalous prior divorce; Rockefeller was heartsick over the match.
- Edith's daughter Muriel rebelled flamboyantly (smoking cigars in public, performing under the stage name "Nawanna Micor"), while daughter Mathilde married her 45-year-old Swiss riding instructor Max Oser in 1923 against Edith's furious objection; Edith warned Mathilde that the McCormick family carried hereditary insanity and refused to see her grandchildren for years, once telling Mathilde "Children really aren't at all important."
- Rockefeller's granddaughter through Bessie, Margaret Strong (raised largely in Europe by her invalid father Charles Strong and his friend George Santayana), married George de Cuevas in 1927; in his 1937 will, after giving away nearly his entire fortune, Rockefeller left his remaining $26.4 million estate (only $9.8 million after taxes) chiefly to Margaret, a final tribute to Bessie.
- Of Junior's six children, daughter Babs (Abby) clashed hardest with her father, sneaking cigarettes despite a $2,500 no-smoking bonus offer, getting two speeding tickets in 1923-24, and calling her 1925 wedding day (to lawyer David Milton) "her first day of freedom"; she later divorced and remarried twice more.
- Eldest son John D. Rockefeller III inherited his father's anxious perfectionism, filling his diary with self-critical entries ("I have no personal attraction," "I have no real friends"), avoided alcohol and Sunday activity even at Princeton during the Jazz Age, married Blanchette Ferry Hooker in 1932, and became the family's most conscientious philanthropist (Rockefeller Foundation, Lincoln Center, Population Council, Asia Society) while suffering agoraphobia like his aunt Edith.
- Nelson Rockefeller was the outgoing, publicity-loving exception, elected officer at Dartmouth, married Mary Todhunter Clark in 1930, wrote a Princeton thesis (coached by Inglis) defending Standard Oil against monopoly charges, later served as assistant secretary of state, HEW undersecretary under Eisenhower, four-term governor of New York starting 1959, and vice president under Gerald Ford; his 1962 divorce and 1963 remarriage to Margaretta "Happy" Murphy damaged his presidential ambitions.
- Laurance Rockefeller, said to resemble Senior most physically, dropped out of Harvard Law School, invested early in aviation (Eastern Airlines with Eddie Rickenbacker, McDonnell Aircraft) and became a major conservationist, founding the Rockresorts luxury-resort company.
- Winthrop Rockefeller struggled most visibly: expelled from Yale after being caught in a shower with a woman, he worked as an oil-field roustabout in Texas for Humble Oil (owned by Jersey Standard), embraced ordinary manual-labor life, married and quickly divorced Barbara "Bobo" Sears in 1948 (a marriage his parents boycotted), and later became the first Republican governor of Arkansas in 94 years, elected in 1966.
- David Rockefeller, the youngest, was Senior's favorite grandchild and the one thought to most resemble him; he earned a economics doctorate at the University of Chicago (thesis critiquing corporate concentration, agreeing with the 1911 Standard Oil breakup), collected a 40,000-specimen beetle collection, and became the only Rockefeller since Senior himself to build a full career in one company, eventually chairman of Chase Manhattan Bank.
- Chernow frames the chapter as showing how the same colossal fortune produced radically different outcomes depending on the parenting: Edith's chaotic self-absorption bred troubled, estranged grandchildren, while Junior's harsh but stable discipline, whatever psychological cost, produced children who each found a serious public vocation.
