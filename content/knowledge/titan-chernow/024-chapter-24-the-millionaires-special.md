# Chapter 24: The Millionaires' Special

> **Key principle:** Junior's 1901 train tour of southern black colleges launched a philanthropic machine, the General Education Board, that transformed southern schooling and public health, even as it repeatedly bowed to segregationist pressure and never escaped the charge that it was buying back a reputation.

*Synthesized from Chapter 24 of Titan: The Life of John D. Rockefeller, Sr. by Ron Chernow.*

## Key lessons

- In April 1901, a chartered train of northern millionaires, dubbed by the press "The Millionaires' Special," toured black colleges across the South, including Tuskegee (where 27-year-old Junior met Booker T. Washington) and the family's own Spelman Seminary in Atlanta; the trip left Junior calling it the most instructive experience of his life and set him on a lifelong philanthropic path.
- The tour led to the General Education Board (GEB), chartered by Congress in January 1903 with Senator Aldrich's help and a one-million-dollar founding gift; Junior wanted to call it the Negro Education Board, but Gates gave it the deliberately neutral name to broaden its mandate to education "without distinction of race, sex or creed."
- Southern segregationist politics shaped the board from the start: at a Southern Education Board meeting, Washington and Lee's president warned northern donors that educating Black southerners without also lifting poor whites would backfire, and GEB executive secretary Wallace Buttrick himself told a Tennessee audience "the Negro is an inferior race," reflecting the era's prevailing paternalism even among reformers who genuinely wanted to help.
- Despite its origins, roughly nine-tenths of GEB money ultimately went to white schools or medical education rather than Black education, drawing later condemnation from W.E.B. Du Bois for backing segregated, vocational-only schooling for Black students; still, the GEB helped create some 800 southern high schools by 1910 and gave $10 million in 1905 and $32 million in 1907 to higher education.
- The GEB pioneered a template of using Rockefeller money to seed government partnerships rather than fund everything outright: it paid traveling professors to organize local high schools, and with Dr. Seaman Knapp at the USDA it funded demonstration farms that helped over 100,000 farms modernize cotton and crop cultivation by 1912, widening the southern tax base that could support public schools.
- The most dramatic public-health success was the campaign against hookworm, launched after Dr. Charles Wardell Stiles's 1902 discovery that hookworm, not laziness, caused chronic listlessness among poor southerners, a claim initially mocked (the New York Sun's "Germ of Laziness Found?" headline) until Gates, hearing Stiles's case in 1909, committed a symbolic $1 million to the Rockefeller Sanitary Commission for the Eradication of Hookworm; within five years nearly half a million people were cured, and the program later expanded worldwide.
- A parallel education-reform breakthrough came from Abraham Flexner's blistering 1910 Flexner Report on American medical schools (funded via the Carnegie Foundation but embraced by Gates), which exposed diploma-mill schools with no labs or standards; it triggered the closure or merger of over a hundred schools, including most remaining homeopathic colleges, and led the GEB to pour more than $78 million into rebuilding medical education on the Johns Hopkins full-time faculty model by 1928.
- Rockefeller's fraught, deeply personal relationship with University of Chicago president William Rainey Harper ran through the chapter: after years of Harper's chronic overspending and a humiliating 1903 board confrontation, Harper died of cancer in January 1906 at 50, and Rockefeller, though he had just been savaged by Tarbell, wrote him a tender farewell letter and mourned him "as though a member of my own family had been taken."
- On Gates's insistence that a true philanthropist must "wean" his own institutions to prove disinterestedness, Rockefeller made a final $10 million gift to the University of Chicago in December 1910 (bringing his total there to $35 million) and then formally withdrew from governing it, a deliberate model of founder-not-owner philanthropy.
- The chapter closes on the 1905 "tainted money" firestorm: a $100,000 Rockefeller gift to the Congregational American Board of Commissioners for Foreign Missions was denounced by the Rev. Washington Gladden as blood money from monopoly, a controversy Mark Twain mocked in a satirical open letter from Satan, while Rockefeller himself, unbowed, kept appearing cheerfully at his Cleveland Baptist church through the uproar.
