# Chapter 26: The World's Richest Fugitive

> **Key principle:** Hunted by subpoena-serving prosecutors and a hostile Roosevelt administration, Rockefeller retreated into an armed, guarded fortress existence at Pocantico even as, almost in parallel, he hired his first publicist and stumbled into the discovery that being friendly to reporters served him better than decades of silence.

*Synthesized from Chapter 26 of Titan: The Life of John D. Rockefeller, Sr. by Ron Chernow.*

## Key lessons

- Standard Oil executives, including a $100,000 contribution from Henry Rogers, funneled money into Theodore Roosevelt's 1904 re-election campaign hoping to blunt his trust-busting, but Roosevelt turned on them anyway; Henry Clay Frick later summarized the miscalculation as "we bought the son of a bitch, but he wouldn't stay bought."
- Roosevelt used the Bureau of Corporations under James Garfield to build a case, then in 1906 signed the Hepburn Act extending Interstate Commerce Commission authority over pipelines and released a damaging 500-page report on Standard Oil's secret railroad rebates, setting up the federal Sherman Antitrust suit that Attorney General William Moody's investigation, led by Frank Kellogg, formally opened in June 1906.
- Missouri attorney general Herbert Hadley led the most aggressive state pursuit, targeting Standard Oil subsidiaries Waters-Pierce and Republic Oil; his process servers famously leapt onto Henry Rogers's moving car to serve a subpoena, and Rockefeller himself became a literal fugitive, retreating first to a guarded Pocantico Hills and then fleeing by boat to a fortified Lakewood, New Jersey estate ringed with floodlights and searched delivery wagons.
- Rockefeller's hideout was comically betrayed by a daily shipment of his favorite cheese via the New York Central Railroad, which a local hack driver recognized and tipped off to reporters, confirming he was holed up at Pocantico; the press mocked him for being unable to visit his newborn grandson John D. Rockefeller III (born March 1906) because of the manhunt.
- Rockefeller repeatedly asked to resign the ceremonial title of Standard Oil of New Jersey president, arguing it made him a legal and PR target despite his near-total withdrawal from operations after 1905 (he stopped even drawing a salary), but Archbold and Henry Rogers refused, telling him bluntly that if any of them went to jail over the pending cases, "he would have to go with us."
- Amid the legal siege, Standard Oil hired its first professional publicist, Joseph I. C. Clarke, a novelty in corporate America at the time, who began arranging friendly golf outings and light interviews between Rockefeller and reporters, producing articles with titles like "The Human Side of John D. Rockefeller."
- A June 1906 transatlantic crossing to visit his ill daughter Bessie Strong in Compiègne, France (undertaken partly to dodge subpoenas and partly for her health) produced an unlikely friendship: reporter William Hoster, sent to expose Rockefeller as a broken invalid, instead found him vigorous, playful, and self-aware about the press's caricatures, and Rockefeller invited him to travel with the family.
- Tragedy struck that November: Bessie Rockefeller Strong, long ill and living abroad with her husband Charles Strong, suffered a fatal paralytic stroke on November 14, 1906, news that arrived just as the federal government moved to prosecute Standard Oil under the Sherman Act; Rockefeller's relationship with Charles cooled afterward over disputes about raising granddaughter Margaret in Europe rather than in the Rockefeller fold.
- Beginning in 1908, Rockefeller published a ghostwritten memoir, "Random Reminiscences of Men and Events" (serialized in The World's Work, with publisher Frank Doubleday and aide Starr Murphy's help), presenting himself as a genial gardener and golfer; on Gates's advice he gave outsized space to the notorious Widow Backus episode precisely because it had done him the most reputational damage.
- The rehabilitation campaign peaked on May 20, 1908, when Mark Twain personally introduced Rockefeller to a room of forty or fifty New York magazine publishers at the Aldine Club; Twain recorded that Rockefeller spoke "sweetly, sanely, simply, humanly" and won the room over completely, even as the antitrust case that would define his final public battle was already moving toward its climax.
