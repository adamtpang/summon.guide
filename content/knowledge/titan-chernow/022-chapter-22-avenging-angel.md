# Chapter 22: Avenging Angel

> **Key principle:** Between 1902 and 1905 Ida Tarbell's McClure's series on Standard Oil, researched with real documentary rigor but shaped by her own Titusville-bred hatred of Rockefeller, permanently fixed his image as a hooded villain; Rockefeller's refusal to answer even her factual errors, combined with a separate press hunt that finally exposed his bigamist father's secret second life, meant the personal cost of the muckraking era fell on the whole family, not just on Standard Oil's business practices.

*Synthesized from Chapter 22 of Titan: The Life of John D. Rockefeller, Sr. by Ron Chernow.*

## Key lessons

- Ohio's new attorney general Frank Monnett revived the antitrust fight in 1897, charging that Standard Oil of Ohio had never really left the trust; when the state supreme court ordered Standard to produce its books in December 1898, sixteen boxes of company records were burned, and Monnett accused Standard of trying to bribe him, though he was soon pushed out of Ohio politics by pro-Standard senator Joseph Foraker (who himself took $44,500 in Archbold lobbying fees in 1900 alone).
- In June 1899 Standard Oil reorganized once more, becoming a New Jersey holding company (Standard Oil of New Jersey) controlling stock in dozens of subsidiary companies; Rockefeller accepted only the nominal, unpaid presidency and never attended a meeting, while Archbold ran daily operations as vice president.
- Even as muckrakers attacked, Standard Oil's actual global monopoly was eroding: Russia briefly overtook the U.S. as the world's top oil producer, Shell and Royal Dutch expanded abroad, Texas's 1901 Spindletop gusher spawned 500 new companies (birthing Gulf Oil and Texaco), and Texas expelled Standard's Waters-Pierce subsidiary for antitrust violations in 1900, permanently barring Standard from the new boom state.
- President Theodore Roosevelt combined provocation with pragmatism: he sued to break up J.P. Morgan's Northern Securities railroad holding company in 1902 and, to force passage of his Bureau of Corporations, leaked telegrams to the press purportedly from "John D. Rockefeller" opposing the bill; the telegrams were actually sent by Junior after Archbold pressured him, a lobbying blunder that mortified Junior and left him convinced he was unsuited to Standard Oil's rough business world.
- Ida Tarbell grew up in the Pennsylvania oil region and watched the 1872 South Improvement Company crisis destroy her father's independent refining business and sour his once-jovial temperament; she carried that formative grievance into her work for Samuel McClure's magazine, where after acclaimed serialized biographies of Napoleon and Lincoln she launched a planned three-part Standard Oil series in November 1902 that ran, by popular demand, to nineteen installments.
- Tarbell was never a neutral reporter: her brother Will was treasurer of the rival Pure Oil Company and fed her anti-Standard material and contacts, and Henry Demarest Lloyd, before his death in 1903, handed her his own research notes and steered independent refiners her way.
- In a strange twist, Standard Oil's own Henry H. Rogers voluntarily met with Tarbell for nearly two years (1902-1904), granting access partly to clear his name in an old Buffalo refinery "explosion" conspiracy charge; Rockefeller later believed Rogers had fed her damaging material out of resentment over having been forced to sell his Standard Oil stock, though the full truth of Rogers's motives remains disputed.
- Tarbell's most famous and least accurate charge was the story of the "Widow Backus," who claimed Rockefeller cheated her out of her late husband's lubricating-oil plant in 1878; in fact Rockefeller's negotiators paid roughly what her own agent had valued the obsolete plant at, voluntarily added $10,000 out of sympathy, and Backus went on to become a wealthy woman (worth about $300,000 at death) by investing the proceeds in Cleveland real estate.
- She also revived, though this time more skeptically, the myth that Standard men blew up a rival Buffalo refinery in 1881; no explosion was ever proven to have occurred, and the underlying case had ended in only $250 fines against the accusers, not Standard's men.
- Tarbell's Cleveland-based researcher John Siddall ran an aggressive shadow campaign: obtaining Rockefeller family photos under false pretenses, planting an impersonator at a church picnic, and orchestrating a covert observation of Rockefeller delivering a Sunday-school talk at Euclid Avenue Baptist Church on June 14, 1903, which fed Tarbell's later, viciously personal two-part character study (July-August 1905) describing Rockefeller as a "living mummy" with a "reptilian" appearance, explicitly weaponizing his alopecia (total hair loss, first severe around 1901) as a symbol of moral corruption.
- Rockefeller maintained an unbending policy of public silence throughout, reasoning "if I step on that worm I will call attention to it. If I ignore it, it will disappear," and largely stuck to this even when Tarbell got provable facts wrong, on the theory that rebutting a few errors would force him to relitigate the whole record; he privately admitted grudging respect for her skill ("How clever she is, compared with poor Lloyd") while insisting he barely read her work.
- Tarbell's most damaging personal source was Rockefeller's own estranged brother Frank, who, still bitter from the 1890s Corrigan stock dispute, gave her a rambling, venomous, disguised-identity interview in his Cleveland office in 1904, accusing John of ruining his life and once musing he might have shot him "if I had met him on the street."
- Siddall's research accidentally uncovered the family's deepest secret: that Rockefeller's father, "Big Bill," presumed dead for decades, was still alive, living a bigamous double life in the Dakotas and Illinois under the alias "Dr. William Levingston." Joseph Pulitzer's World offered an $8,000 reward and sent reporters (notably J.W. Slaght and A.B. Macdonald) on a years-long, near-farcical manhunt; Bill died in Freeport, Illinois in 1906 with his second wife, Margaret, only learning of his bigamy and true identity at his deathbed, and the World finally confirmed and published the whole double life on February 2, 1908, two years after Bill's death, in a story splashed as prominently as a national election.
