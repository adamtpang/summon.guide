# Chapter 15: Widow's Funeral

> **Key principle:** Rockefeller spent decades managing his father's double life as a con man and bigamist from a distance, and when his mother Eliza died in 1889 he used her funeral to publicly enforce the lie that she had died a widow, a final act of control over a relationship he could never resolve directly.

*Synthesized from Chapter 15 of Titan: The Life of John D. Rockefeller, Sr. by Ron Chernow.*

## Key lessons

- In his later years, Big Bill Rockefeller continued touring under the alias "Dr. William Levingston," selling fake cures across Illinois, Minnesota, Iowa, and the Dakotas; he hired a young man named Charles H. Johnston, dressed him in feathers and war paint, and passed him off as an "Indian prince" assistant to draw in customers, even charging Johnston $1,000 to "apprentice" in the con.
- Bill secretly bragged to Johnston for years about a rich son in Cleveland, claiming "I started John D. Rockefeller in the oil business. I loaned him the first money," and boasting his Standard Oil stake was worth $375,000, before an 1880s injury scare forced him to admit outright that he was John D. Rockefeller's father.
- To manage his father without publicly acknowledging the bigamy (Bill's second, illegal wife was Margaret Allen), Rockefeller bought him a 160-acre ranch in Park River, North Dakota in 1881 on the condition Margaret never live there, routing some of the paperwork through his brother-in-law Pierson Briggs and later forcing Bill to sign deeds under his real name, "William Avery Rockefeller," to strip Margaret of any legal claim.
- Eliza Rockefeller died on March 28, 1889, at age seventy-six, after a stroke suffered at son William's Fifth Avenue mansion; John and William kept a bedside vigil, and she was buried in the family plot at Lake View Cemetery in Cleveland, having never learned of her husband's second, bigamous marriage.
- Rockefeller made sure Eliza's funeral, and her death certificate, formally described her as a widow, and he personally briefed the officiating minister, Reverend George T. Dowling, to emphasize her "years of widowhood" and fidelity; Bill was pointedly absent from the service, having cited asthma as his excuse.
- Furious at his father's absence, Rockefeller retaliated that October by forcing Bill to sell the Park River ranch, again making him sign as "William Avery Rockefeller, widower of Cleveland" so Margaret Allen could not inherit any of the proceeds.
- The estrangement never fully resolved: Bill visited New York in 1890 with his brother Egbert and later attended a 1902 reunion at Forest Hill at age ninety-two, where he publicly needled and embarrassed John ("Here comes Johnnie... look out how you trade with him") in front of guests, while remaining warm and close with his more indulgent son Frank.
- A parallel family thread traces Rockefeller's troubled relationship with his volatile younger brother Frank, a Union Civil War veteran who ran up huge losses (Colonel Oliver Payne reported roughly $100,000 lost in Chicago speculation in 1882) and repeatedly needed bailing out; Rockefeller and William financed an 8,000-acre Kansas cattle ranch for him even as Frank testified against Standard Oil's railroad rebates in 1884 congressional hearings.
- In 1886 Rockefeller created a Standard Oil of Ohio vice presidency for Frank, but Frank clashed with office chief Colonel W. P. Thompson and was gradually sidelined as real authority shifted from Cleveland to New York in February 1887; an official company history later described him as regarded by many as "a millstone around the neck of a more talented man," kept on mainly because his name was Rockefeller.
