# Chapter 3: Bound to Be Rich

> **Key principle:** Landing his first job as a bookkeeper at Hewitt and Tuttle on September 26, 1855 ("Job Day"), the sixteen-year-old Rockefeller built the twin foundations of his life, a fanatical devotion to precise record-keeping (embodied in his cherished Ledger A) and total commitment to the Erie Street Baptist Mission Church, both of which let him finally escape his father's chaos.

*Synthesized from Chapter 3 of Titan: The Life of John D. Rockefeller, Sr. by Ron Chernow.*

## Key lessons

- In early 1853 Bill moved the family to Strongsville, Ohio, near Cleveland, boarding them with relatives, the Humistons, while he drifted further from the household; townspeople soon discovered his double life as "Dr. William A. Rockefeller, the Celebrated Cancer Specialist," a traveling quack charging $25 for bogus cancer cures, earning him the lasting nickname "Doc Rockefeller."
- Bill secretly married a second, unsuspecting wife, seventeen-year-old Margaret Allen of Norwich, Ontario, on June 12, 1855, under the alias "Dr. William Levingston," beginning a decades-long bigamy that neither wife discovered for years; Chernow argues this second marriage, not financial hardship, is why Bill pulled John out of high school just weeks before graduation in 1855 to cut expenses and push him into work.
- After a grueling six-week, six-day-a-week job search walking Cleveland's commercial district (a period he later celebrated annually as "Job Day"), sixteen-year-old Rockefeller was hired as an assistant bookkeeper at Hewitt and Tuttle, commission merchants and produce shippers, on September 26, 1855, initially without pay; he received his first wages, $50, three months later.
- Rockefeller treated bookkeeping as almost sacred, pouncing on billing errors of a few cents and investigating a ship captain's inflated damage claims; he later called the firm's exposure to rail, canal, and lake transportation formative, saying "my eyes were opened to the business of transportation."
- He began keeping a personal account book he called Ledger A starting in September 1855, meticulously recording every cent earned and spent; he kept it in a safety-deposit vault for the rest of his life and, decades later, would display it to Bible classes, once declaring "You couldn't get it from me for all the modern ledgers in New York."
- Ledger A also documents that Rockefeller was philanthropic from the start: he gave roughly 6 percent of his wages to charity in his first year, rising past 10 percent by age twenty, donating to causes including the Five Points Mission in Manhattan, a black church, a Catholic orphanage, and money to help a black man in Cincinnati buy his wife out of slavery in 1859.
- Rockefeller joined the Erie Street Baptist Mission Church as a teenager (baptized in 1854 by Deacon Alexander Sked) and threw himself into it completely: teaching Sunday school, serving as trustee and unpaid clerk, sweeping the floors, lighting lamps, and once single-handedly raising $2,000 in pledges after services to save the church from foreclosure on its mortgage.
- In 1857, Bill had John personally oversee construction of a substantial brick family house on Cheshire Street in Cleveland, handing over the money and design instructions and telling him "I shall be away and must rely on your judgment"; the eighteen-year-old solicited bids from eight contractors and delivered the house under budget, then was made to pay his own father rent to live in it.
- After building the house, Bill effectively abandoned the family for good, resettling with Margaret Allen, eventually in Philadelphia, while John became the family's de facto head; Rockefeller's grandfather John Davison died June 1, 1858, leaving Eliza an annuity, and William (John's brother) also worked under him as a bookkeeper.
- Feeling underpaid as chief bookkeeper (Hewitt offered $700 against his ask of $800) and sensing the firm's postwar-panic weakness, Rockefeller left Hewitt and Tuttle to co-found a produce commission partnership, Clark and Rockefeller, with Englishman Maurice B. Clark on April 1, 1858; Bill lent him the $1,000 needed to buy in, charging his own son 10 percent interest.
