# Chapter 7: Millionaires' Row

> **Key principle:** As Rockefeller's oil fortune grew, he moved to Cleveland's grandest street but built the plainest house on it, and ran his household on the same anxious thrift, religious discipline, and controlled routine that defined his business.

*Synthesized from Chapter 7 of Titan: The Life of John D. Rockefeller, Sr. by Ron Chernow.*

## Key lessons

- In August 1868, after securing favorable rebates from the Lake Shore Railroad, Rockefeller and his wife Cettie moved from Cheshire Street to a $40,000 brick house at 424 Euclid Avenue in Cleveland, known as "Millionaires' Row" for its mansions belonging to men like Amasa Stone and Henry B. Payne. His house was deliberately modest compared to his wealth and his neighbors' showier homes, reflecting both Puritan discomfort with display and a wish not to draw attention.
- Rockefeller devoted more energy to landscaping than architecture; when a neighboring house blocked his garden view, he had it jacked up and rolled on greased logs to a girls' school a block away rather than simply demolish it, a spectacle covered by local papers.
- He was an avid horseman and trotting-horse breeder (paying $10,000-$12,500 for individual trotters in the 1870s), using racing and driving as stress relief for job-induced neck pain, and prized a calm, patient style with animals that mirrored his unflappable business demeanor.
- Domestically he was the opposite of his philandering father: a devoted, temperate, church-centered husband who avoided clubs, theater, opera, and alcohol entirely, and structured each day (work, napping, gardening, family time) with near-mechanical regularity, even wiring a private telegraph line between home and office so he could work from home several afternoons a week.
- He and Cettie had five children at the Euclid Avenue house: Elizabeth ("Bessie," b. 1866), Alice (b. 1869, died in infancy), Alta (b. 1871), Edith (b. 1872), and John Jr. (b. 1874), all delivered by Cleveland's first female physician, Dr. Myra Herrick.
- Rockefeller was an affectionate, playful father (balancing plates on his nose, leading bicycle "ghost" rides at night at his Forest Hill estate) but kept the children almost entirely isolated from outside friendships and from any knowledge of the family's true wealth, hiring governesses and barring them from his offices and refineries until adulthood.
- He ran the household as a miniature market economy: children earned pennies for chores like killing flies, pulling weeds, and sharpening pencils, and were rewarded for thrift (a dime bonus for each day they abstained from candy); the four children famously shared a single bicycle at Cettie's insistence, "so they will learn to give up to one another."
- Sundays were tightly regimented around prayer, scripture, and hymns, with no novels or secular reading permitted; punctuality was enforced with a penny fine for lateness to morning family prayers, and John Jr. reportedly timed the walk to school down to the second.
- Rockefeller's estranged father, "Big Bill," lived a double life in Illinois under the alias "Dr. William Levingston," a traveling con man selling fake cancer and kidney cures, while his abandoned first wife Eliza and grandchildren were kept only vaguely aware of his whereabouts; John secretly sent money but kept his father psychologically and geographically banished from the family's respectable life.
- Cettie Rockefeller was portrayed as even more austere than her husband, wearing patched clothes, insisting two dresses sufficed for a woman's wardrobe, and running a strict "home talk" ritual each Sunday where each child confessed a "besetting sin"; her sister Lucy ("Aunt Lute") lived with the family and provided a rare window onto secular culture by reading contemporary literature aloud.
