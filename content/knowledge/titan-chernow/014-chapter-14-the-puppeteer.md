# Chapter 14: The Puppeteer

> **Key principle:** Rockefeller directed Standard Oil's most aggressive and often unscrupulous practices, from railroad rebates to spying on competitors to predatory pricing, from several removes, communicating in deliberately opaque letters so that when scandal broke he could credibly claim ignorance while his lieutenants took the blame.

*Synthesized from Chapter 14 of Titan: The Life of John D. Rockefeller, Sr. by Ron Chernow.*

## Key lessons

- Standard Oil's dominance faced real foreign competition for the first time in the 1880s: the Nobel brothers built a Russian oil empire around the Baku fields on the Caspian Sea (their tanker Zoroaster was the world's first oil tanker), and the Paris Rothschilds financed Russian exports through their Bnito company, together undercutting Standard's kerosene across Europe by the mid-1880s.
- In the Far East, London merchant Marcus Samuel used the Suez Canal and his tanker Murex to ship cheap Russian kerosene to Asia faster than Standard could, defeating Standard's marketing efforts; Samuel's firm became Shell Transport and Trading Company in 1897, and it later merged with the Dutch driller Royal Dutch, permanently ending Standard's global monopoly.
- Chernow frames Rockefeller as a "puppeteer" who by the 1880s held no direct operational role at Standard Oil, instead setting policy and sales targets (Archbold's 1891 letter shows station managers were expected to capture at least 85 percent of their district's trade) while writing terse, name-free letters that left him legal and moral distance from the tactics his subordinates used to hit those numbers.
- Railroad collusion continued well past 1880 despite Rockefeller's later claims otherwise: Standard used its Galena-Signal lubricant monopoly and its leased tank-car fleet (rented to 196 railroads by the late 1880s, collecting mileage tribute on both loaded and empty runs) to extract rebates, documented in detail through Colonel W. P. Thompson's letters, including a March 1886 Pennsylvania Railroad discount of 52 cents versus a posted 78-cent rate.
- Standard's marketing subsidiaries, largely run by absorbed local operators, became notorious for ruthless tactics: F. D. Carley of Chess, Carley and Company (Kentucky) ordered a railroad to "turn the screw" on rival shipper George Rice, and Henry Clay Pierce of Waters-Pierce Company (based in St. Louis, covering Arkansas to Texas) evaded Mexican import duties and later received a $200,000 personal loan from Rockefeller in 1892 despite years of documented "duplicity."
- Standard maintained an extensive intelligence network of paid informants (including bribed bookkeepers of rival refiners) to track every barrel independents sold, and it practiced geographic price discrimination, cutting prices to cost in contested towns like Cincinnati while raising them where competitors had been driven out, such as Chicago.
- Standard entered the natural gas business in the mid-1880s through Daniel O'Day, and its franchise fights turned openly corrupt: in Detroit an agent was given $15,000 cash and $10,000 in stock to sway officials, and in Toledo, former Ohio governor Charles Foster secretly fronted a Standard-controlled gas company while collecting political payoffs from Rockefeller.
- Persistent critics coalesced into the trust's earliest public opposition: Bradford pipeline owner Lewis Emery Jr. and Marietta refiner George Rice both fed information to journalist Henry Demarest Lloyd, whose March 1881 Atlantic Monthly exposé "Story of a Great Monopoly" (going through six printings) introduced Rockefeller to a national audience, popularized the "octopus" nickname, and helped push antitrust reform onto the political agenda.
- Rockefeller's public strategy was near-total silence, summed up in his own phrase that "the Standard Oil Company's business was that of saying nothing and sawing wood"; he refused interviews, banned photographers, and used intermediaries like the Jennings Publishing Company to plant favorable coverage, even as some insiders (manager William Jay Cooke, and senior partner William G. Warden in a remarkable 1887 letter urging Rockefeller to repair the company's reputation for his wife Cettie's sake) voiced private moral discomfort that Rockefeller answered only with a bland, noncommittal reply.
