# Chapter 31: Confessional

> **Key principle:** Freed from Cettie's long illness, Rockefeller became a warmer public figure at his Florida retreat, and in a secret 1917-1920 oral-history project with journalist William O. Inglis, he finally spoke his uncensored mind about Standard Oil's critics, revealing the resentment his placid public persona had always concealed.

*Synthesized from Chapter 31 of Titan: The Life of John D. Rockefeller, Sr. by Ron Chernow.*

## Key lessons

- After Cettie's death, Rockefeller built a substitute household: his cousin Fanny Evans became his housekeeper-companion (they addressed each other formally as "Mr. Rockefeller" and "Mrs. Evans"), and Swiss valet John Yordi managed his diet and entertained him on the organ.
- He bought a modest gray-shingled house called The Casements at Ormond Beach, Florida in September 1918, moving in for winters starting 1919; locals affectionately dubbed him "Neighbor John" after he told a boy who'd called out "Hello John D." that "Hello Neighbor John" would have been nicer.
- At Ormond Beach he formed real friendships, notably with Civil War general Adelbert Ames, and hosted casual visits from Henry Ford ("As soon as I saw his face I knew what had made the Standard Oil Company") and humorist Will Rogers, who joked about Rockefeller's golf skill and gasoline prices.
- In 1916-17 John Singer Sargent painted two portraits of Rockefeller at Junior and Abby's urging, over Rockefeller's initial objection to Sargent's high price ("What about Kohlbach?"); sculptor Paul Manship also made two busts around the same time, capturing what Chernow calls Rockefeller's split nature: saintly upward gaze in one, hard stern lips in the other.
- The famous dime-and-nickel giveaway (adults got dimes, children got nickels, meant to teach thrift) was Rockefeller's own invention, not publicist Ivy Lee's; Lee's contribution was turning the private habit into a public trademark. Rockefeller is estimated to have given away 20,000-30,000 coins.
- Starting in 1915, Ivy Lee cultivated sportswriter William O. Inglis as a friendly biographer; Rockefeller golfed with him for six weeks in 1917 as a trial before agreeing to a formal interview.
- Between November 1, 1917 and December 13, 1920 (with a gap from mid-1919 to late 1920), Inglis interviewed Rockefeller almost daily, reading aloud passages from Henry Demarest Lloyd's and Ida Tarbell's exposes and recording his responses, producing a 480,000-word transcript.
- The transcript revealed a far angrier, more defensive Rockefeller than the public ever saw: he called Tarbell's critique "dangerous" for pretending fairness while smuggling in "evil and prejudicial stuff," fantasized that she was secretly guilt-ridden, and lashed out at "socialists and anarchists" as "a stench... a poison." He never once mentioned the 1911 Supreme Court dissolution of Standard Oil in the entire interview, and declined to have the decision read aloud to him ("I shirked it; left it to the lawyers").
- Rockefeller gave roughly $70 million to World War I relief, including $22 million through the Rockefeller Foundation for Belgian famine relief, and took the Allied victory (fueled substantially by American oil) as vindication of his life's work, telling Inglis that "there must have been a Providence" behind his fortune's role in the war.
- Inglis finished a fawning, uncritical 1924 manuscript that reviewers including editor William Allen White and Rockefeller Foundation president George Vincent judged too sycophantic to publish; Junior, on Ivy Lee's suggestion, showed the draft to Ida Tarbell herself, who by then liked Junior personally but agreed the biography was one-sided, and it was shelved permanently in the Rockefeller archives.
