# Chapter 1: The Flimflam Man

> **Key principle:** Rockefeller's father was a bigamist, snake-oil salesman, and full-time confidence man whose charm masked chronic deceit; the son's discipline, secrecy, and distrust of drink and bluster trace directly back to growing up in his father's shadow.

*Synthesized from Chapter 1 of Titan: The Life of John D. Rockefeller, Sr. by Ron Chernow.*

## Key lessons

- The Rockefeller line traced to Johann Peter Rockefeller, a German miller who emigrated to New Jersey around 1723; John D.'s grandfather, Godfrey Rockefeller, was a genial but feckless, drink-prone farmer married to the far stronger Lucy Avery, whose relatives had settled Puritan New England since around 1630. John D. later said plainly, "My grandmother was a brave woman. Her husband was not so brave as she."
- Godfrey and Lucy migrated west to Richford, New York around 1832-1834, settling a stony sixty-acre hillside that Godfrey, disappointed it wasn't Michigan, nicknamed "Michigan Hill." Their third child, William Avery Rockefeller (born 1810), grew up to become John D.'s father.
- William Avery Rockefeller, later nicknamed "Big Bill" or "Devil Bill," built a career as an itinerant confidence man: he posed as a deaf-mute peddler carrying a slate reading "I am deaf and dumb," and later reinvented himself as a self-styled "botanic physician," selling fake medicines including harmless purplish berries falsely claimed to induce abortion.
- Bill met Eliza Davison, daughter of the comparatively well-off farmer John Davison, and married her on February 18, 1837, despite her father's strong objections. Richford gossip held that Bill pursued Eliza chiefly for her father's money, having earlier jilted his live-in girlfriend Nancy Brown with a $400 settlement.
- In fact, Bill did not fully break with Nancy Brown: he brought her into the household as a "housekeeper" and fathered children by both women simultaneously. Eliza gave birth to daughter Lucy in 1838; Nancy's daughter Clorinda followed soon after; John Davison Rockefeller was born July 8, 1839, in an 8-by-10-foot bedroom in Richford; Nancy then had a second daughter, Cornelia. John D. thus grew up sandwiched between two illegitimate half-sisters he apparently never fully knew about.
- Eliza's brothers eventually forced Bill to send Nancy and her daughters away to live with her parents in nearby Harford Mills. Cornelia later became a schoolteacher who resembled Bill closely but never publicized the kinship, likely to avoid exposing her own illegitimacy.
- Bill was frequently absent for weeks or months at a time on mysterious trips, leaving Eliza to run the farm and manage credit at the general store (telling the shopkeeper "Give my family anything they want while I'm away... and when I come back I'll settle"). He would return suddenly with fine horses, cash, and gifts, settle the debts, and charm his way back into the household, prompting locals to nickname him Devil Bill amid rumors he was a horse thief or gambler.
- Neighbors described the Rockefeller children in Richford as poorly clothed and neglected; Eliza at times relied on the household of Bill's brother Jacob Rockefeller for support. Chernow notes Rockefeller's later reminiscences of an idyllic childhood conveniently omitted Richford and his father almost entirely, an early sign of his lifelong habit of suppressing unpleasant memories while preserving those of his mother and grandmother.
- Around 1843, when John D. was about three or four, the family left Richford for Moravia, New York, closer to Eliza's father. Chernow attributes the move less to the "stingy soil" explanation Rockefeller himself gave in later life than to Eliza's desire to escape the town's low moral tone and her husband's dissolute Rockefeller relatives.
- Rockefeller later reflected on Richford's cautionary lesson bluntly: "I shudder to think of what I should have been if I had remained in Richford all my life... men who hunt a little, fish a little, and drink whiskey a little, and only attain a little success in life, and all for the lack of a little religion."
