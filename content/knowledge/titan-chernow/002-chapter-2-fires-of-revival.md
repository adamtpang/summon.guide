# Chapter 2: Fires of Revival

> **Key principle:** Growing up in the Second Great Awakening's "Burned-Over District" of upstate New York, young John D. absorbed his mother's evangelical Baptist creed of working hard, saving relentlessly, and giving generously, while his often-absent, scandal-shadowed father taught him showmanship, hard bargaining, and an abiding secrecy about family matters.

*Synthesized from Chapter 2 of Titan: The Life of John D. Rockefeller, Sr. by Ron Chernow.*

## Key lessons

- Around 1843 the family moved from Richford to a ninety-two-acre farm above Owasco Lake near Moravia, New York, which Rockefeller remembered as his idyllic childhood home; there Eliza gave birth to Mary Ann and twins Frank and Frances, the latter dying just before her second birthday, an early brush with death Rockefeller carried for life.
- In Moravia, Bill briefly played respectable citizen, running a legitimate logging business, helping site the town school, and heading the local temperance committee, even as he continued disappearing for long stretches and, per Rockefeller's own later account, cultivated a deliberate policy of "firing and hiring over" his workers to keep them from growing complacent.
- Young John showed an early entrepreneurial streak: he bought candy in bulk to resell to siblings at a profit, raised turkeys from a wild hen's nest at age seven to sell, and dropped every coin he earned into a blue china bowl on the mantel at his mother's urging.
- Rockefeller's Baptist faith, rooted in the Second Great Awakening revivalism sweeping upstate New York (the so-called "Burned-Over District"), taught him that earning and giving money were both religious duties; he later said, "I have always regarded it as a religious duty to get all I could honorably and to give all I could."
- Eliza Davison Rockefeller ran the household essentially as a single parent, enforcing discipline with a birch switch ("laying on Macduff," in her words) even after John, at around age ten, helped save a drowning boy while ice-skating against her orders; she punished him anyway, saying the tanning would "do for the next time."
- The chapter details serious allegations against Bill during the Moravia years: persistent local rumors that he led a horse-theft ring feeding the Loomis Brothers gang, and a formal 1849 grand jury indictment for the rape of a household helper, Anne Vanderbeak, in May 1848 (Bill never went to trial and fled the county; the case's collapse remains unresolved).
- The rape indictment ruptured Bill's fragile truce with his father-in-law John Davison, who refused to post bail, sued Bill for the roughly $1,200 he had lent him, and had the family's movable property seized by the sheriff; Bill abandoned the family for months before resettling them in Owego, New York, in spring 1850.
- Bill's contradictory business ethic left a deep mark on John: scrupulous about honoring written contracts and paying debts on time, yet a shameless huckster who once won a $1,000 discount on a farm by shooting-contest wager and sold worthless "physic bush" berries as medicine; Rockefeller later called his father a model of practical, resourceful business virtue, glossing over the darker record.
- In Owego (1850-1853), Eliza leaned further on John as her practical deputy, and he entered the well-regarded Owego Academy in 1852 alongside brother William, excelling at nothing in particular but showing fascination with new inventions like the telegraph; contemporaries recalled him as an unremarkable, quiet, hardworking boy, not a standout.
- A recurring theme Chernow draws out is Rockefeller's lifelong, deliberate suppression of unpleasant memories about his father, epitomized by his flat denial in old age that Bill ever fled "under compulsion," and by the family taboo, observed by friends for decades, against ever discussing Big Bill; Chernow quotes Bertrand Russell's line that Rockefeller's words, thoughts, and feelings came from his mother, but his actions came from his father, "with the addition of a great caution generated by early unpleasantness."
