# Chapter 16: A Matter of Trust

> **Key principle:** Rockefeller staked millions of his own money on a chemist's unproven promise to make worthless Ohio crude marketable, a bet that secured Standard Oil's crude supply for a generation even as the trust's exposed political muscle helped push Congress to pass the Sherman Antitrust Act.

*Synthesized from Chapter 16 of Titan: The Life of John D. Rockefeller, Sr. by Ron Chernow.*

## Key lessons

- Through the mid-1880s Standard Oil worried constantly that Pennsylvania's oil fields, the industry's only major source, would run dry; in 1885 John D. Archbold was so certain no oil existed elsewhere that he scoffed, "I'll drink every gallon of oil produced west of the Mississippi," and briefly sold down his own Standard Oil shares.
- In May 1885, prospectors searching for natural gas near Lima, Ohio struck oil instead, proving crude existed outside Pennsylvania, but the find was nearly worthless: high sulfur content gave "Lima crude" a foul, skunk-like smell that ruined it for kerosene, and it sold for as little as fifteen cents a barrel.
- Rockefeller's board, led by the cautious Charles Pratt, resisted buying up the Lima leases for years; to break the deadlock Rockefeller personally pledged to underwrite the investment out of his own pocket, offering roughly $3 million (about $47 million in 1996 dollars) and telling the board, "If it is a failure, I will take the loss," which finally won Pratt's assent.
- Rockefeller hired German-born chemist Herman Frasch in 1886 to find a way to desulfurize Lima crude; Frasch achieved a breakthrough on October 13, 1888, using copper oxides to strip the sulfur, an invention that made the Ohio-Indiana field the country's top crude producer in the 1890s (Standard's share of American crude output peaked at 33 percent in 1898).
- Vindicated, Standard Oil moved into oil production for the first time on a large scale: a production committee under Archbold spent $22 million in its first two years (starting 1889), and the trust bought Union Oil and other firms, acquiring some three hundred thousand acres in Pennsylvania and West Virginia by 1890. It also built the giant Whiting, Indiana refinery (processing 36,000 barrels a day), where chemist William M. Burton later invented petroleum "cracking."
- Standard Oil's political entanglements drew fire in this period: Ohio's 1884 "Coal-oil Legislature" elected Henry B. Payne, father of Standard treasurer Oliver Payne, to the U.S. Senate amid rumors (never proven) that Oliver had distributed roughly $100,000 in bribes; West Virginia senator Johnson N. Camden openly solicited Standard Oil money and favors for years while in office (1881-1887).
- After the Interstate Commerce Act of 1887 banned railroad rebates and pools, Standard Oil publicly claimed compliance while quietly continuing preferential treatment through accounting subterfuge, as documented in Colonel W. P. Thompson's letters describing a disguised rebate arrangement with the Lake Shore Railroad.
- At a February 1888 New York state senate hearing, Rockefeller gave now-legendary evasive testimony, coached by lawyer Joseph Choate, that impressed even his interrogator, Roger Pryor; under oath he disclosed for the first time the 1882 Standard Oil Trust agreement, naming its eight trustees, roughly seven hundred shareholders, and forty-one member companies, while denying membership in the (misnamed) "Southern Improvement Company."
- Mounting public anger at trusts culminated in the Sherman Antitrust Act, signed by President Benjamin Harrison on July 2, 1890, banning combinations in restraint of trade; Standard Oil, singled out by name in the legislative debate as the law's chief target, was barely affected in practice for years, since the vague statute was widely mocked as the "Swiss Cheese Act."
