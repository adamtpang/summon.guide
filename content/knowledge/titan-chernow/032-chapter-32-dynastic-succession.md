# Chapter 32: Dynastic Succession

> **Key principle:** Convinced by Junior's conduct during Ludlow that his son could be trusted, Rockefeller handed over the family fortune in a rapid five-year burst starting in 1917, elevating Junior above his sisters and turning him into the sole steward of Rockefeller wealth, while Junior in turn raised his own children under a strict, allowance-and-account-book regime modeled on his father's frugality.

*Synthesized from Chapter 32 of Titan: The Life of John D. Rockefeller, Sr. by Ron Chernow.*

## Key lessons

- Father and son remained emotionally reserved with each other even in old age; Junior admitted "Neither Father nor I had the temperament which gives itself freely," and Rockefeller once told interviewer Inglis, "I have never had time to become really acquainted with my son."
- A rare clash occurred over art: in January 1915 Junior asked to borrow over $1 million to buy J.P. Morgan's Chinese porcelain collection (sold through dealer Joseph Duveen after Morgan's 1913 death); Rockefeller, viewing art collecting as wasteful, initially refused, then relented and gave the money outright after Junior's impassioned defense that it was his "only hobby." This launched Junior's serious art collecting, later expressed in The Cloisters and Colonial Williamsburg.
- Starting March 13, 1917 (20,000 shares of Standard Oil of Indiana) and accelerating through 1918-1920, Rockefeller transferred huge blocks of stock (Standard Oil of California, Atlantic Refining, Vacuum Oil, Standard Oil of New Jersey) and bonds to Junior, including a February 17, 1920 gift of $65 million in Liberty Loan bonds, in what Chernow calls the largest intrafamily money transfer in history at the time.
- Between 1917 and 1922 Rockefeller gave away roughly $200 million more to charity and $475 million to his children, almost all to Junior; Junior's net worth rose from about $20 million in 1917 to roughly $500 million within five years, more than all of Rockefeller's combined philanthropic institutions.
- By contrast, daughters Alta and Edith each received a $12 million trust set up in 1917 (about $140 million each in today's dollars) that terminated their allowances, a fraction of what Junior received; Junior later rationalized this as reflecting that only he would carry on his father's philanthropic "sense of duty and stewardship."
- Junior and Abby Aldrich Rockefeller had six children (Abby "Babs," John III, Nelson, Laurance, Winthrop, and David, the last born 1915) and in 1911 built a nine-story mansion at 10 West 54th Street; from 1908 they summered on Mount Desert Island, Maine, buying the Eyrie in Seal Harbor in 1910 and expanding it to 107 rooms.
- Junior became a major conservationist on Mount Desert Island, personally designing 57 miles of auto-free carriage roads and donating land that became Lafayette National Park in 1919 (renamed Acadia National Park in 1929), the first national park in the eastern United States.
- Abby was politically more liberal than her husband, supporting Planned Parenthood, the League of Nations, and labor causes (funding roughly a third of the National Women's Trade Union League's budget after Ludlow); she also ran a Red Cross auxiliary of 500 workers during World War I and wrote passionately against lynching and antisemitism in a 1923 letter to her sons.
- Rockefeller doted on his grandchildren as a playful, joke-telling grandfather, dispensing nickels with lectures about thrift, while Junior was a stricter father: children received only a 30-cent allowance, had to account for every penny in ledgers audited every Saturday, split money into thirds (spend, save, donate), and earned pocket change from chores like killing flies (10 cents per hundred) or trapping mice (5 cents each).
- The chapter closes noting that despite Junior's rigid, sermon-heavy household routine (daily Bible readings, Sunday nature-walk lectures), the six children never became as devoutly religious as their father or grandfather, foreshadowing the family's gradual secularization in later generations.
