# Chapter 4: Baptism in Business

> **Key principle:** As a young partner in the Cleveland produce commission house Clark and Rockefeller (founded April 1, 1858), Rockefeller combined obsessive bookkeeping precision, aggressive but polite salesmanship, and skillful bank borrowing to grow the firm rapidly, while his refusal to enlist in the Civil War and his cold handling of family and partners revealed the harder edges of his character.

*Synthesized from Chapter 4 of Titan: The Life of John D. Rockefeller, Sr. by Ron Chernow.*

## Key lessons

- Clark and Rockefeller opened at 32 River Street in Cleveland trading grain, fish, salt, and other produce; an early setback came when a frost-damaged bean shipment forced the partners to hand-sort spoiled beans themselves, and the firm ended its first year with a solid $4,400 profit, roughly triple Rockefeller's prior salary at Hewitt and Tuttle.
- Rockefeller's father Bill continued lending him money at steep rates, and by his own later admission Bill would sometimes call in loans "just at the moment when I required the money most" as a kind of test, leaving Rockefeller privately furious even as he publicly framed these dealings as valuable business lessons from his father.
- On April 1, 1859, George W. Gardner, a well-connected Cleveland scion (later the city's mayor), joined as a third partner, and the firm was renamed Clark, Gardner and Company, dropping Rockefeller's name from the sign; Rockefeller called this demotion "a great injustice" in private but outwardly accepted it without protest, a pattern of suppressed resentment Chernow treats as characteristic.
- Rockefeller and the more easygoing Gardner clashed repeatedly: Rockefeller angrily refused an invitation to join Gardner's yacht outing, calling him "the most extravagant young man I ever knew," and on another occasion, after the firm skipped insurance on a major uninsured grain shipment to save a $150 premium, Rockefeller paced in anguish overnight until word came the ship had survived a storm on Lake Erie.
- Gardner also provided the earliest documented evidence that Rockefeller knew about his father's secret second life: when Gardner asked for Bill's Philadelphia address to make a business contact, Rockefeller stalled and claimed he'd forgotten to get it, convincing Gardner that his partner was hiding something about his father he refused to discuss.
- Rockefeller proved an effective if pushy salesman, touring Ohio and Indiana to win produce consignments through a polite, low-pressure pitch, and secured his first outside loan of $2,000 from banker Truman P. Handy on the strength of his sterling reputation at the Erie Street Baptist Mission Church; he later said the moment made him feel "a man of importance in the community."
- Despite his fervent, long-held abolitionist sympathies (dating back to a school essay condemning slavery), Rockefeller did not enlist when the Civil War broke out in 1861, citing his responsibility to keep the fledgling firm running and support his family; like J. P. Morgan and other well-off young men, he paid roughly $300 for a substitute and later helped finance the enlistment costs of dozens of soldiers.
- Rockefeller's youngest brother Frank, eager for battlefield glory, was initially refused financial help to enlist (in one telling, by John himself), lied about his age to join the Seventh Ohio Volunteer Infantry, and was wounded twice, at Chancellorsville and Cedar Mountain; this episode fed a lifelong resentment between Frank and John, with Frank feeling he paid in blood for a war John profited from at home.
- The Civil War proved a major boon for the firm: wartime demand, inflated commodity prices, and Cleveland's rising importance as an east-west shipping hub after Mississippi River routes were cut off drove annual profits to $17,000 by 1862, nearly four times prewar levels.
- At the end of 1862 Rockefeller pushed George Gardner out of the firm, which was renamed simply Clark and Rockefeller and expanded to occupy four warehouse addresses on River Street; Chernow notes Rockefeller subsequently erased Gardner almost entirely from his own retellings of this period, foreshadowing the wealth about to arrive from the new oil fields opening around Titusville, Pennsylvania.
