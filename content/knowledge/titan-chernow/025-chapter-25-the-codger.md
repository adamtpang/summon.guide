# Chapter 25: The Codger

> **Key principle:** Freed from daily business, Rockefeller became a genial, oddly dressed, fanatically punctual old man obsessed with living to one hundred and with squeezing every dollar even as he gave away millions, while his anxious son Junior absorbed the public's mockery in his place.

*Synthesized from Chapter 25 of Titan: The Life of John D. Rockefeller, Sr. by Ron Chernow.*

## Key lessons

- Fearing assassination amid death threats after the Tarbell exposés, Rockefeller ringed Forest Hill with an eight-foot iron fence in 1906, hired Pinkerton guards, and kept a revolver by his bed, yet Chernow says he grew visibly more cheerful and relaxed as retirement set in around 1906.
- His look turned eccentric: alopecia left him bald, so he experimented with wigs and skullcaps before settling into loud golf caps, goggles, and once a yellow silk coat over a Japanese paper vest, an outfit his gardener Tom Pyle described as giving him an "eerily cadaverous appearance."
- Rockefeller followed an unvarying, minute-by-minute daily regimen aimed at reaching age 100: up at 6 a.m., an hour of newspapers, correspondence (up to 2,000 letters a day arrived at Pocantico, mostly money requests), golf from 10:15 to noon, naps, motoring, formal dinner, and rounds of a card game called numerica, a rhythm observed firsthand by aide William O. Inglis as "bordering on the superhuman."
- Despite an untaxed annual income Chernow estimates near $58 million in 1902 (roughly a billion dollars a year in today's terms), Rockefeller spent only about $439,000 on household expenses and remained a fanatical skinflint, auditing every estate's books, charging Pocantico for trees "sold" to his New Jersey property, and once telling a doctor billing him $3,000 that he would "stand a trial" rather than pay what he called extortion.
- He and his wife Cettie exchanged deliberately modest holiday gifts, pens, ties, $500 checks, even as her personal bond portfolio topped $1 million, a ritual of frugality Chernow reads as their way of proving they had not been spoiled by wealth.
- Cettie's health collapsed steadily through this period: bedridden with "grippe pneumonia" in 1906, largely confined through 1907-1909 with emphysema and lung congestion, yet Rockefeller kept to his fixed seasonal migration between Augusta, Lakewood, Pocantico, and Forest Hill rather than staying by her side, a choice Chernow attributes partly to his discomfort around illness and mortality.
- Junior, meanwhile, bore the public ridicule his father was spared: reporters mocked his frugality (a nickel tip to a barber made headlines), his 1902 Brown University speech comparing corporate consolidation to pruning the "American Beauty" rose became a byword for ruthless capitalism, and his weekly Bible class at Fifth Avenue Baptist Church, taken over from Charles Evans Hughes in 1900, was infiltrated by reporters looking to embarrass him; he finally gave up the class in 1908 at Abby's urging.
- Junior's exposure to Standard Oil's "seamy" side, especially secret political payoffs to party bosses like Mark Hanna that executive John D. Archbold shrugged off as routine, compounded the guilt that helped trigger his nervous breakdown beginning in late 1904; Senior kept him on a modest allowance (rising from $10,000 to $18,000 a year through 1907) well into his thirties rather than transferring real wealth, in part, Chernow argues, to shield him from the antitrust liability tied to Standard Oil stock.
- The chapter's other throughline is the building of Kykuit, the family mansion at Pocantico's highest point, planned from 1904 after fire destroyed the old Parsons-Wentworth house; Rockefeller personally dictated the sun angles for each room using a rotating box-and-turntable model, then feuded for years with landscape architect William Welles Bosworth as the grounds budget ballooned from a quoted $30,000 to $750,000 by 1910.
- After the family moved in during 1908, Cettie and Senior privately found the house flawed (noisy elevator, leaking dining room, dormer windows too stuffy) and had it substantially rebuilt from 1911 to 1913; Rockefeller nonetheless delighted in the grounds' engineering, including an underground supply tunnel and a network of electric lights he could use to illuminate the whole estate at night.
