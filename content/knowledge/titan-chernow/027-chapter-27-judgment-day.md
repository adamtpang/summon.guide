# Chapter 27: Judgment Day

> **Key principle:** After years of subpoenas, a record-breaking fine, and a bribery scandal that finally drove Junior out of the company, the Supreme Court's May 1911 order breaking Standard Oil into 34 companies backfired spectacularly, turning Rockefeller from a mere millionaire into history's first billionaire.

*Synthesized from Chapter 27 of Titan: The Life of John D. Rockefeller, Sr. by Ron Chernow.*

## Key lessons

- On November 18, 1906, the federal government filed suit in Missouri to dissolve Standard Oil of New Jersey under the Sherman Act, naming John and William Rockefeller, Henry Flagler, and other executives as defendants; a 1907 government report found the trust still controlled 87 percent of kerosene refining, more than twenty times the size of its nearest rival, Pure Oil.
- In a separate 1907 Chicago rebate case, federal judge Kenesaw Mountain Landis (later baseball's first commissioner) subpoenaed Rockefeller, who fled to Massachusetts before voluntarily testifying; on the stand he gave famously evasive, halting answers ("I believe, your Honor...they operate an oil refinery in New Jersey"), winning immunity from prosecution and turning the appearance into a public-relations win even as Landis fumed.
- Landis retaliated on August 3, 1907, with a record $29.24 million fine against Standard Oil of Indiana, the largest corporate penalty in American history to that point; Rockefeller, informed of the verdict mid-round on a Cleveland golf course, reportedly said only "Twenty-nine million, two hundred and forty thousand" before hitting his best drive of the day, and privately predicted (correctly) that "Judge Landis will be dead a long time before this fine is paid" — an appeals court threw the fine out in 1908.
- During the Panic of 1907, Rockefeller became an unlikely hero of Wall Street, publicly pledging to give "half of all he possessed" to preserve the nation's credit, depositing roughly $10 million in National City Bank, and contributing to J. P. Morgan's rescue pool that kept dozens of brokerages from collapsing, a rare moment of positive press for him.
- The most damaging blow came from a bribery scandal: successor John Archbold had secretly paid Senator Joseph Foraker of Ohio roughly $44,000 and Senator Matthew Quay of Pennsylvania about $42,500 in the late 1890s and early 1900s for legislative favors; stolen Archbold letters, obtained by two disgruntled Standard Oil office boys and sold to William Randolph Hearst for $20,500, were read aloud by Hearst at 1908 campaign rallies, ending Foraker's and Sibley's political careers.
- The Archbold exposure "sickened" Junior and crystallized his decision to leave the oil business entirely for full-time philanthropy; he quietly resigned as a Standard Oil director at the January 11, 1910 board meeting, a departure first made public two months later alongside the bill to incorporate the Rockefeller Foundation.
- Around the same period Junior forged his own public identity by serving as foreman of a 1910 New York grand jury investigating "white slavery" (forced prostitution), throwing himself into the work despite admitting he "never talked to a single prostitute," and later founding the Bureau of Social Hygiene in 1913 to study urban vice and public health.
- On May 15, 1911, Chief Justice Edward White read a 20,000-word opinion ordering Standard Oil of New Jersey dissolved within six months into 34 separate companies; Rockefeller, golfing at Pocantico when he heard, reportedly told his golf partner, a local priest, simply "Buy Standard Oil," advice that proved extraordinarily prescient.
- Because Rockefeller owned roughly a quarter of the old trust and had deliberately kept Standard Oil's stated capitalization low for decades, the spun-off companies (later Exxon, Mobil, Chevron, Amoco, Conoco, and others) were revealed to be packed with hidden value; their combined share prices exploded once freed from antitrust litigation and boosted by the exploding automobile market, pushing Rockefeller's net worth to a lifetime peak of roughly $900 million by 1913.
- Chernow frames the breakup as the luckiest stroke of Rockefeller's career: freed from the trust's stodgy, aging leadership, companies like Standard of Indiana (whose scientist William Burton patented the valuable oil-"cracking" process in 1913) innovated faster, and reformers like Theodore Roosevelt were left complaining that the dissolution had, in practice, doubled Rockefeller's fortune rather than punished him.
