# Chapter 12: Insurrection in the Oil Fields

> **Key principle:** As Standard Oil neared total control of American refining, it faced its most serious challenges yet, a railroad incursion by the Pennsylvania Railroad, mass producer unrest over the "immediate shipment" pipeline policy, criminal indictment, and a rival seaboard pipeline, and Rockefeller defeated every one through a mix of financial pressure, legislative bribery, and eventual absorption of his opponents.

*Synthesized from Chapter 12 of Titan: The Life of John D. Rockefeller, Sr. by Ron Chernow.*

## Key lessons

- A new oil field discovered at Bradford, Pennsylvania sent crude prices from $4 a barrel in 1876 down to 70 cents by 1878; when Standard Oil's storage tanks overflowed despite Daniel O'Day's crews connecting new wells at a furious pace (Bradford tankage grew from about 1 million to 4.5 million barrels between April and November 1878), Standard declared an "immediate shipment" policy refusing new storage, sparking the 1878 crisis where masked, sheet-covered producer mobs threatened arson and even hanged a state investigator, William McCandless, in effigy after he cleared Standard Oil of wrongdoing.
- The Pennsylvania Railroad's subsidiary Empire Transportation Company, led by Colonel Joseph D. Potts, tried to break into refining and long-distance pipelines in direct competition with Standard around 1877; Rockefeller responded by idling Pittsburgh refineries, undercutting Empire's kerosene prices everywhere, and pressuring the Erie and New York Central to cut rates, financially crippling the Pennsylvania Railroad, which was simultaneously devastated by the violent Great Railroad Strike of 1877 (in Pittsburgh alone, strikers burned roughly 500 tank cars, 120 locomotives, and 27 buildings).
- Facing collapse, Pennsylvania Railroad president Tom Scott capitulated in October 1877, selling Standard Oil all of Empire's refineries, pipelines, and shipping assets for about $3.4 million, with $2.5 million due by certified check within 24 hours; Rockefeller personally raced Cleveland banks for cash and bought Empire's money-losing lake steamships with his own borrowed funds when Standard's board balked. Standard became the designated "evener" over a new four-railroad pool (Pennsylvania 47 percent, Erie and New York Central 21 percent each, B&O 11 percent).
- Johnson N. Camden, Standard's secret West Virginia ally, completed the conquest of Baltimore refiners by controlling barrel-stave supplies and waterfront terminal property; by December 1877 he told Rockefeller they had "cleared up every seed" of competing refining interest in Baltimore. By 1878, only five years after the Cleveland Massacre, the 38-year-old Rockefeller controlled roughly 90 percent of American oil refining.
- Independent producers, desperate to bypass Standard's rail-and-pipeline stranglehold, backed two long-distance pipeline projects: Lewis Emery Jr.'s smaller Equitable Petroleum Company (Bradford to Buffalo, opened August 1878) and Byron Benson's more ambitious Tidewater Pipe Line, a 110-mile line from Bradford over the Allegheny mountains to Williamsport, the first pipeline ever to attempt distances beyond 30 miles.
- To stop Tidewater, Standard bought "dead line" strips of Pennsylvania farmland to block its route, warned tank-car and pipe manufacturers not to deal with it, and (via Camden) secured an exclusive Maryland pipeline charter through an approximately $40,000 bribe; Standard also spent heavily (roughly $15,000, or about $220,000 today) bribing New York legislators through operatives like Smith M. Weed to kill "free-pipe" bills that would have let rival pipelines claim eminent domain.
- Despite the sabotage, Tidewater's pipeline successfully pumped oil over the mountains on May 28, 1879, a celebrated engineering feat; Rockefeller then fought a rate war that forced Tidewater to half capacity until its own owner Byron Benson proposed collusion instead of competition, and by 1883 Standard Oil controlled 88.5 percent of Pennsylvania's pipeline trade to Tidewater's 11.5 percent.
- In April 1879, a Clarion County, Pennsylvania grand jury indicted nine Standard Oil officials, including Rockefeller, Flagler, O'Day, and Archbold, for conspiracy to monopolize the oil trade; Rockefeller (living in New York) evaded extradition through political string-pulling with New York's governor and the Pennsylvania Railroad, and the criminal and civil cases were dropped after Standard Oil publicly agreed on Christmas Eve 1879 to renounce secret rebates, a pledge Chernow notes was largely rhetorical.
- Rockefeller began quietly buying newspaper influence (investing $5,000 in the Cleveland Herald and $10,000 in the Leader) and recruited former adversaries onto Standard's payroll, including producer attorney Roger Sherman and lawyer Virgil Kline, both of whom had previously won cases against the company, in order to neutralize and discredit them within the reform movement.
- New York's 1879 Hepburn Committee hearings exposed pervasive railroad favoritism toward Standard Oil (the New York Central alone had roughly 6,000 secret rate contracts) and drew a memorable tribute from William H. Vanderbilt calling Standard's executives smarter and more able than any businessmen he had encountered; John D. Archbold's evasive, high-handed testimony (including outright perjury about Standard's control of Acme Oil) exemplified the company's approach to legal scrutiny.
