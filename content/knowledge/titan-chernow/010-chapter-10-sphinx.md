# Chapter 10: Sphinx

> **Key principle:** As Standard Oil's undisputed leader, Rockefeller cultivated an almost supernatural inscrutability, silent, punctual, expressionless, and obsessed with minute cost details, while building intense personal loyalty among subordinates and turning cold and unforgiving toward those who doubted him.

*Synthesized from Chapter 10 of Titan: The Life of John D. Rockefeller, Sr. by Ron Chernow.*

## Key lessons

- In April 1874, Standard Oil moved into a new four-story building at 43 Euclid Avenue; Rockefeller arrived at exactly 9:15 each morning, immaculately dressed with an onyx "R" on his cuff links, and was famous among staff for near-invisibility, gliding silently in and out so that even secretaries said "he's never there, and yet he's always there."
- He treated silence as a weapon and a virtue, living by maxims like "Success comes from keeping the ears open and the mouth closed," and could unnerve angry visitors by simply staring at them wordlessly until they lost their composure; he trained his face into an expressionless mask so subordinates couldn't read good or bad news from telegrams.
- Despite his fearsome reputation among rivals, employees generally described him as calm, courteous, and fair; he reportedly had an unusually low resting pulse of 52, rarely raised his voice, and even let a new accountant who mistook him for a stranger and rudely dismissed his exercise machine go unpunished once the man realized who he was.
- He personally handled hiring for years, prized employees' people skills as much as technical ability ("I pay more for that ability than for any other under the sun"), paid somewhat above industry average with pensions and hospitalization, but had zero tolerance for unionizing and imposed strict moral standards, penalizing adultery and divorce among staff.
- He took a close personal interest in protege John D. Archbold's struggle with alcoholism; after an 1881 relapse, Archbold sent Rockefeller a written pledge of sobriety every Sunday for eight months, a ritual that continued despite at least one further relapse years later.
- Rockefeller delegated broad authority once he trusted a subordinate ("nobody does anything if he can get anybody else to do it"), obsessed over precise, revised business correspondence, and managed the sprawling company primarily through numbers: "I charted my course by figures, nothing but figures." He carried a small red notebook on plant visits, noting improvement suggestions that subordinates dreaded seeing him pull out.
- His famous eye for detail included ordering an experiment reducing the solder used to seal export kerosene cans from 40 drops to 38 (which leaked) and settling on 39, a change that reportedly saved $2,500 in the first year alone and far more as export volume grew; he applied similar frugality to barrel staves and iron hoops without weakening the containers.
- Standard Oil diversified beyond kerosene and naphtha starting in 1874 into paraffin wax, asphalt, lubricants, candles, dyes, and paints, and in 1880 acquired Chesebrough Manufacturing (maker of Vaseline) to strengthen its petroleum-jelly business.
- Rockefeller's relationship with his original technical partner Sam Andrews soured over Andrews's preference for large dividends over reinvestment; when Andrews angrily offered to sell his stake for $1 million in 1878, Rockefeller bought it on the spot, then quickly resold the shares to William H. Vanderbilt for a $300,000 profit. Andrews, feeling cheated, refused Rockefeller's offer to buy the stock back at the original price, a decision that cost him a fortune historians estimate could have reached roughly $900 million by the 1930s had he kept the shares; he spent his money instead on a lavish, widely mocked hundred-room Euclid Avenue mansion nicknamed "Andrews's Folly."
- The chapter records that Rockefeller's private remarks about business rivals occasionally carried an ugly ethnic edge, including a boast about having "beaten a Jew" through faster mental arithmetic in a negotiation with Dr. David Hostetter, and repeated disparagement of Sam Andrews as an "ignorant Englishman."
