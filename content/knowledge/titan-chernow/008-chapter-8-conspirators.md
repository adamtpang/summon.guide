# Chapter 8: Conspirators

> **Key principle:** Convinced that "ruinous competition" from overbuilt refining capacity was destroying the oil industry, Rockefeller helped design the secretive South Improvement Company, a rebate-and-drawback cartel with major railroads; though the scheme was exposed and killed within weeks, the six-week panic it caused let him buy up nearly all of Cleveland's rival refiners.

*Synthesized from Chapter 8 of Titan: The Life of John D. Rockefeller, Sr. by Ron Chernow.*

## Key lessons

- After the Civil War, oil prices swung wildly (from $12/barrel in 1864 to as low as $2.40 soon after) and by 1870 refining capacity was triple actual crude output, with Rockefeller estimating 90 percent of refineries operating at a loss; he concluded the industry needed "cooperation" to replace destructive competition.
- On January 10, 1870, Rockefeller, Andrews and Flagler was reorganized into the joint-stock Standard Oil Company (Ohio), capitalized at $1 million (about $11 million in today's money), with John D. Rockefeller as president, William Rockefeller as vice president, and Henry Flagler as secretary-treasurer; it paid a 105 percent dividend in its first year despite the 1869 "Black Friday" gold-market panic.
- In late 1871, Peter H. Watson (a Lake Shore Railroad official) brought Rockefeller and Flagler a plan devised by Pennsylvania Railroad chief Tom Scott: the South Improvement Company (SIC), a shell corporation through which the Pennsylvania, Erie, and New York Central railroads would grant SIC-member refiners (dominated by Standard Oil) rebates up to 50 percent, plus "drawbacks," secret rebates paid to SIC members on every barrel shipped by their non-member competitors.
- Standard Oil and its principals controlled roughly 900 of the SIC's 2,000 shares; the scheme also made Standard Oil the "evener" apportioning traffic among the three railroads (45 percent Pennsylvania, 27.5 percent each to Erie and New York Central) to end the railroads' own ruinous rate wars.
- The plan leaked in late February 1872 when a Lake Shore freight agent's substitute prematurely announced the new doubled freight rates; Oil Creek producers and refiners erupted, with 3,000 people rallying at the Titusville Opera House on February 27, 1872, denouncing Rockefeller's group as "the Anaconda" and "the Forty Thieves." A young refiner named John D. Archbold (who would later succeed Rockefeller as Standard Oil's president) was a leading voice against the SIC at that rally.
- Producers organized boycotts refusing to sell crude to SIC members, lobbied the Pennsylvania legislature and Congress, and some threatened violence; Rockefeller kept a revolver by his bed and posted guards at his home and office, while refusing to speak publicly to the press.
- Under mounting political pressure, the railroads abandoned the SIC by late March 1872, and Rockefeller formally wired producers on April 8, 1872 that the SIC contracts were void; no shipment or rebate was ever actually made under the plan.
- In the roughly six weeks between the SIC's exposure and collapse (dubbed the "Cleveland Massacre"), Rockefeller bought 22 of Cleveland's 26 refineries, including his biggest rival, Clark, Payne and Company (owned by Colonel Oliver H. Payne), for $400,000; he typically paid only a fraction of construction cost for distressed plants, arguing insolvent refiners' goodwill was worthless, while critics like refiner J. W. Fawcett said rivals were terrified into selling by rumors of Standard Oil's railroad power.
- Several refiners, including John H. Heisel and Robert Hanna (uncle of Mark Hanna), later described being warned bluntly that they could never survive competing with Standard Oil; Rockefeller maintained he never threatened anyone and was rescuing failing businessmen, telling one, "I have ways of making money you know nothing about."
- Rockefeller justified the consolidation as "cooperation" replacing "ruinous competition," framing Standard Oil in explicitly religious terms as "an angel of mercy" and "the Moses who delivered them from their folly," and insisted decades later, "It was right... If I had to do it tomorrow I would do it again the same way." He forced refiners who sold out to sign contracts barring them from reentering the oil business.
