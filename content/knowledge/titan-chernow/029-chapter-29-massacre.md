# Chapter 29: Massacre

> **Key principle:** A 1914 massacre of striking miners' families at a company the Rockefellers controlled but barely managed became the family's worst public catastrophe, and the crisis is what finally split John D. Rockefeller Jr.'s convictions from his father's uncompromising anti-labor orthodoxy.

*Synthesized from Chapter 29 of Titan: The Life of John D. Rockefeller, Sr. by Ron Chernow.*

## Key lessons

- The Rockefellers acquired control of Colorado Fuel and Iron (CFI) in November 1902 for $6 million (40 percent of stock, 43 percent of bonds), on a tip from George Gould, then installed Gates's uncle LaMont Montgomery Bowers as vice president in 1907; CFI remained a passive, absentee-owned investment that paid no dividends for years.
- Rockefeller and Junior treated union recognition as non-negotiable; Junior wrote CFI's president in 1903 that recognizing a union would be "a sign of evident weakness," and after a January 1910 mine explosion killed 79 men, Bowers blamed careless miners rather than the company.
- On September 26, 1913, roughly 9,000 CFI workers struck for union recognition, better pay, and safer conditions; coal companies evicted strikers from company housing, and evicted families set up tent colonies, the largest at Ludlow, while companies armed guards with an improvised armored car nicknamed the "Death Special."
- Junior testified before a House subcommittee on April 6, 1914, admitting he had not visited Colorado in ten years, hadn't attended a CFI board meeting since the strike began, and defended the open shop as a principle worth losing "all his property" to defend; his father rewarded the testimony with a gift of 10,000 CFI shares.
- On April 20, 1914, National Guardsmen fired on the Ludlow tent colony and set fire to it; two women and eleven children suffocated in a dirt bunker dug under one tent. Junior later wrote an internal memo insisting "There was no Ludlow massacre," attributing the deaths to accidental suffocation rather than the militia's arson.
- Public backlash was severe: Upton Sinclair organized "mourning parade" pickets outside 26 Broadway, Helen Keller called Rockefeller "the monster of capitalism," protesters stormed Kykuit and set fire to the dairy barn, and Junior began carrying a Smith & Wesson .38 in his office out of fear for his life.
- William Lyon Mackenzie King, a Canadian labor economist (later Canada's prime minister for 22 years), was hired in June 1914 to head the Rockefeller Foundation's new Department of Industrial Relations; he became Junior's closest confidant and steadily convinced him that his father's "secrecy" era of business was over and that transparency and worker representation were now necessary.
- Publicist Ivy Lee (hired mid-1914, eventually $15,000/year) coached Junior toward candor with the press, though Lee's own early "Facts Concerning the Struggle in Colorado" bulletins were criticized as one-sided, earning him the nickname "Poison Ivy" from Upton Sinclair.
- Junior's turning-point testimony came in January 1915 before the U.S. Commission on Industrial Relations, where he admitted he had "taken too narrow a view" of a director's responsibilities and later, in September 1915, personally toured the Colorado coalfields with King, donned overalls, went down a mine shaft, danced with miners' wives, and unveiled an employee-representation plan that 2,404 of 2,846 miners approved by secret ballot.
- Junior forced out Bowers over resistance to the reforms, and the episode permanently cooled his relationship with Gates, who wrote an outraged memo wishing Junior had fought unions to the point of "being carried struggling—shrieking from the court room." The chapter frames Ludlow as the moment Junior stepped out from his father's shadow, even as his "company union" plan was later outlawed by the 1935 Wagner Act.
