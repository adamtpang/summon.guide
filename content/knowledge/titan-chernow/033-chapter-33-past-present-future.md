# Chapter 33: Past, Present, Future

> **Key principle:** In his final active decades Rockefeller became a puckish, camera-friendly folk hero even as his siblings and old rivals died off, while Junior redirected the family's giving toward historic preservation, national parks, and liberal Protestantism, and Abby Aldrich Rockefeller pushed the family, against Junior's own taste, into founding the Museum of Modern Art.

*Synthesized from Chapter 33 of Titan: The Life of John D. Rockefeller, Sr. by Ron Chernow.*

## Key lessons

- Brother Frank Rockefeller died in April 1917 still estranged, reportedly fearing on his deathbed that John might visit; John nonetheless canceled Frank's outstanding loans after the funeral. Brother William died in 1922 of pneumonia after a throat-cancer diagnosis, leaving a $200 million estate almost entirely to his four children rather than to charity.
- Rockefeller made annual nostalgia trips to his boyhood Finger Lakes towns (Richford, Moravia, Owego) with brother William starting in 1919; on his last visit to the Moravia house he said "Farewell old home," and it burned down days later.
- In extreme old age (weighing under 100 pounds, shorter than his son) Rockefeller became newly playful and flirtatious, pinching women's knees under blankets on his afternoon drives, keeping a lady friend (Mrs. Ira Warner of Bridgeport), owning 60 suits, and mugging enthusiastically for Movietone newsreel cameras on his 90th birthday in 1929.
- Public opinion of Rockefeller warmed dramatically in the 1920s: Pulitzer's World called his philanthropy the wisest use of a fortune in history, and Hearst's papers praised the Rockefellers' giving as unmatched "since the ark stranded on Ararat." The public now associated him more with philanthropy than Standard Oil.
- Junior suffered chronic stress (migraines, stomach ailments, temporary deafness) from managing the fortune, checking into Dr. John H. Kellogg's Battle Creek Sanitarium in late 1922; his charitable income averaged around $11.5 million a year through the 1920s, exceeding the Rockefeller Foundation's own annual grants.
- Junior found new key advisers after Gates's 1929 death and Starr Murphy's 1921 death: lawyer Thomas M. Debevoise ("the Prime Minister") and especially Raymond B. Fosdick, who steered Junior toward internationalism, the League of Nations, and the Council on Foreign Relations (founded 1921).
- Junior pushed the family toward interdenominational, modernist Protestantism, delivering a widely noted 1917 speech at the Baptist Social Union arguing "a life, not a creed" should define religion; he recruited the controversial modernist preacher Harry Emerson Fosdick to head what became Riverside Church (dedicated 1931, built on Junior's $10 million gift), whose facade honors Darwin, Einstein, Confucius, and Buddha alongside Christian figures.
- Junior became a major conservationist: after an epiphany at the Grand Tetons in 1926 he secretly bought over 33,000 acres in Jackson Hole through the front "Snake River Land Company," land that eventually became part of Grand Teton National Park in 1950; he also funded Shenandoah, Great Smoky Mountains National Park, and California redwood preservation (including $1 million to save Bull Creek Flat).
- Junior's largest personal passion was restoring Colonial Williamsburg, Virginia, starting in 1926 (initially concealed under the code name "Mr. David"), eventually spending $55 million and calling it the project he gave more time and attention to than anything else, including Rockefeller Center; he also created The Cloisters museum of medieval art in Fort Tryon Park, which opened in 1938 built substantially on his own collection.
- Abby Aldrich Rockefeller, Junior's wife, championed modern art against his open disapproval (he called it lacking "beauty"), co-founding the Museum of Modern Art in 1929 with Lillie Bliss and Mary Sullivan; despite his personal distaste, Junior ultimately became MoMA's chief financial benefactor, donating roughly $6 million and, in 1938, the land where his own West 54th Street mansion had stood so the museum could expand.
