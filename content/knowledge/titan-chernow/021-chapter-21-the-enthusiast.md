# Chapter 21: The Enthusiast

> **Key principle:** Rockefeller applied the same time-and-motion discipline that built Standard Oil to golf, cycling, and estate landscaping, becoming a genuinely playful old man in public even as his private world absorbed the quiet unraveling of his wife's health and two of his three daughters' marriages.

*Synthesized from Chapter 21 of Titan: The Life of John D. Rockefeller, Sr. by Ron Chernow.*

## Key lessons

- Rockefeller took up golf at nearly age sixty (first full round on April 2, 1899, sixty-four strokes for nine holes) and attacked it with engineering precision: he had a photographer and later a film crew document his swing to correct flaws, staked his own foot into the ground with a croquet wicket to stop it twisting, and recorded every score in ledgers by name, date, and place; by 1906 he was spending over $27,000 a year on the game alone.
- He bought roughly 400 acres in the Pocantico Hills of Westchester in 1893 (eventually expanding to about 3,000 acres) and personally engineered its roads and landscaping, teaching himself civil engineering well enough to lay out a 3-percent grade so he could bicycle up to his own front door, and to estimate cubic feet of earth to be moved by eye.
- Rockefeller became an enthusiastic amateur health crank in retirement: he was devoted to homeopathic physician Dr. Hamilton Biggar, took up osteopathy with near-religious fervor ("Osteopathy! Osteopathy! Osteopathy!"), insisted guests chew each bite of food (and even liquids) ten times, and played a Baptist-approved card-substitute parlor game called Numerica after dinner.
- Around 1893 Rockefeller began losing his hair (alopecia), and by August 1901 it was total; he took to wearing rotating wigs for different occasions (golf, church, walks) after testing his first one nervously at Euclid Avenue Baptist Church, and the sudden change in his appearance, striking right as the muckraking era began, fed the public image of him as a hairless, sinister "monk of the inquisition."
- Philosopher William James, visiting Rockefeller at the Lakewood, New Jersey golf retreat, left a famous character sketch calling him "a most love-able person" who nonetheless radiated more "Urkraft" (primitive force) than anyone he had ever met, and "so complex, subtle, oily, fierce, strongly bad and strongly good a human being."
- Rockefeller's wife Cettie, always frail, suffered what was likely a mild stroke in April 1904 (during the height of Ida Tarbell's McClure's series) that left her nearly paralyzed and never fully recovered; she grew increasingly demanding of servants while Rockefeller treated her with elaborate, gentle indulgence, quietly overriding her wishes (like a closed window) without her noticing.
- Eldest daughter Bessie, married to philosopher Charles Strong, suffered a severe stroke or breakdown around 1902-03 that impaired her mind; she lapsed into episodes of childlike babbling in French about the family's wealth and charity, a decline the family concealed from the public behind the cover story of "delicate health."
- Daughter Edith married Harold McCormick (International Harvester heir) in 1895 and became a famously extravagant Chicago socialite ("Princess of Standard Oil"), amassing a $2 million pearl necklace and a Cartier necklace with 1,657 diamonds; the death of her son Jack from scarlet fever at Pocantico in January 1901 devastated the family and led Edith and Harold to found the John McCormick Institution of Infectious Diseases in his memory.
- Rockefeller invested heavily in International Harvester (eventually $25-30 million in stock, loans up to $60 million) to support Harold McCormick's position after J.P. Morgan partner George Perkins merged the reaper companies in 1902, but grew disillusioned with McCormick and Perkins's dividend policy and gradually sold out his position.
- Daughter Alta married the cold, rigid lawyer Ezra Parmalee Prentice in 1901 after Rockefeller had twice intervened to break up her earlier romantic attachments (to a middle-aged pastor and to a sickly young minister); Alta and Parmalee's finances became a running battle with family auditor Junior, with Rockefeller alternately withholding and then restoring allowances, eventually guaranteeing Parmalee $30,000-$60,000 a year and Alta a $50,000 allowance by the mid-1910s.
- Despite enormous wealth, Rockefeller kept his adult children on strict allowances rather than distributing capital, making Junior the family's internal auditor and a persistent source of friction with his brothers-in-law, illustrating how the same rigid financial control that built Standard Oil also governed, and strained, the Rockefeller family itself.
