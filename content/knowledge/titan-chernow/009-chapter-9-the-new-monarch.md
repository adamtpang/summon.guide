# Chapter 9: The New Monarch

> **Key principle:** Having learned that voluntary cartels like the Pittsburgh Plan and the Treaty of Titusville always collapsed from cheating, Rockefeller pivoted decisively to buying out rivals outright city by city, then cemented his monopoly by controlling the physical infrastructure of oil: tank cars, railroad terminals, and eventually pipelines.

*Synthesized from Chapter 9 of Titan: The Life of John D. Rockefeller, Sr. by Ron Chernow.*

## Key lessons

- In May 1872, weeks after the South Improvement Company collapsed, Rockefeller tried a public alternative: the National Refiners' Association (the "Pittsburgh Plan"), an open cartel with himself as president meant to set quotas and negotiate railroad rates; Oil Creek refiners rejected it as the SIC in disguise, and cheating and "free rider" refiners who stayed outside doomed it. Rockefeller dissolved it at Saratoga Springs on June 24, 1873.
- On December 19, 1872, Rockefeller signed the "Treaty of Titusville" with producers, agreeing to buy oil at five dollars a barrel (nearly double market price) in exchange for production limits; producers could not enforce discipline among themselves, cheating drove crude down to two dollars a barrel, and Rockefeller terminated the deal by January 1873, concluding voluntary alliances were "ropes of sand."
- The Panic of 1873 (triggered by the September 18 failure of Jay Cooke and Company) crashed crude prices to as low as forty-eight cents a barrel and triggered a six-year depression; Rockefeller used the downturn, much as Carnegie did in steel, to buy distressed rivals cheaply while Standard Oil's conservative finances and bank access let it survive comfortably.
- Rockefeller ran the growing company with obsessive secrecy: acquired refineries kept their original names and stationery, correspondence used code names, and he privately warned sellers not to "put on any more style" or reveal their new wealth, fearing both public exposure and attracting new competitors into refining.
- In late 1874, at a six-hour secret summit in Saratoga Springs, Rockefeller persuaded Pittsburgh's Charles Lockhart and William Warden and Philadelphia refiners to sell out after showing Warden Standard's books, proving Standard could sell kerosene below Warden's own production cost; within two years only one of twenty-two former Pittsburgh refiners remained independent.
- Rockefeller absorbed Charles Pratt and Company in New York (bringing in future Standard executive Henry H. Rogers, inventor of a naphtha-separation process) and, through protege John D. Archbold, consolidated the Titusville-area refiners via a front company called the Acme Oil Company, formed September 1875; Archbold, once a fiery anti-SIC orator, became Rockefeller's chief lieutenant and eventual successor, buying or leasing 27 refineries within months.
- In May 1875, Rockefeller covertly bought J. N. Camden and Company of Parkersburg, West Virginia (renamed Camden Consolidated Oil Company), closing his last territorial gap by capturing refiners served by the Baltimore and Ohio Railroad; by his late thirties Rockefeller effectively controlled all major American refining centers and, since no comparable crude deposits existed outside western Pennsylvania, the world kerosene market.
- Sellers were routinely offered a choice of cash or Standard Oil stock; most distrustful refiners chose cash and, in Rockefeller's telling, "laughed in their sleeves" at his offer, while those who kept stock (like Clark, Payne's Oliver Payne) were later enriched enormously.
- Rockefeller entrenched Standard's power over transportation: an April 1874 deal gave Standard control of the Erie Railroad's Weehawken, New Jersey terminal in exchange for shipping half its output on Erie tracks; Standard also financed and owned most tank cars leased to railroads, letting it dictate terms and even block competitors' shipments. The September 1874 "Rutter Circular" equalized freight rates for crude between Oil Creek and Cleveland, eliminating the last transport advantage of Pennsylvania-based refiners and provoking fresh Oil Creek protests.
- To counter the Pennsylvania Railroad's move into pipelines via its Empire Transportation subsidiary, Rockefeller directed the hard-driving Irish executive Daniel O'Day to build Standard's own pipeline network (American Transfer Company, 1874) and combined it with Jacob Vandergrift's United Pipe Lines, giving Standard roughly a third of all crude flowing out of Oil Creek and a chokehold on drillers who depended on pipeline access to sell their oil.
