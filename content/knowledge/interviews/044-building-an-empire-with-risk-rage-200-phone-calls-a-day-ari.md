# Building an Empire with Risk, Rage & 200 Phone Calls A Day | Ari Emanuel, WME & TKO

> **Key principle (Ari Emanuel):** Emanuel describes his career as a puzzle assembled on the phone: read about someone, call them (repeatedly if needed), build the relationship and let it create serendipity years later. He says the deals that built Endeavor, the William Morris merger, IMG and the UFC, worked because he committed fully to a strategy he believed in and accepted that the road would be brutal. Talking about his book "Roll the Calls," he is just as candid about the cost: decades of rage rooted in dyslexia and insecurity, and a slow transformation, through therapy and a psychedelic experience, into someone who still competes hard but no longer lets fear and legacy-chasing drive him.

*David Senra in conversation with Ari Emanuel, CEO of TKO and co-founder of Endeavor and MARI. [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QeolP6gwSb4) · 84 min · published 2026-10-07.*

## Key lessons

- **Roll the calls to create serendipity.** The book's title comes from Emanuel shouting "roll the calls" to his assistant as he walks into the office or gets in the car. He traced a chain that started with raising money for a merchant bank, reading an article by Marc Andreessen, asking him to invest, and Andreessen bringing in Egon Durban, the Silver Lake investor who later backed Endeavor, IMG and the UFC. He said he still does not know where any given call will lead, which is the point.
- **Run your own school of entrepreneurship.** Emanuel described reading George Gilder's "Life After Television" around 1992 and simply calling the author. That conversation shaped the premise he and three partners started their agency with after leaving ICM in 1995: distribution would multiply and content creators would become more valuable. He met Michael Rapino the same way, after reading about Live Nation when it was worth about 200 million dollars, and Elon Musk after reading he was starting a car company. If people do not call back, he calls again, using humor and self-deprecation so it does not read as stalking.
- **There is no such thing as a merger.** A Harvard professor told Emanuel and his partner Patrick that one side always conquers. William Morris laughed at a takeover idea in 2003, and Emanuel admits he wrecked that first meeting by losing his temper, delaying the deal four years. In between, he and Patrick poached clients and agents and listened for internal dissent, then returned when a William Morris executive suggested it was time.
- **Win the merger before it closes.** After both boards approved terms but before closing, Emanuel quietly brought William Morris's top earners in music and unscripted into his plan, because they disliked their own leadership. When he got cold feet, Patrick challenged whether he really believed they could win. In the 2008 to 2009 crisis, they paid about 24 million dollars and then 20 million to buy out two senior executives, then lost a lawsuit over a building, taking costs toward 80 million plus bonuses. Senra and Emanuel framed it as effectively acquiring a century-old company with its roster, and the best deal they did.
- **Remember who backed you when you had nothing.** When the four founders left ICM, they had a table, phones, commissions frozen in escrow by a lawsuit, and no money. Russell Goldsmith at City National lent them a million dollars without collateral, then helped again during the merger crunch. Emanuel repaid the loyalty years later during the IMG financing, when Durban found a way to allocate Goldsmith bonds he needed for a deal of his own.
- **Do not haggle away a strategy you believe in.** Competing for IMG against a bid Emanuel recalled as about 1.9 billion dollars, Durban pushed them to about 2.4 billion, arguing they would never remember the extra 400 million but would remember losing. Emanuel said he later quoted that logic internally to block a lowball on another deal. He added that projections will be wrong in both directions: the IMG college business they fought to keep proved worth little, while the international business carried everything.
- **Own an asset, not just a commission.** Emanuel, Durban and Mark Shapiro concluded that a representation business negotiating rights for everyone else should own sports content, because streaming services would need it to keep subscribers engaged. Emanuel wondered aloud why Michael Ovitz never bought an advertising agency when he took over Coca-Cola's advertising work, and suggested that question may have shaped their own push into ownership. They bought the UFC for 4.2 billion dollars amid critics calling it the most expensive deal in sports history.
- **Pitch to the end, and make one move when you have no move.** After closing, Fox stepped back, Amazon pulled a deal at the last minute after four months of negotiation, and other buyers were tied up in consolidation; Emanuel said the stress made him physically ill. ESPN's Kevin Mayer finally messaged that ESPN would meet their price mid-pitch at Viacom, and Emanuel ended the meeting immediately. When Dana White refused eight events on ESPN and insisted on ten, Emanuel called Mayer and blew up, arguing ESPN needed stars to sell its paywall. Bob Iger, who started in boxing, quietly approved ten, and days later ESPN also bought the pay-per-views.
- **Stop telling yourself a story.** Borrowing from Brené Brown, Emanuel said he told an anxious CFO at MARI, his new live-events company, to keep making calls rather than catastrophize about a fundraise, because the product, story and thesis were good. David Geffen's advice when Emanuel was afraid to merge was similar: get in traffic and get hit, meaning believe in yourself and stop standing still.
- **Rage has a ceiling.** A car accident at 32 showed him life is not a dress rehearsal, and getting sick during the UFC financing reminded him he was not invincible. He said rage came from trying to control outcomes and losing, and from insecurity rooted in dyslexia in a family that prized education. He concluded he could never reach the heights he wanted, or be happy, operating that way, and he described its toll on his marriage.
- **Get off the field so you are better on it.** Emanuel credited Jeff Bezos with telling him to be fully on the field when working and fully off it otherwise. A 2022 psychedelic experience in New Mexico, after asking his brother Zeke only whether it could kill him, helped him see "legacy" as ego; he noted almost no one remembers Alfred Sloan. He insisted it did not reduce his drive, disputing Marc Andreessen's view that psychedelics make founders work less: he still rises at 5, follows a long morning routine he calls his drug, and works constantly, but only on what feels meaningful.
- **Let a public persona open doors.** Emanuel thought "Entourage," based on him, would hurt his career; his ex-wife hated it and he avoided watching it. It ended up letting him get anyone on the phone, and Senra compared it to podcasts making cold meetings disappear. Emanuel said he had no plan B: "it was agency or die."
