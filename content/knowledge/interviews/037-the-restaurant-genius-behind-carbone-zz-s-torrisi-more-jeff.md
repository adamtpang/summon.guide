# The Restaurant Genius Behind Carbone, ZZ's, Torrisi & More | Jeff Zalaznick

> **Key principle (Jeff Zalaznick):** Zalaznick's founding thesis at Major Food Group is that almost every great dish has already been invented; the opportunity is making the best version of something people already love (better veal, better lobster, better tomato sauce) rather than inventing something new, and wrapping it in a complete story, space, service, and history, so people don't just try it once but keep coming back.

*David Senra in conversation with Jeff Zalaznick, co-founder of Major Food Group. [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7WBfRhPbS5I) · 108 min · published 2026-07-19.*

## Key lessons

- **Belief has to come before evidence, not after.** Zalaznick described going, within a single conversation with restaurateur Joe Bastianich, from "I can be like these guys" to "I'm going to dominate this," despite having no restaurant of his own yet and comparing it directly to Larry Bird realizing in three days of rookie camp that he wasn't just going to survive in the NBA, he was going to dominate it. He said this conviction, not accumulated proof, is what let him quit a content-site business and go looking for a restaurant space with total confidence.
- **The best partnership is built on shared ambition discovered in one long conversation, not a resume.** Zalaznick met his future partner Mario Carbone after a single meal so good he called ahead for a table despite not knowing the owners; the two ended up talking until 4 or 5 a.m., mapping out the exact roadmap, starting with an elevated Italian-American restaurant, then hotels, then a full hospitality company, that Major Food Group has followed for over a decade since.
- **The most counterintuitive move often unlocks the space others assume is fixed.** When Major Food Group took over the historic Four Seasons space (The Pool and The Grill), everyone assumed the room would remain what it had been for sixty years, a power-lunch destination. Zalaznick's first decision was to stop serving lunch entirely, forcing the room to re-establish itself as a dinner destination instead, because in his analysis lunch restaurants with a full liquor program don't make money the way dinner does.
- **A restaurant has to tell a complete story, not just serve food.** Zalaznick said Major Food Group researches historical menus, architecture, and the exact period of a building (down to remaking chairs Mies van der Rohe designed in 1959) so that every detail, uniform, music, service style, subconsciously reinforces one coherent narrative. He said the only metric that actually matters is whether the customer comes back, since getting a first-time visitor through the door once is easy for almost any new restaurant.
- **Take something familiar and simply make the best possible version of it.** Zalaznick's founding insight for Carbone was that his favorite Italian-American restaurants (Il Mulino, Rao's) served food that wasn't objectively very good, so his idea was to apply French-level ingredient sourcing (real veal for the chop, properly cooked lobster for the fra diavolo) to dishes New Yorkers already loved, rather than invent a new cuisine. He compared this directly to James Dyson's approach to vacuum cleaners: identifying an existing product everyone already uses and simply engineering a better version of it.
- **An act of vandalism became the company's most iconic asset.** Zalaznick negotiated to keep the original "Rocco" neon sign on Carbone's first location, paying $10,000 for the right; the outgoing tenant smashed the neon glass out of spite on his way out. Rather than replace it, Zalaznick added "Carbone" in neon above the broken "Rocco" sign, creating what became one of the most recognized signs in fine dining.
- **Reject the austere, no-substitutions style of fine dining that dominated at the time.** Zalaznick said Carbone deliberately fought against a prevailing culture of rigid tasting menus with no accommodation for preference, printing "a piacere" (whatever you want) at the top of a deliberately oversized 70-item menu, on the belief that true excellence includes being able to execute any reasonable request well, not just five fixed dishes.
- **Convert a hard, unpredictable business into a predictable one wherever you can.** Zalaznick said Major Food Group moved into private members' clubs (like ZZ's) specifically because a subscription model with annual dues removes much of the volatility inherent to restaurants, where weather, seasonality, and a hundred other variables affect a single night's business. He said the same brand equity built through restaurants also scales far beyond physical seats through packaged retail products like Carbone tomato sauce, sold in thousands of stores.
- **Use a simple, brutal heuristic to evaluate a new market.** Zalaznick said his test for whether a city is worth entering is finding two or three existing restaurants there doing $20 million or more in annual revenue; if that exists, the underlying demand is real and a well-executed, expensive concept can outcompete anything already there.
- **Reuse existing bones instead of building from scratch, and set a hard time constraint to force discipline.** To open Carbone Miami during COVID, Zalaznick gave his designer unlimited budget but only 90 days and one rule: nothing structural (kitchen, bar, bathroom location) could move. The result opened on the same floor plan as a prior restaurant that had done about $7 million a year; Carbone Miami now does over $30 million a year in the identical footprint.
