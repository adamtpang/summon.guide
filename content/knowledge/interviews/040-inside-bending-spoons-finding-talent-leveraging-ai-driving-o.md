# Inside Bending Spoons: Finding Talent, Leveraging AI & Driving Operational Excellence | Luca Ferrari

> **Key principle (Luca Ferrari):** Ferrari says Bending Spoons wins not by spotting underpriced companies but by being a dramatically better operator than any other buyer, so that almost any business it acquires is worth more inside the platform than outside it. He centralizes hiring and firing as a science built on over 100 tested signals rather than experience or CVs, insists on a culture of extreme ownership and relentless simplification (no job titles, minimal process), and reuses one shared technology platform across every acquired business so each new deal gets cheaper and faster to transform than the last. He treats the whole enterprise as a long-term compounding machine, buying to hold and integrate rather than to flip.

*David Senra in conversation with Luca Ferrari, co-founder of Bending Spoons. [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MCbHnlpwZf0) · 121 min · published 2026-09-13.*

## Key lessons

- **Set the standard as aspirationally impossible, not comfortably achievable.** Ferrari said Bending Spoons' explicit aim is to build "the best company there ever was," a goal he calls nearly impossible but still the right one to chase, because even falling short of it produces far greater rewards, learning, and talent attraction than aiming for merely adequate. He said this same all-or-nothing posture governs his personal life: a 10-out-of-10 relationship with his wife is worth more than 100 adequate ones.
- **Favor talent over experience when the field changes faster than experience compounds.** Ferrari said Bending Spoons deliberately hires young graduates over seasoned executives because customer expectations and technical practice shift so fast that years of experience go stale, and because experience gained under low standards or political incentives can make someone worse, not better. He traced this belief to his failed first startup, Evertale, where the single best contributor on a 12-person team was also one of the least experienced.
- **Build hiring into a predictive science when track records are too short to trust.** Ferrari said that for candidates without long track records, Bending Spoons relies on more than 100 tested signals (test performance, personal projects, even how a candidate treats a scheduling coordinator during the interview process) rather than CVs or interview charm, because interview politeness proved a poor predictor of actual collaborative behavior on the job.
- **Centralize hiring and firing entirely, and give hiring managers no incentive to rush.** Ferrari said Bending Spoons removes hiring decisions from team managers, whose incentives favor filling a seat quickly with an "adequate" candidate, and puts them with a dedicated talent team of engineers who are compensated on trust alone, with no bonuses tied to headcount speed, so they can hold out for exceptional fits across the company's full talent pipeline.
- **Give people more work than feels comfortable, deliberately.** Ferrari said every promising employee should have far more on their plate than fits easily in a day, both because a larger pool of possible tasks mathematically raises the expected value of what gets chosen, and because handling that "immensity of the possible" early, rather than at the top of a career, is itself a trainable skill that compounds.
- **Make complexity guilty until proven innocent.** Ferrari described "relentless simplification" as a standing rule: anyone proposing to add a rule, process, team, or feature carries the burden of proving it is worth the (nonlinear) added complexity, while anyone objecting to an addition does not have to prove anything. He gave eliminating job titles company-wide as a concrete example: incremental fixes save a little, but full removal saved what he estimated as hundreds to thousands of person-hours and ended every status dispute permanently.
- **Buy to hold and transform, never to flip, and integrate deeply instead of running acquisitions standalone.** Ferrari drew a sharp line against comparisons to private equity: Bending Spoons has never sold a material business, rebuilds acquired companies' technology and culture from the ground up, and folds each one onto a single shared operating platform (payments, AB testing, lifetime-value prediction, talent, credentials) built over roughly 13 years, so every new acquisition benefits from, and contributes to, the same infrastructure.
- **Let scale-appropriate talent access replace headcount.** Ferrari used Evernote as the concrete case: acquired for roughly 200 million dollars at about 90 million in revenue and near breakeven, it went from 350 employees to 20, while revenue grew and profitability rose sharply. He attributed this to access to far deeper talent (800,000 job applications for fewer than 300 hires in a year) and to a shared AI-powered technology platform no standalone company that size could justify building alone.
- **Use AI agents as an internal knowledge and execution layer, not just a coding tool.** Ferrari described "Old Spooner," an internal Slack-based agent with the same system access as the employee it mimics, letting a general manager diagnose a customer-reported bug, check its prevalence, find the code-level cause, and route a fix to the right engineer in about three minutes, work he said would otherwise take weeks of human coordination.
- **Only make big, irreversible decisions as late as you can afford to.** Ferrari said Bending Spoons treats "productive procrastination" (delaying a decision, not avoiding work) as an asset: for the IPO, the company knew by early 2025 that going public was likely but withheld the final commitment until spring 2026, preferring to gather more information right up until the decision could no longer be deferred.
- **Negotiate from a position you can defend without much back and forth.** Ferrari said Bending Spoons puts forward one number it considers fair and competitive rather than opening low and negotiating upward, is comfortable telling sellers to shop the offer around, and says the company has not been outbid on a wanted acquisition in at least five years, a posture he said only works because the operational advantage behind the offer is real.
