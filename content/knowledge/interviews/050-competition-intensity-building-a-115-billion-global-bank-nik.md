# Competition, Intensity & Building a $115 Billion Global Bank | Nik Storonsky, Revolut

> **Key principle (Nik Storonsky):** Storonsky describes Revolut as a machine: inputs (people, structure, products, regulation) that he watches constantly, intervening wherever a bottleneck appears, then moving to the next one. The former trader runs product as a portfolio of small, time-boxed bets judged by gross profit rather than intuition, including his own, and insists on metrics because objective numbers remove office politics. He argues that founder-led companies beat bank bureaucracies run by professional managers protecting their jobs, that Europe's caps and fragmentation forced Revolut to get genuinely good, and that founders should keep investors financially involved but away from decisions.

## Key lessons

- **Compete against your own best effort, not the field.** Storonsky said he has needed to be number one at whatever he chose since childhood sports, and that his goal now is to be the number one bank in at least 100 countries (Revolut is live in about 38 and adds 10 to 15 markets every year or two). Yet he said he barely watches competitors: he asks how an outsider would beat him, and aims to operate at close to 100 percent every day so that even a loss leaves nothing to blame himself for. Asked whether he is coming for Jamie Dimon's lunch, he said he is coming for his dinner: retail, enterprise and eventually investment banking.
- **Your opponent's real weakness may be that it is not run by a founder.** Storonsky said most bank CEOs stay three or four years, collect bonuses and play defense because their real goal is to protect their jobs. Many are not technical, so they avoid necessary but risky moves such as replacing a legacy third-party core banking system. He sees himself and the company as one entity, and argued to regulators that this makes him less of a reckless risk-taker than a professional board, since he is the person most motivated for the company to survive.
- **Do not let a shortcut on regulation become a ceiling.** Revolut started on a lighter e-money license because it took about a year instead of three or four for a bank license. Storonsky now calls that a mistake: by the time Revolut applied for a UK banking license it had seven or eight million customers, an almost unprecedented position that he said made regulators far more cautious, and the process took nearly five years. He argues a full license is unavoidable for anyone who wants salaries, savings and primary accounts, and pointed to Ireland, where he said about 80 percent of adults use Revolut, versus roughly 20 percent in the UK and France.
- **Treat the organization as a machine and fix one bottleneck at a time.** When the early team of 10 to 30 grew and speed fell, Storonsky split it into many small independent teams working on the same platform. When regulation and compliance became the constraint, he built regulatory and government affairs functions; when hiring good people fast enough became the constraint, he built a recruiting process. He described the cycle as build, hit a bottleneck, identify it, solve it, repeat.
- **Fund small bets and let the numbers kill them.** Borrowing from his trading background, Storonsky funds "bets" of up to about ten people (backend and frontend developers, a product owner, a designer) to ship a product in 9 to 18 months, then tracks each by gross profit against the cohort of roughly 50 earlier bets. Those tracking in the bottom quartile are sunset; the fast growers get more teams until they become departments and major profit lines. He said he does not trust his own intuition about what to build because it has been wrong too often, and that measuring outcomes makes internal politics irrelevant.
- **Spot one team's innovation and standardize it everywhere.** Storonsky has about 50 direct reports but often goes straight to individual designers and developers. His example: one design team uploaded Revolut's code-level design system into a language model and prompted it to produce clickable flows, about three to four times faster than drawing screens in Figma, and he then pushed that method to every designer. He said AI has already automated about 90 percent of customer support (once more than 5,000 people), sped engineers up about 30 percent, shrinks the ideal bet team toward two or three people, and that his next goal is a full-stack AI employee with its own email, Slack account and system access.
- **Hire self-guided missiles and stop spending management time on average people.** Storonsky sorts people into tiers: brilliant people set and reach their own goals, good people reach a goal once given it, average people need weekly direction, and below-average people miss even then. He fires the average as well, which he said many good executives struggle to do because they weigh empathy above winning. He hires people with top-percentile career progression at 25 to 30 and kept only two of the 50-plus experienced executives he hired in the first five years, citing a negative selection bias: the truly great experienced operators are already running their own companies or retired.
- **Let constraints, not cheap capital, train you.** Revolut ran negative gross margins for its first three to five years before optimizing to about 80 percent positive, in a European market Storonsky described as capped interchange (about 20 basis points versus roughly 1.5 percent in the US), restricted credit, country-by-country licensing, many languages and strict employment law. He contrasted this with a California habit of solving problems with money, such as hiring an expensive VP of sales and 50 reps before building a cheap, repeatable way to sell. Of 140 companies in his first coworking space, he said three survived five years and all of them worked weekends.
- **Keep investors as financiers, not players.** Storonsky compared early investors to cheerleaders who try to play the match. He said early UK backers imposed liquidation preferences, board seats and reduced founder voting rights, gave advice such as hiring experienced executives that proved wrong, and one pushed him to fire his best person for being too hardcore; he refused. Over successive rounds he removed every VC from the board. A later clash with a major investor over shareholder-agreement changes led him to start QuantumLight, a separate fund that uses machine-learning models to pick 30 to 40 companies with equal checks; he said its first, Series B, fund shows about a 56 percent IRR.
- **Buy trust in the physical world when it is cheaper than digital impressions.** Revolut spent nothing on marketing for roughly its first seven years, and Storonsky said COVID cut revenue about 70 percent because the product was still tied to travel. When he did invest, he hired marketers from gaming companies, which he saw as the biggest and most disciplined performance spenders. Because a branchless bank starts with less trust, he now treats physical space as an impression arbitrage, putting ads in about half of Europe's airports and opening a "Rev" experience store in Barcelona.
- **Use the cash machine to fund moonshots.** Storonsky said Revolut's structural edge is one ledger and one configurable system across countries, unlike incumbents stitched together from acquired banks, so entering a new market is mostly configuration. He wants the resulting cash flow to fund bets beyond finance, starting with Pragma, a transformer model trained on Revolut's roughly 30 million daily transactions for uses such as fraud and credit.
