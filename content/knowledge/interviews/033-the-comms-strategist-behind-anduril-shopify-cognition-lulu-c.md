# The Comms Strategist Behind Anduril, Shopify & Cognition | Lulu Cheng Meservey

> **Key principle (Lulu Cheng Meservey):** Meservey argues that traditional PR forces a founder's message through someone else's prism, which fails exactly when the idea is unconventional enough to matter. Her alternative, narrative sovereignty, means going directly to the small number of people who actually matter, building an unassailable manifesto that reads like a counterinsurgency's founding document, and keeping the technology itself ambient in the background of a human story rather than the thing being sold.

*David Senra in conversation with Lulu Cheng Meservey, founder of Rostra. [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DFImJfJGXl0) · 78 min · published 2026-08-12.*

## Key lessons

- **Treat a new company's launch like a counterinsurgency, not a marketing campaign.** Meservey said her training in counterinsurgency taught her that an insurgent group starting with fewer numbers, less money, and less legitimacy than its opponents can still win through sheer intensity, and that a manifesto exists to concentrate that intensity into something people cannot get out of their heads. She applied this directly to Anduril's early days, when the company had no access to mainstream media and Palmer Luckey was widely treated as a pariah in tech.
- **Go direct to the small number of people who actually matter, and stop optimizing for mass reach.** Meservey said Anduril bypassed traditional press to speak directly to Marines at Quantico, the CIA, and National Defense University, because the actual audience that mattered was never the hundreds of thousands of casual readers a mainstream story would reach. She cited Brian Armstrong appearing on a podcast with roughly 100 listeners as more valuable than a major outlet, because it reached the specific people whose opinion counted.
- **Every retelling through an intermediary waters down intensity.** Meservey described the traditional chain (founder to PR person to reporter to editor to publication to audience) as a process that loses conviction at every handoff. Her prescription is that the founder has to be the one telling the story, since only the founder has the personal reputation to make people believe something before there is evidence to back it up.
- **Put the technology in the background of a human story; do not sell it directly.** Meservey called this the "Jetsons Rule," technology that is merely ambient in a show about a family sitcom persuades far more effectively than dystopian or over-eager utopian stories that make the tech the protagonist. She said Shopify's most effective story is not about the platform but about one merchant, like SKIMS, going from someone's dream to a thriving business, with Shopify staying deliberately ambient in that telling.
- **Compress the idea into an analogy people already understand, and let someone outside the idea do the compressing.** Meservey said a founder describing a fragmented "connect home cooks to hungry customers" pitch became clear the moment she reflected it back as "turning a cook into a chef," and that a system finding operational inefficiencies became "Ozempic for companies." She said this compression works best coming from someone who is not necessarily smarter or more expert, just able to see the core the founder cannot see from inside their own thinking.
- **Recognize the audience's emotional reaction before you deliver hard news.** Meservey criticized a commencement speech in which a senior AI executive described massive coming job losses while smiling and sounding secure, arguing that failing to acknowledge the listener's fear (rather than the specific words used) is what destroys trust and makes the audience stop believing anything else the speaker says.
- **Refuse templates because originality is where the founder's actual advantage lives.** Meservey said she does not reuse templates for clients even though it would save her time, because doing so papers over the specific texture of each situation and settles for being no better than her own work months ago. She extended the same argument to AI-generated writing: relying on it puts a founder at the average of everyone using the same shared model, which is only an improvement if the founder is genuinely below average to start.
- **Beauty and authenticity are the same signal, and both are hard to fake.** Meservey said she has never seen a "fake" company (bought engagement, templated launch videos, a random-account follower base) that was also beautiful, because a fake project has no one who cared enough to put real effort into it. She pointed to General Matter's simple, deliberated website and short manifesto as evidence that visible restraint and care, not more content, is what reads as real.
- **People will invent a narrative if you do not give them a coherent one, so give them yours.** Using Nabokov's story "Signs and Symbols" as an illustration, Meservey argued that scattered facts (a hire here, an office opening there, a fundraise) get woven into a story by the audience whether the founder likes it or not; the only thing a founder controls is whether that story is the one they intended.
- **Trust your own conviction against consensus, and never change the thing you are compounding.** Meservey said real belief means being willing to side with one person's judgment (your own) over a million people's doubt, because compounding requires holding a single, unchanging principle; changing what you are compounding restarts the clock rather than building on it.
