# From HOA Management to $4B in Revenue & $6.3B Deal for Amex GBT | Alexander Taubman, Long Lake

> **Key principle (Alexander Taubman):** Taubman founded Long Lake three years ago on the bet that AI's hardest problem is no longer whether the models work but getting them into the messy legacy systems of ordinary businesses. Long Lake's answer is to be the owner rather than the vendor: buy profitable, high-retention service companies, put engineers next to the people in the field, and run every business on Nexus, a shared AI platform that gets better with each industry it enters. He measures success by revenue growth and headcount growth, not cuts, and treats being a permanent, never-selling owner as the thing that lets small annual improvements compound into something enormous.

*David Senra in conversation with Alexander Taubman, co-founder of Long Lake. [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=w3-nMklTFjY) · 62 min · published 2026-09-30.*

## Key lessons

- **The bottleneck has moved from intelligence to diffusion.** Taubman said that four years ago models could not reliably do addition, and now they are solving famous open math problems, yet the economy barely looks different. After 10 to 15 years working with founder-owned businesses, he concluded the hard part is legacy data, workflows and change management. Long Lake exists to carry frontier capability into those businesses, and he framed the cash it generates as part of what funds the next round of model scaling.
- **Own the business instead of selling it software.** Investors have asked to license Nexus, but Taubman said Long Lake has never wanted to sell software because a vendor does not care about the outcome. Senra tied this to Larry Ellison's point that adoption is a human problem, not a software problem. Taubman added a twist: owning the company does not mean forcing anyone to use the tools. If a tool has to be mandated, he said, the platform is not good enough.
- **Treat your own employees as the customer.** Long Lake thinks of its roughly 30,000 team members as design partners and wants the tools good enough to spread virally inside each company. He said activating a cohort used to take around six months and now takes days. Employees describe it as no longer feeling like work, and Taubman expects that to become a retention moat: nobody wants to leave for a competitor down the street without the tools.
- **Productivity gains should add heads, not cut them.** Taubman called it a contrarian, positive-sum view: Long Lake's oldest holdings in HOA management are hiring more people, not fewer. His analogy was a salesperson whose AI help triples their quota; you want more of those people, not a third as many. Senra stressed that the common caricature (buy, gut, replace humans) is the opposite of the actual thesis.
- **Build one horizontal platform and let every new industry improve it.** Taubman said 70 to 80 percent of Nexus is shared across industries: model routing across multiple providers, security and agent orchestration. The last 20 to 30 percent, mapping each business's workflows and data, is the hard part, and roughly 70 percent of the engineering team sits with field staff across 15 to 20 states doing it. He cited Kelly Johnson's Skunk Works and Danaher's motto of common sense rigorously applied.
- **A capability built for one vertical pays off in the next.** In HOA management, an email about finding a snow removal vendor could take 45 minutes to an hour to research and answer. Nexus now drafts it overnight, often in the manager's own voice, so the reply takes five or ten minutes to edit. Because about 55 percent of inbound travel-agent requests also arrive by email, that tool is ready on day one inside Amex GBT.
- **Run a wide funnel and a very tight filter.** Long Lake keeps 15 to 20 candidate industries force-ranked on a whiteboard and sees 4,000 to 5,000 deals a year, yet enters only one or two new industries annually. It had built a travel thesis for about a year before the Amex GBT deal. Hiring works the same way: about 75 percent of hires come from referrals, and this quarter it hired roughly 3 percent of close to 1,000 applicants. The rule is that each new hire must raise the team's average.
- **Remove threshold resistance.** Taubman's grandfather, a shopping-center pioneer, studied how even a small step or a dark hallway at a store's entrance cut foot traffic, and moved doors to the front with no threshold. Taubman applies the same idea to AI adoption: make the tool so easy that people simply walk in. The company name comes from Long Lake Road in Michigan, where the family business sat for decades.
- **Learn to buy companies before you need to.** Taubman's training ran through Goldman's Special Situations Group, where a mentor let him manage a couple of hundred million dollars of capital in his early twenties, and then almost a decade at Oaktree working with Howard Marks and Bruce Karsh. On nights and weekends he helped run Taubman Capital, the family's own money, doing close to 50 founder-company deals, including assembling a national platform from about 20 local go-kart tracks.
- **Pick partners who raise your ambition, not your valuation.** When Long Lake weighed several acquisitions, an early investor asked why not do them all. Taubman said the best gift a partner can give is making you more ambitious, and that his father and grandfather kept real estate partners for 50 years. General Catalyst led the first outside round of about $100 million, and Taubman said they believed in the thesis before the team fully did.
- **Never sell, and let compounding do the rest.** Long Lake has bought close to 40 companies across five industries and sold none. Taubman expects over $4 billion of revenue next year from businesses already earning 20 to 25 percent plus margins, enough to compound without raising again. From Henry Singleton he takes "stay flexible" rather than detailed plans, and from John Malone root-cause thinking about which businesses survive a post-AGI world. Tripling value every five years for 20 years, he said, is how you reach a trillion dollars.
