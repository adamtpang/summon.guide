# Building Defense Technologies to Protect Democracies | Torsten Reil, Helsing

> **Key principle (Torsten Reil):** Reil argues that autonomy in warfare is no longer optional because remote control and human presence on the battlefield do not survive contact with drones and jamming. The winning posture is deterrence: mass-produce hardware that is good enough, push accuracy and coordination into software, and make an attack too costly to attempt. None of that happens without founders willing to commit unpopular, high-conviction capital before the market agrees with them.

*David Senra in conversation with Torsten Reil of Helsing. [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OduvOCrVpgo) · 84 min · published 2026-08-26.*

## Key lessons

- **Autonomy stopped being optional once jamming defeated remote control.** Reil described how video links needed to fly a drone are jammed along most front lines, and GPS is jammed too, so a drone must either trail a fiber optic cable or navigate and strike without a human in the loop. Once the other side's drones are autonomous as well, he said the era of sending humans into tanks, artillery, or infantry positions is effectively over.
- **Deterrence, not combat, is the actual goal.** Reil said the company was built so that a large enough number of coordinated strike drones at a border makes invasion prohibitively costly, aiming to get conflict itself down to zero rather than to win a war after it starts. He framed this as the same underlying idea as Cold War deterrence: be so costly to attack that no one tries.
- **Simplify the hardware and let software absorb the complexity.** The operating thesis is to design hardware "good enough" to mass produce, then use AI to regain the accuracy and coordination that expensive, exquisite systems used to provide in the metal itself. That shift let a strike drone line scale from dozens of units a month to a baseline of 1,000, then to 3,000, once the underlying programs of record existed to justify the volume.
- **Take the highest-risk government contract first, on purpose, because you can afford to fail at it.** Reil said the founders decided early to go straight for a program of record (a funded, official government contract as prime, not subcontractor) rather than chase quick revenue, specifically because Daniel Ek's roughly 100 million euro commitment meant they could survive losing the first attempt. They won an electronic-warfare upgrade contract for the Euro Fighter as the "dark horse" against established consortia, becoming, in his account, the fastest founding-to-program-of-record run in the sector.
- **Ambition, not regulation or capital, is the actual limiting factor.** Reil argued that roughly 95 percent of company success comes down to aggression and ambition, calling excuses about bureaucracy or the tax environment a distraction from the real gap: too few European founders willing to set a target as large as Spotify's and treat anything below that as failure.
- **Stop selling the company; the game itself is the reward.** Reil and Senra both pushed back on founders who sell and retire, with Reil naming this pattern as culturally reinforced in Europe (an easy, pleasant retirement) and describing his own team's decision, a few months into Helsing, to go "full throttle" toward total success or total failure rather than build a modest, sellable business.
- **Hiring pressure and performance accountability have to be built as one linked system.** Reil said Helsing signed a recruitment agency before the company was even founded, because a full hiring pipeline is what makes it low-cost to move an underperformer out; if a role can be refilled quickly, managers stop tolerating attitude problems just to hit a deadline. Quarterly calibration reviews focus on interrogating why a manager gave a rating, not on rating the employee directly, so the bar for "good performance" stays consistent company-wide.
- **Culture emerges from talent density, not from a workshop.** When the team spontaneously rewrote Helsing's values, "whatever it takes" and "the truth is in the field" came out of employees describing real front-line experiences, not a facilitated exercise. Reil's read: get exceptional people with real initiative in a room and the culture writes itself.
- **Distribute manufacturing to remove the single point of failure.** Helsing's "resilience factories" are deliberately not one large centralized plant, and its autonomous fighter jet is designed to take off from a road or highway rather than a fixed airbase, because a concentrated facility or runway is an easy, obvious target in a drone-saturated war.
- **Public commentary produces nothing; building is the only lever that matters.** Reil called social-media commentary "entirely worthless," arguing it is incentivized by platforms even though it has no positive output, and that founders are undervalued relative to commentators precisely because founders are too busy having real impact to narrate it.
