# Product Mastery, Pain Tolerance & Relentless Ambition | Peter Rahal of David Protein, Medici & RXBAR

> **Key principle (Peter Rahal):** After selling RXBAR, Rahal tried life as an investor and found it miserable: the feedback loops were too long and he could not stand sitting in the back seat behind founders with less drive. He came back to the one domain where he can personally fix the product, food, and built David Protein around a refined brand identity, a protein bar as the wedge into a broader food platform, and an organization he treats as a product in its own right. He is candid that his edge comes from a childhood chip on his shoulder and a taste for suffering, and that his job is to keep finding what blocks the flow of the business and remove it.

## Key lessons

- **If you need short feedback loops, investing will make you miserable.** After the RXBAR sale, Rahal moved to Miami and set up a family office. He said the real skill in private investing is winning allocations in obvious deals, and that waiting five years to learn whether a call was right was torture compared with operating. He assumed every founder had his tenacity, and kept finding himself the one pointing out that the car was on fire, which he said is a bad sign for any company.
- **Return to the domain where you can fix the product yourself.** Rahal had false starts in garbage recycling, synthetic biology and other consumer goods, and spent about three years getting over the feeling that he should do something grander, partly from Elon's influence. His rule as investor and operator: if the leader has to call someone else to fix the product, the company is in trouble. He has been in food since age 12 and accepted that "protein bar guy" might be his epitaph.
- **Get your life in order before you go all in.** Rahal described his style as all in and burning bridges: friends drift away and his health suffers. After a marriage and divorce he attributes to hubris, all within about nine months, he deliberately waited, found his wife, and started David once his non-compete ran out in October 2022, putting in $2 million of his own money.
- **Treat a brand like a person.** Rahal described a brand as having parents (the founders), values, a tone of voice, clothes and friends, meaning its partnerships, such as David's work with Andrew Huberman. David's values, intelligence, beauty and discipline, come from Michelangelo's sculpture and the chisel that made it. He said he spent about six months on the identity, and once it is clear, marketing is just hammering the same points for decades, the way the oldest brands did.
- **Make the product itself the marketing.** David's comparison chart ranked boiled cod first and David second on protein per calorie, because a chart where you win everything looks fake. Then the team actually sold frozen cod online at around $55, which Rahal admitted had no product-market fit but worked as a riddle that kept the conversation on protein-to-calorie ratio. He calls this product as marketing, and said the food industry is boring enough to need it.
- **Kill any single point of failure.** David depended on EPG, a patented modified fat from a single supplier, Epogee, that delivers fat's taste and mouthfeel with far fewer calories. David grew from about 90 to 150 percent of Epogee's available supply. From day one Rahal had negotiated a supply agreement with change-of-control protection and first claim on inventory, and when Epogee approached him he bought the company. Rivals without agreements lost supply, an antitrust suit was dismissed three times, and he said he handled the communication poorly and should have called those founders himself.
- **Choose investors by how they diligence you, not by price.** Rahal turned down a call with Neil Mehta's Greenoaks, saying a food company should grow on profitable sales and a credit line. Then about 20 former RXBAR employees told him they were being paid to describe his leadership. When the Epogee deal came he raised $85 million from Greenoaks and Valor without an auction, saying an auction burns management time and that he would rather price the round so investors make money.
- **Design the organization for speed, even at a cost.** Medici, named for the family that created conditions for the Renaissance, centralizes cash, law, people and regulatory work while semi-autonomous business units, led by former founders or product people, run their own P&Ls with cross-functional supply, demand and finance teams. Rahal accepts duplicated payroll because speed is the design goal, and said he would leave if the company became a "big, fat, stupid" organization.
- **Hire founders and people with something to prove.** Rahal said his best people are former founders, including one recruited after David bought up his supply, and that he likes "some inexperience and some damage." He said corporate hires must be "baptized" through onboarding, including packing boxes, so they drop cover-your-ass habits.
- **Lead by reacting to problems, then getting out of the way.** Rahal has about 25 direct reports and expects them to bring him problems rather than have him manage their to-do lists. He dislikes founder titles as anti-meritocratic and calls his core job "reactionary leadership support": spot what blocks the river, fix it, step back. He admits he rarely acknowledges wins and asks others to mark milestones for him.
- **Channel the chip on your shoulder or it turns destructive.** Rahal traced his anger and contrarian streak to being labeled disabled for dyslexia and overhearing teachers question whether he was stupid. Therapy and psychedelics did not erase it, so he channels it into exercise and company building. He said David will do about $300 million this year and is run-rating over $400 million after two years, with products spanning bars, ready-to-drink shakes, frozen desserts and confections.
