# How Mark Zuckerberg, Palmer Luckey, Josh Kushner & Scott Wu Think | Jeremy Stern

> **Key principle (Jeremy Stern):** Stern, who writes long-form profiles for Colossus, says he never starts with a person. He starts with a question (is defense tech real, is there an AI leader people could trust, is the American dream still alive) and then goes looking for the one life that best answers it, spending six months to a year and dozens of interviews until "a whole galaxy opens up" and the piece finds its true subject. Across Zuckerberg, Palmer Luckey, Scott Wu and Josh Kushner, the common thread he and Senra keep returning to is founders who refuse to sell or step away, and whose competitive drive is matched by a coherent sense of what they are actually building. His own operating belief came from a college professor: a gift is an obligation, and wasting it is a kind of sin.

## Key lessons

- **Start with the question, then find the person who embodies it.** Stern said every profile begins with a theme he already cares about before he picks a subject. The Palmer Luckey piece started after October 7th, when Israel's high-tech defenses failed against attackers using hang gliders and construction equipment, and Ukraine mixed drone swarms with trench warfare. His question was whether defense tech was real or hype; Luckey was meant to be a 3,000-word interview and became a 15,000-word life story.
- **Give the research enough time for the real subject to surface.** For the Zuckerberg piece Stern spent five or six months and spoke with almost 40 other people, including critics and rivals. He usually asks for at least two 90-minute conversations separated by a long gap, to catch a subject in two different environments. He said it is usually only after reading every book and talking to family and colleagues that he knows what the piece is really about.
- **Pay attention to what a founder's chosen history says about him.** Zuckerberg's fascination with Augustus goes back to high school Latin, and Stern said he can still recite lines of Virgil at 42. He once ended early meetings by shouting "Domination," until Sean Parker warned it could surface in an antitrust case, and he named his personal AI agent after Agrippa, Augustus's key lieutenant. Stern found it revealing that Zuckerberg remembers Augustus mainly as the man who turned a pillage economy into a trade economy, leaving out the standing army and the end of the republic.
- **The edge can be unsexy, methodical improvement over decades.** Senra and Stern discussed Zuckerberg's own framing that his advantage is sitting with a team and improving one product for a very long time, without needing to raise money or ask permission. Stern said Meta's AI bull case rests on 20 years of dull work (data-center contracts, tax incentives, municipal negotiations) that left it owning distribution to roughly half of humanity, while pure AI labs still rent compute.
- **Being able to say no to a fortune is the tell that cannot be faked.** Stern pointed to Yahoo's roughly billion-dollar offer for Facebook around 2006: management and the board leaned toward yes, Zuckerberg said no immediately, and much of the early management team left over it. He and Senra put Zuckerberg and Elon Musk in a rare category of founders who will likely never step back, and said Scott Wu shows the same refusal by turning down acquisition offers for Cognition.
- **Ruthless competitors do not have to be cruel.** Stern said that across family, colleagues and even enemies, the two things people repeated about Zuckerberg were that his competitive drive is underestimated and that he is not actually unkind to people. Senra quoted a former researcher calling him a terminator who never stops. Stern drew a line between Zuckerberg, competitive at the one thing he does, and Wu, who Stern understands will start a dice or coin game in the five minutes before a meeting just to win something.
- **When a subject is unguarded, get out of the way.** Stern went looking for an AI leader who was a humanist people might trust, and Scott Wu's name came up most. Because Wu was not media trained and was willing to be introspective, Stern broke from his usual narrator-driven form and let much of the piece read close to a transcript. He said Wu was the subject who seemed most plausibly the same person in every setting.
- **Founders who arrive late, through genuine interests, can be the most interesting.** Stern said investors like founders whose idea traces back to childhood, but he also values people who only reached company-building after years of following what they loved. He traced the Josh Kushner piece to a family story running from the Bielski partisans in the Belarusian forest to presidents dining in the grandmother's home, and to Kushner asking Rick Rubin, in effect, what to do if he loses the magic.
- **Bring craft from outside the field to the field.** Stern came from politics, foreign policy and the army, not tech. He said technologists are now the most culturally central figures in America, as physicists were in the 1940s, yet the prose covering them rarely lasts. His bet with Colossus founder Patrick O'Shaughnessy was to bring the American long-form magazine tradition of The New Yorker and Esquire to business writing.
- **A gift creates an obligation.** Stern's Rick Rubin figure was his college Russian professor, Natalia, who had escaped the Soviet Union with her infant daughter and whom he called every Sunday during his year and a half in Russia. She told him that wasting a gift on drinking and drift was a sin against his own future. He named his daughter after her and still keeps her voicemails backed up, and he said that idea is the lens he now brings to everyone he writes about.
