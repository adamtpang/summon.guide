# Building One of AI's Fastest-Growing Companies | Mati Staniszewski, ElevenLabs

> **Key principle (Mati Staniszewski):** Staniszewski frames ElevenLabs as a research lab paired to a product deployment engine, deliberately restricted to audio so the team never dilutes its one true advantage against far larger competitors. He treats forward deployed engineers as a two-way pipe: they solve one customer's integration problem, then carry the reusable pattern back into the product so every later customer benefits. He turned down repeated acquisition offers because he believes the company's founders, not a buyer's balance sheet, are the ones best positioned to keep building toward what he sees as a generational shift in how people communicate with technology.

*David Senra in conversation with Mati Staniszewski of ElevenLabs. [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RFccAuyPPOg) · 71 min · published 2026-09-09.*

## Key lessons

- **Let your first idea fail usefully before committing to a narrower one.** Staniszewski said ElevenLabs started in 2022 trying to solve dubbing for Polish audiences, but two things stopped that: the underlying speech research (transcription, translation, regeneration) was not good enough anywhere yet, and the creators they pitched kept saying they had more urgent problems, like narrating a script before recording. Rather than force the original idea, the team narrowed to just making generated speech sound genuinely human first.
- **Pick one narrow area where you can build the real advantage, and say no to everything adjacent.** Staniszewski said ElevenLabs stays solely focused on audio research because that is where the team believes its edge is durable; on the product side, the test for any new opportunity is whether audio is the actual bottleneck. He gave the example of pausing early work on lip-dubbing and avatars two years ago because the bottleneck there was video quality, not audio, so the company's strength would not have mattered.
- **Copy the customer-immersion habit that made you effective at your last job.** Staniszewski said the forward deployed engineer model came directly from his time at Palantir, where new hires were sent to work physically alongside customers (he described flying to Aberdeen within his first weeks) instead of staying behind an approval process the way he had at BlackRock. At ElevenLabs, FDEs sit inside the product team, not go-to-market, specifically so they can push the product in the direction real integration work reveals.
- **Treat every hard customer deployment as R&D you can resell to everyone else.** Staniszewski said the second and more often missed half of the FDE model is bringing the domain knowledge learned solving one customer's problem (a healthcare workflow, a CRM integration) back into the product, so the next customer in that domain benefits without repeating the same hard integration work. He called this accelerating R&D through the customer base itself.
- **Prove impact at the smallest scale before expanding a customer relationship.** Staniszewski walked through Deutsche Telekom as an example: marketing content came first because it was the safest, most provably valuable use case two years ago, and only after that value was demonstrated did the team move to support (which required integrating with CRM systems and phone infrastructure) and then in-network agent deployment.
- **Make transparency and no titles do the coordination work that hierarchy usually does.** Staniszewski said ElevenLabs runs on small autonomous teams (usually under ten people), a flat structure with roughly five layers max, and near-total internal document access, so that a "best idea wins" culture can function without needing managers to broker information. He said AI is what makes this scale: teams can already get direct signal from the person closest to a problem instead of a manager's summary.
- **Put engineers inside every function, not just inside engineering.** Staniszewski said ElevenLabs infuses talent, ops, legal, and other traditionally non-technical teams with engineers who both automate work and raise the whole team's technical fluency, and pointed to Bending Spoons founder Luca Ferrari doing something similar by centralizing HR under engineers as a comparable example he had heard.
- **Design for imperfection instead of eliminating it.** Staniszewski said ElevenLabs initially tried to build a voice agent with zero disfluencies, and satisfaction collapsed until they deliberately reintroduced the "ums," pauses, and hesitations that make speech sound human. He connected this to why people bond more with podcast voices than written prose: voice carries emotion, intonation, and imperfection that text cannot.
- **Refuse acquisition if the company is still your best idea.** Staniszewski said ElevenLabs has turned down three or four concrete acquisition offers, most recently in mid-2025, because he and his co-founder believe they are early in a once-in-a-generation shift in human-computer communication and see continuing to build independently as the only option that matches the size of that opportunity.
- **Let voice carry information text cannot, and use that for accessibility, not just marketing.** Staniszewski described ElevenLabs restoring speech for people who lost their voices to ALS or throat cancer, including a musician who returned to touring with his old band using an AI-recreated voice and a former congresswoman who used a restored voice to speak in Congress after losing hers; he said over 10,000 people have had voices rebuilt this way.
