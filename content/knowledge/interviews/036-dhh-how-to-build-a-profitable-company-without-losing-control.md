# DHH: How to Build a Profitable Company Without Losing Control

> **Key principle (David Heinemeier Hansson):** Hansson argues that scarcity, not talent, is what forces good software (and good art) to stay simple, pointing to Getting Real's half-page chapters and the first Star Wars and Terminator films as proof that constrained resources produce sharper work than unlimited ones. He built 37signals around distrusting that instinct in himself: keep teams small, keep old product versions running unchanged for customers who bought them, and refuse investors or a board so no one else's agenda can dilute the discipline.

*David Senra in conversation with David Heinemeier Hansson (DHH), co-founder of 37signals. [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=76rR68ktQvo) · 102 min · published 2026-07-26.*

## Key lessons

- **Constraints are a feature you have to protect, not a limitation to escape.** Hansson said the first version of Basecamp shipped in 380 hours because he only had 10 hours a week to build it, and that this scarcity is exactly what kept the product from bloating. He said AI acceleration now threatens the same discipline: with ten agents and unlimited hours, bloat arrives on its own, so distilling and cutting features has become a deliberate, harder-won practice rather than an automatic byproduct of limited time.
- **Treat an old product version like a beloved printer: never force an upgrade on someone who just wants the job done.** Hansson described customers who signed up for the 2004 version of Basecamp and are still happily using it today, unaware of sixteen years of new features, because it solved their one problem and they don't want it to change. 37signals kept that original version running as a standalone, nearly expense-free line that "is still making millions of dollars" in pure profit, alongside two newer, actively evolved versions for customers who want more.
- **A guiding star who sees further ahead can pull you into a shift you were resisting.** Hansson credited Tobi Lütke's insistence on AI's coming impact, backed by Shopify's internal tools built years ahead of anything commercial, with moving him from abstract enthusiasm for AI to actually adopting it in his own workflow. He compared it to racing: a driver who looks at the apex rather than the turn-in point sees further ahead, and Lütke's ability to keep his gaze there, while running a 7,000-person company, is what finally convinced Hansson to change his mind.
- **Give people a finished, curated product; don't hand them a construction kit.** Hansson said his Omarchy Linux distribution is built on the principle of omakase, chef's choice, arguing most people can recognize quality instantly but can't articulate what they want, so the builder's job is to make every decision for them rather than leave them "assembling their own burger." He said the same principle drove his early adoption of Ruby: taking an underused but well-designed tool and packaging it so other people could realize its value without the setup cost he had already paid.
- **Out-teach your competition instead of trying to outspend them.** Citing Kathy Sierra's writing as the direct source of the idea, Hansson said 37signals could never out-market Microsoft with more advertising, so the company's entire strategy for two decades has been giving away more insight, software, and observations than anyone else, on the belief that a reciprocal exchange (Charlie Munger's phrase) will convert some fraction of that audience into paying customers.
- **A gift that costs you real effort earns respect that unsolicited criticism doesn't.** Hansson distinguished between a paying customer's feedback, which he treats as an earned exchange he owes real consideration, and unsolicited criticism from someone who spent five minutes reacting to four thousand hours of his work, which he considers a privilege that has to be paid for. He said the deepest version of this principle is retiring from having customers altogether, continuing to build and share things purely because he enjoys it, with no claim on his time from anyone.
- **Turning down VC money because you don't trust yourself to resist it is a legitimate strategy.** Hansson said 37signals had roughly forty VC firms interested after Basecamp's early traction, and turned nearly all of them down specifically because he didn't trust himself not to be tempted by a large check that would ultimately compromise the business. Jeff Bezos's early investment was different, he said, not mainly for the money but because Bezos's confidence in their instincts ("You should do that") gave them the reassurance to keep saying no to everyone else.
- **Permanent cost discipline compounds more reliably than chasing revenue.** Hansson quoted Andrew Carnegie's line that profits and prices are cyclical but cost savings, once achieved, are permanent, and said he has direct control over expenses in a way he never has over whether a customer decides to pay. He described genuine satisfaction in cutting a small recurring expense, not primarily for the money saved but because it makes the whole business run more like an efficient engine with a smaller, higher-competence team.
- **When an internal minority threatens to consume the company's culture, cut deep and fast rather than manage it slowly.** Hansson said 37signals banned political discussion in company channels in 2021 after realizing an activist contingent risked "eating the entire company, the soul of the company," and offered up to six months' severance to anyone who wanted to leave over the policy. About a third of the 60-person company took the offer, and he called the resulting cultural clarity worth every dollar spent.
- **Outside allies matter most exactly when you feel most isolated.** Hansson said Marc Andreessen, introduced through Tobi Lütke during the 2021 fallout, was unexpectedly generous with contacts, historical context on the broader activist movement, and reassurance that 37signals was not uniquely under siege, despite Hansson's own history of criticizing venture capital. He said that support gave him and Jason Fried the fortitude to hold the policy without apologizing or reverting while a large share of the public was hostile.
- **Enjoying the material rewards of capitalism openly is not in tension with building for independence.** Hansson argued there is a moral case for reinvesting success into the economy (buying the car built by Italian craftsmen) and rejected the "lifestyle business" label leveled at 37signals, since the company proves profitability, independence, and a 40-hour week are compatible rather than a compromise on ambition.
