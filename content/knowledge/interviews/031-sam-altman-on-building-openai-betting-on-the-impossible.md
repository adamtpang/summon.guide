# Sam Altman on Building OpenAI & Betting on the Impossible

> **Key principle (Sam Altman):** Altman describes OpenAI's founding thesis as a straight application of power-law investing logic to research: fund the non-consensus, high-conviction bet on AGI that almost everyone in the field called impossible in 2015, because the payoff if right dwarfs everything else. He carries the same discipline into running the company now, ruthlessly killing good products (Sora, the Atlas browser) to protect focus on the few things that matter, and insists that iterative deployment, shipping something you are still embarrassed by, is both the best way to build a good product and the best way to build a safe one.

*David Senra in conversation with Sam Altman, co-founder and CEO of OpenAI. [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kG8AoExkX40) · 78 min · published 2026-08-23.*

## Key lessons

- **Fund the non-consensus bet, not the crowded one.** Altman said that when OpenAI started chasing AGI in 2015, the field's intellectual giants dismissed it, while in the same year there were "many many thousands" of founders building nearly identical photo-sharing apps. His investing background taught him to look for founders and researchers with a real, differentiated, possibly-wrong conviction rather than a thin variation on whatever the herd is already doing.
- **Two AI risks matter, and concentrating power to fix one creates the other.** Altman named loss of control (AI becoming too powerful to guarantee containment) and power concentration (one company, model, or person accumulating too much control) as the two risks he worries about most. He rejected the "benevolent dictator" pitch, curing disease and cutting costs in exchange for people's autonomy, as fundamentally anti-human, arguing people should stay deeply empowered over their own future rather than trade it away in the name of safety.
- **Ship the version you are embarrassed by; that is also how you make it safe.** Altman said OpenAI's whole approach, learned from Y Combinator, is to put technology into the world, watch where it breaks, and fix it in public rather than trying to solve every safety question inside a closed lab. He pointed to nearly four years of ChatGPT reaching a billion weekly users as evidence this iterative approach produced far more safety progress than most experts predicted.
- **The hardest skill in a company is killing good ideas to fund great ones.** Altman named this as the thing he is personally "terrible at" and said every entrepreneur is bad at it by nature. He cited killing Sora (a good, fun product that used too much compute) and the Atlas browser (which he called "the best web browser" but not the most important use of talent) to keep compute and people concentrated on general-purpose intelligence.
- **Be a platform, not a product company, and sell at every point on the cost curve.** OpenAI's stated strategy is one direct interface (the merged ChatGPT and Codex) plus one API, rather than competing product-by-product with its own customers. Altman said the company aims to offer both frontier-quality intelligence for science and cheap, fast intelligence for high-volume work, letting the ecosystem build whatever specific products it wants on top.
- **Notice your own revealed preference, not your stated one.** Altman admitted that despite building tools like Codex, he still clicks through email and keeps a to-do list the same way he has for twenty years, calling it "psychologically inconsistent": he would say he prefers the new way, but his actual behavior says otherwise, which he takes as evidence the product world has not yet had its "iPhone moment" for AI-native workflows.
- **Seek out advisors who can't be predicted, not ones who validate you.** Altman named Paul Graham and Peter Thiel as the two people whose thinking is least like extrapolating the next likely word, and therefore the most valuable when he is stuck. He cited Thiel's advice, two months after ChatGPT launched, to ignore the pressure to chase other product lines and double down purely because the simple text box was already working, as a clear example of "simple genius" cutting through internal doubt.
- **Learn far more from your successes than your failures.** Altman said failures mostly teach generic lessons about grit that people already half-know, while dissecting what specifically worked in Y Combinator or OpenAI gave him lessons he could actually reapply. He credited YC's broader shift, more capital and leverage flowing to young technical founders without a proven résumé, as a precondition without which he does not think OpenAI could have existed.
- **Running a research lab with no product for years breaks the normal startup playbook.** Altman described OpenAI's first four and a half years before any product launch as the opposite of YC advice, and said the team had to invent substitute signals for "do customers like this," including public leaderboards during its Dota 2 reinforcement-learning work and demos for external experts researchers wanted to impress, because there was no real customer feedback to replace.
- **Economic and social adoption of a new technology moves slower than the technology itself.** Altman said he expected far more software disruption right after GPT-4 than actually happened, attributing the gap to sheer economic inertia, people keep buying from the same vendors and working the same way, and called that inertia a partly healthy brake on rapid change rather than purely a drag on progress.
