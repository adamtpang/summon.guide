# Founder of CAA Michael Ovitz: 'I Just Knew I Was Going to Outwork Every One of Them'

> **Key principle (Michael Ovitz):** Ovitz built CAA on the idea that the underlying mechanics of any business, talent needing financing, distribution, and marketing, are identical whether the "talent" is a movie star, a musician, or a technical founder, and he applies the same lens decades later to the technology and biotech deals he now works on. His deeper operating belief is that conviction has to be proven through outworking every competitor, not argued for, and that refusing to be categorized as a specialist is what let him keep learning across entirely new fields for fifty years.

*David Senra in conversation with Michael Ovitz, founder of CAA. [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SHh03lzOJz0) · 67 min · published 2026-08-09.*

## Key lessons

- **The mechanics of any business are the same underneath the surface.** Ovitz said whether it is a filmmaker, musician, or engineer, the same sequence repeats: talent needs someone to help execute, financing has to be secured, the work needs distribution, and then it needs marketing and a culture built to last. He pointed to CAA still operating on the same business model and values fifty years after its 1974 founding as proof the underlying structure holds regardless of industry.
- **Refuse to be typecast as a specialist once you can be a generalist.** Ovitz said the entertainment business once forced people into narrow lanes (a career only in animation, or only in documentaries), but he now works across consumer products, defense, biopharma, and technology despite having no formal training in any of them, because he can learn enough vocabulary fast enough to follow brilliant people in each field. He said this was true of him "his whole life," independent of AI, though he considers AI a direct amplifier of that same instinct.
- **Prove doubters wrong through visible, sustained outwork rather than argument.** When Ovitz started CAA in 1974, a friend told him he'd be job-hunting within a year, and four partners at a rival literary agency told him he would never land movie stars. His response was structural: every time CAA signed a movie star, roughly once a week, he took out a full-page ad in Variety, tore it out, and mailed it to those four partners for the next ten years.
- **Competition is for people without a monopoly to build toward.** Ovitz cited Peter Thiel's "competition is for losers" framing approvingly, describing his own use of time as a direct expression of it: he keeps meetings to 20 minutes instead of a default hour, cuts out small talk entirely, saying he would rather hear about someone's kids over dinner than in a meeting, and treats every minute spent socially instead of on building the business as a cost.
- **Protect the vision the way a filmmaker protects a film, even when it is expensive to do.** Ovitz described letting Barry Levinson give Dustin Hoffman a full week to find his character on "Rain Man" against schedule pressure, and letting a filmmaker's creative authority override notes from producers or financiers who are not close enough to the work to earn a say. His generalization: dedication, curiosity, and protecting the vision are the common traits of anyone successful, regardless of the field they are in.
- **Build a formal mechanism for ideas to reach the top from anywhere in the company.** Every 90 days, Ovitz sat roughly 200 CAA staff, including mailroom employees, in a chair-touching circle with no visible way out, and gave everyone 30 seconds to pitch an idea they could not pass on. A mailroom employee's pitch about cable was good enough that Ovitz promoted him to agent on the spot.
- **Never ask someone to do something you would not do yourself.** Ovitz said he learned this from Magic Johnson's fast breaks, where Johnson had no ego about who actually scored the basket as long as the team won; at CAA, if a department head asked someone to complete a task and it was not done by 5:00, Ovitz or another senior partner would do it themselves rather than let it slide.
- **Look for people who hold their ground until you actually prove them wrong.** Ovitz described consulting for Steve Jobs and said Jobs's best trait was taking a completely intransigent position on an idea, sometimes one he did not even believe, purely to get a reaction back, and then genuinely changing his mind if the pushback was good. Ovitz said he built CAA's culture around the same principle: no idea is too dumb to say out loud, because a bad idea can trigger a good one.
- **Never dwell on a lost deal.** Quoting Barry Diller's line after losing a bid for a media asset to Sumner Redstone, "He won, we lost. Next case," Ovitz said he refuses to look backward on mistakes because dwelling changes nothing, and that founders often come to him specifically because he has made nearly every mistake there is to make and can flag it before they repeat it.
- **A short, focused burst of a new technology can define an entire work, even if the technology itself is used sparingly.** Ovitz recalled convincing Michael Crichton that Steven Spielberg was the only director who could realize "Jurassic Park" because Spielberg could integrate believable CGI dinosaurs with practical parts without the result looking fake, noting the actual CGI screen time was a small fraction of the film's runtime, yet it redefined the industry's before-and-after line.
