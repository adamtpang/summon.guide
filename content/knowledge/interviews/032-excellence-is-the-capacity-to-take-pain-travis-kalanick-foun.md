# Excellence Is the Capacity To Take Pain | Travis Kalanick, Founder of Uber

> **Key principle (Travis Kalanick):** Kalanick frames company building through what he calls the meta problem: the rate at which you solve problems must stay greater than or equal to the rate at which you create them, or you drown. He treats adversity as the raw material of excellence itself, comparing it to a marathoner at mile 21 who keeps pushing specifically because it hurts, and warns that a founder who gets too used to pain can lose the fierceness that made the pain productive in the first place.

*David Senra in conversation with Travis Kalanick, founder of Uber and founder of Adams. [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QVnU5lGlKE8) · 109 min · published 2026-08-16.*

## Key lessons

- **Treat expansion itself as a problem you must schedule capacity for.** Kalanick's "meta problem" holds that creating a problem (his example: deciding to enter China) does not show its true difficulty right away; it "comes ashore" roughly six months later in a form you must have predicted and staffed for. If your management capacity to solve problems falls behind your rate of creating them, you have to stop creating new ones until solving catches back up.
- **Entering a fundamentally different market means starting over, not porting your playbook.** Kalanick said Uber's China expansion required rebuilding basics most people assume travel, including the GPS mapping system itself, because almost nothing carried over. He named only two Western companies he considered to have actually succeeded there, Apple and Tesla, and said succeeding requires being "receptive" to doing things differently rather than assuming your existing system is exportable.
- **Efficiency edge, not price alone, wins a subsidy war.** Kalanick described network effects as compounding efficiency: a faster signup flow, shorter pickup times, and better driver routing all lower a competitor's true cost per ride, which forces a rival with a smaller network to subsidize harder just to keep pace. He said this is also why a smaller, well-funded challenger can gain share fast against an incumbent, until the incumbent's efficiency advantage outstrips whatever subsidy the challenger can still afford.
- **Distinguish competing hard from competing unfairly.** Kalanick said Uber never lobbied for a regulation to hurt a rival and never donated to a campaign to gain an edge, contrasting this with the taxi medallion system, which he described as a government-sanctioned cartel that made competition illegal outside of official permission. His framing: capitalism is a customer's right to choose and a founder's right to start a competing business; a taxi medallion regime, in his account, is neither.
- **Excellence means staying capable of feeling the pain, not getting used to it.** Kalanick agreed explicitly with the maxim "excellence is the capacity to take pain," comparing it to a marathoner who is visibly suffering at mile 21 specifically because that suffering is what produces the extra push. He warned that an entrepreneur who lives inside constant adversity long enough can grow numb to it, and said losing that fierceness toward "what is right and wrong" is a real cost of experience, not just a maturity gain.
- **Get attached to your fundraising process, never to a price.** Kalanick described running multiple simultaneous "rooms" of investors at different check sizes during a raise, building a live demand curve across price points rather than anchoring to a single number a founder gets emotionally attached to. He said fixating on a price instead of running the process is what causes term sheets to collapse or drag out, because the founder has no real alternative to negotiate against.
- **A high bar for a venture investor is simply "do no harm."** Kalanick compared an operating founder to a grandmaster playing chess 60 to 80 hours a week and a VC to an enthusiast who checks in once a quarter but still wants to leave a mark, arguing most investors cannot resist intervening even when they are not close enough to the daily reality to intervene well. He linked this directly to his own account of the 2017 conflict with Benchmark, which he said escalated because he never told them the company was already preparing to IPO.
- **Find "the line" between order and chaos rather than defaulting to either.** Kalanick described Uber's city-launch process, where a single mandatory pricing call (covering regulation, vehicle type, local wages, and geography) was the one hard rule a 23-year-old city launcher had to clear, with everything else left open. Too much structure slows people down and demoralizes them; too little produces the same result through chaos, so the leader's job is finding the minimum rule set that keeps speed without losing control.
- **Match the machine to the task instead of defaulting to a general-purpose humanoid.** At Adams, Kalanick's current company, he argued that humanoid robots make sense only for varied, low-scale tasks in human-built environments (folding laundry, taking out trash), while high-volume industrial tasks (a thousand pancakes an hour, moving freight pallets) need purpose-built specialized machines, not a row of humanoids doing a job an iron apparatus could do faster and cheaper.
- **An idea has to fit who you are before it becomes your next company.** Kalanick described picking his next venture the way he'd describe dating: an idea presents itself, and whether it is right depends on whether it matches your particular obsessions and skills, which for him led from Uber Eats logistics into real estate, mining, and food production once he traced the full physical supply chain of "grown, mined, or manufactured" goods.
