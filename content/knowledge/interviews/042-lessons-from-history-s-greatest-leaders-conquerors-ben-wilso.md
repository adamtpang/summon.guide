# Lessons from History’s Greatest Leaders & Conquerors | Ben Wilson

> **Key principle (Ben Wilson):** Wilson, creator of the "How to Take Over the World" podcast, has spent eight years looking for the through lines that connect conquerors, artists, religious figures and entrepreneurs. He names a short list: vision, the ability to communicate it, focus (which in practice means knowing which trade-offs to make), relentless determination and sincere belief. He frames his own work as helping listeners find the one historical figure who feels like them, so they feel the productive pain the Greeks called zeal. The conversation closes with Wilson describing his stage four cancer diagnosis, and why a study of history leaves him feeling that he has lived a fortunate life.

## Key lessons

- **Use zeal, the pain of an unrealized peer, as fuel.** Wilson said Aristotle defined zeal as the pain felt on seeing someone like you achieve admirable things you are capable of but have not yet done. His favorite example is a 21-year-old Thomas Edison staying up all night with a biography of Michael Faraday, then announcing he was going to hustle. He saw the same pattern in Julius Caesar looking at Alexander and in Rockefeller looking at Amos Lawrence.
- **Study the people who are like you, not just the greatest.** Wilson said he admires Leonardo da Vinci but feels no zeal reading him, because da Vinci's mind seems alien to his own. Walt Disney resonates more: a creative leader who built "empires of the mind" and was curious about things that never were, not things as they are. His podcast is designed to give listeners many lives so that one story lands as "that person is like me."
- **Keep a waste book to lower the stakes of thinking.** Wilson pointed to Isaac Newton calling his notebook a waste book. He keeps a cancer journal plus creative notebooks of poor drawings, episode plans and paragraph-long essays, and he deliberately writes terrible introductions ("Leonardo da Vinci was an artist") just to get into the episode, returning to polish later. Senra added Rick Rubin's account of Eminem filling notebooks he almost never uses.
- **Build the thing you wish existed.** In 2018, after several Tim Ferriss episodes that did not interest him, Wilson asked who he wished Ferriss would interview and landed on Napoleon. Realizing he could be "Tim Ferriss for dead people," he searched for an existing show, found none, and started one. Napoleon, from Andrew Roberts's biography, was episode one; Steve Jobs was episode two, and the format held for eight years.
- **The most important realizations are about what does not matter.** Wilson contrasted Jobs at NeXT, where a Carmel retreat produced five priorities and Jobs declared all of them essential, yielding a late, over-budget, over-engineered machine, with Jobs a decade later shipping an iPod with less storage and fewer formats than rivals because only the user experience mattered. He applied the same lens to Elon Musk taking the Roadster upmarket and to Senra cutting intro music and other trappings from "Founders."
- **Solve the hard problem everyone else is skipping.** Many teams were chasing flight, and nearly all focused on lift and propulsion. Wilbur Wright noticed birds making constant micro-adjustments and decided control was the real problem. The brothers spent years flying gliders on the North Carolina coast, tethered like kites, before adding an engine, at which point lift proved the easy part.
- **Focus means cutting work that pays.** Wilson said he took on side work early because he needed money to keep podcasting, including producing "My First Million" and making intro music for others. He eventually stopped producing other shows, stopped launching side businesses and shut down his agency so his own podcast could be as successful as possible.
- **Leaders share vision, communication and focus, plus obsession.** Wilson's three through lines are a clear goal (he quotes Genghis Khan on needing the vision of a goal to manage your own life), communicating it through whatever medium suits the person, and shutting out everything else. Senra added obsession; Wilson's litmus test is whether you forget to eat, noting Napoleon's 15-minute dinners, Jobs's extreme diets and Joan of Arc's fasting.
- **Clarity of purpose can outweigh every other disadvantage.** Wilson described Joan of Arc, a malnourished teenage girl, turning around a French war effort that dukes and field marshals with competing priorities had failed at for years, simply by insisting on one priority. Senra connected it to the idea of being easy to help: Daniel Ek told him he is easy to understand and therefore easy to help, and Arnold Schwarzenegger described strangers aiding him once he committed publicly to bodybuilding.
- **Determination beats intelligence, and relentlessness unnerves competitors.** Wilson argued neither Musk nor Jobs was the smartest person at his companies, but both were the most determined. He cited his ancestor Brigham Young, less brilliant than many followers but relentless, and a story about The Strokes' frontman pleading with The Killers, fronted by teetotal Brandon Flowers, to slow down after they released yet another album.
- **Sincere belief is irresistible; double-mindedness is unstable.** Wilson argued Napoleon treated destiny as his god and Jobs was a true believer in his own way, while the French king who converted with the line "Paris is worth a mass" was no great conqueror. He quoted Phil Knight in "Shoe Dog," a mediocre seller of encyclopedias and mutual funds who could not keep shoes in stock because he believed in them. Wilson said he reads Schwarzenegger's "Education of a Bodybuilder" every year because it captures obsession while it is happening.
- **Hold two futures at once, and keep perspective.** Wilson said he only saw a doctor after describing symptoms to Claude, which told him to go immediately; he had lost 40 pounds and was diagnosed with metastatic neuroendocrine carcinoma, with an initial prognosis of a year. He described living as if he might die within a year while also living as if he has twenty, and said history (teenage conscripts dying in Napoleon's last campaigns) reminds him he has had a charmed life: a wife and four children he loves, and a vocation he wakes up excited about. He also acknowledged bad moments, so that others with cancer do not feel they must be relentlessly positive.
- **Use your platform for people without one.** Senra replaced the episode's ads with partner donations to Wilson's family fundraiser. Wilson closed by crediting Jon Huntsman's decision to fund a world-class cancer institute in Utah for his care, and asked listeners to also support others with fewer resources and audiences, including through cancer research.
