# Building a Power Company for the Next 50 Years | Zach Dell, Base Power

> **Key principle (Zach Dell):** Base Power's core move, as Dell describes it, is refusing to sell the product at all: instead of selling a home battery, Base owns it, uses it as a wholesale power-market asset while the grid is up, and passes the savings back as a lower bill, a model competitors cannot copy without abandoning their own high-margin sales structure. Dell treats disciplined vertical integration, constant focus on naming the single current constraint, and a strategy every employee can repeat as the actual tools for compounding a cost advantage inside a regulated, historically low-innovation industry. He frames the whole effort as open-ended, a mission meant to outlast the company rather than to be sold.

*David Senra in conversation with Zach Dell, co-founder of Base Power. [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QFk6g5PQtqs) · 76 min · published 2026-09-06.*

## Key lessons

- **Counter-position instead of competing on price.** Dell said Base does not sell batteries, it sells electricity: install the hardware, keep ownership, and use it as a wholesale power-market asset roughly 99 percent of the time the grid is up, passing the savings back as a lower bill. He said this only works as a business model because an incumbent that tried to copy it would have to abandon a high-margin, one-time-sale model that Wall Street already prices and rewards, which public companies rarely do.
- **Name the industry's real incentive problem before building the fix.** Dell said regulated utilities earn a fixed, roughly 9 percent unlevered return on whatever capital they get approved to build (the rate base), not on innovation, so they have almost no financial reason to develop new technology themselves. Base positions itself as the utilities' outsourced research and development engine, building battery fleets the utilities then pay for under ten-year megawatts-as-a-service contracts, so utilities get better technology without taking on the technology risk.
- **Vertically integrate only as far as today's scale justifies, then go deeper.** Dell said Base moved from installing off-the-shelf batteries bought from an outside supplier to designing and building its own battery, the Base Core, in-house at the level of final assembly, test, and pack, because that supplier could no longer keep pace with demand (Base was already sold out through November while recording this in August). He said the same logic will eventually justify manufacturing battery cells or even refining and mining lithium, but only once greater scale makes that depth of integration pay for itself.
- **Put the people who design the product next to the people who build it.** Dell described a campus where engineering, design, and manufacturing sit close enough to reach by golf cart, so an engineer can walk a failing part onto the factory floor, diagnose it, and fix it the same day instead of managing the problem through reports. He attributed this layout, along with open floor plans and the absence of private offices, directly to practices his early hires brought from SpaceX and Tesla.
- **Give every constraint one physical owner and a visible end date.** Base's culture centers on a literal turtle that sits on the desk of whoever owns the company's current critical-path problem, paired with a response squad the team nicknames a hot potato, which meets daily and reports to the whole company weekly until the constraint is resolved and the turtle moves on. Dell traced this discipline to a board member who has worked with Elon Musk for decades and whose only recurring question in every conversation has been "what's the constraint."
- **Make the strategy something every new hire can repeat, not just the founders.** Dell gives a quarterly presentation called Engine of Success to the entire company, from interns to the most senior hires, walking through exactly how Base intends to win against competitors. He backs it with three yearly North Stars, becoming the largest and fastest-growing battery fleet, landing batteries at the lowest cost per kilowatt-hour, and reaching financial sustainability, posted on a factory-floor poster and repeated in the written company update he has sent every month for three and a half years without missing one.
- **Write by hand to force the thinking, not just to record it.** Dell keeps two notebooks, a red one for tactical, in-the-business problems that he carries around the office, and a black one reserved for 30 to 60 minutes each evening, phone and computer left in another room, for strategic, on-the-business thinking. He said moving that process onto a computer made his good ideas disappear, and that he is simply the kind of person who has to "write clearly to think clearly."
- **Reserve one recurring conversation for constraints only, never for good news.** Dell said his weekly one-on-one with co-founder Justin never discusses what is going well, only what threatens the mission right now, on the reasoning that good outcomes do not need airtime because they already take care of themselves. He described the discomfort of a conversation that only ever surfaces problems as something that fades with repetition once a team commits to it.
- **Refuse the start-scale-sell script if the mission is the actual goal.** Dell rejected what he called the standard venture-backed playbook of building a company only to exit it, framing Base's mission (human prosperity through energy abundance) as open-ended enough to outlast the company and possibly outlast him personally. He said the founder's real first strategic choice is deciding whether you are solving for a paycheck or for a mission, since the two produce different vertical-integration timelines and a different sense of when the company is ever finished.
- **Expand along the value chain only when the core position is defensible.** Dell broke the energy business into four functions, make, move, store, and sell (generation, transmission and distribution, storage, and retail), and said Base entered through store and sell because that offered the best return on capital at the company's scale when it started. He said the same store-and-sell infrastructure is now being pointed at AI data-center demand, not because the mission changed, but because a new, faster-growing customer for the same product appeared.
