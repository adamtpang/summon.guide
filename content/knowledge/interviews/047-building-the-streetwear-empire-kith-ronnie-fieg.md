# Building the Streetwear Empire Kith | Ronnie Fieg

> **Key principle (Ronnie Fieg):** Fieg says his favorite entrepreneurs cannot work backwards from money, and Kith is built the same way: every product starts as something he wants to wear himself, and the business only does the 100 percent of things he loves rather than the 10 to 15 percent that a normal retailer would. He refuses wholesale and licensing so he controls how every product is shown and sold, keeps physical stores growing in step with the website, and expands into food, hospitality and sport only when it serves the community that already shows up. In his words, Kith is for the people who know the difference between good product and great product.

## Key lessons

- **Ask for the job, not the envelope.** At his bar mitzvah, a cousin who owned the David Z shoe stores offered 13-year-old Fieg an envelope of cash, knowing the gifts were paying for the party. Fieg asked for a job instead and started in a stockroom the next day. He then worked up through every role, from stock boy and salesperson to manager, assistant buyer and finally buyer and general manager, which is why he says he knows every layer of the business today.
- **Learn taste where people are forced to have opinions.** Fieg credits public school in Jamaica, Queens, the long F train ride into Manhattan, and the 8th Street store floor, where he watched customers with no prior information choose among hundreds of boots. Back then, he said, you had to form your own edit because you had not seen everything online first. He thinks the algorithm era has people outsourcing their taste.
- **Make the product for yourself, and fit it on yourself.** Kith puts out more than 5,000 styles a year, and until recently Fieg was the fit model for four hours a week. With about 20 people in the room (materials, merchandising, design), he decides on fabric, alterations and next iterations live. He criticized brands that put a new fabric on an old pattern without refitting it, which is why the same style suddenly fits badly.
- **Bring back what people cannot get anywhere else.** Fieg saw David Z sell roughly 10,000 pairs of a gray Timberland construction boot, a color Timberland had never made, with no internet. In 2006, at 23, ASICS opened its old catalogs to him and he chose the Gel-Lyte III, the shoe his mother bought him at eight instead of the Reebok Pumps he wanted. He committed to the 1,000-pair minimum across three colorways, and when it released in 2007, a customer who turned out to be a Wall Street Journal editor put a photo of it at the top of the paper's Pursuits section.
- **Be ready when the line forms.** The day that article ran, the line outside the store caught the eye of Adidas's North America chief, who had just moved in across the street. He walked Fieg to the Adidas store at lunch and asked what he would want to work on. Fieg picked the Superstar shell-toe and turned it into a dress shoe for the staff of a downtown lounge, the Black Tie project, his second collaboration.
- **Do only the part you love.** At David Z, Fieg realized his collector customers came back only for his special releases, which made up a small slice of the store. Senra cited Steve Jobs telling Nike to get rid of the crap and focus on the good stuff. Fieg said the crap was still making money, but he wanted to be passionate about everything he sold, which led to opening Kith in 2011 as a small footwear shop with the best wall of top-tier releases he could assemble.
- **Control the experience: no wholesale, no licensing.** Fieg said that once your product sits in a messy airport shop, customers subconsciously attach that experience to your brand. He calls Kith half storytelling and half retail, and he caps the web's share of revenue so physical stores grow alongside it. He opens stores in cities he already travels to, from Tokyo and Paris to Seoul, and treats online demand as a signal of where people need a physical Kith.
- **Serve the customers you have instead of buying new ones.** Kith Treats began as an attempt to vent the smell of a Club Med white chocolate bread onto the street. When that failed, Fieg paid a deli worker $20 to blend cereal into ice cream, saw everyone smile, and built a cereal ice cream bar giving fans a way into the brand for under $10. Senra compared it to Sam Walton's ice cream cart; Fieg said the difference is that he already had the people.
- **Value means giving more than people pay for.** Fieg said luxury should mean how much people love owning something, not a price threshold, and that many luxury houses are overexposed. He prices from last season, trying to deliver more at the same price, and spends $1,300 to $1,400 a square foot on stores he says the product does not strictly need. He and his partner Sam own the company privately with no outside investors.
- **Protect your name before you need it.** When Kith opened, Fieg skipped registering the mark for apparel because he was not making apparel yet. Four years later someone registered it in Italy for apparel just as Kith's clothing took off, and he had to buy it back. He called it a very expensive lesson.
- **Spot a culture before it has an identity.** Using a Miami padel club as his office one season, Fieg noticed players wearing vintage tees and basketball shorts. That became Kith Ivy, a 16,000-square-foot Tribeca club with three courts, a spa, a gym and an Erewhon juice bar, plus padel apparel sold to non-members online. He said he builds every space for himself first, as with his footwear archive of about 800 released and 1,000 unreleased pairs.
