# Principles for Dealing with the Changing World Order by Ray Dalio

> **Key principle — Ray Dalio:** He traces the whole framework back to a personal shock: watching the US default on its gold promise in 1971 and watching the stock market rise 25% instead of crash, because "the same thing happened many, many times before" once he checked the historical record. That single surprise became his method: "to understand what is coming at you, you need to understand what happened before you." He studied 500 years across the Dutch, British, US, and Chinese empires and found the same cause-and-effect sequence every time, which he calls the Big Cycle: rise, top, decline.

## Key lessons

- **The 1971 story is the origin of the whole framework, and it's presented as a personal failure of anticipation, not abstract history.** "In 1971, when I was a young clerk on the floor of the New York Stock Exchange, the United States ran out of money and defaulted on its debts... I expected the stock market to plunge the next day... the market was up, way up, and went on to rise nearly 25 percent." Digging into history, he found "the exact same thing happened in 1933" under Roosevelt, for the identical reason: spending more paper money than there was gold to back it.
- **The resulting trading principle, stated explicitly:** "When central banks print a lot of money to relieve a crisis, buy stocks, gold and commodities because their value will rise and the value of paper money will fall." He names 2008 and 2020 as the same pattern recurring, and says "it almost certainly will happen in the future."
- **An "order" is defined precisely, not used loosely.** "It's a governing system for people dealing with each other" — internal orders (constitutions) govern within countries, world orders (treaties) govern between them, and both "typically change after wars."
- **Empire power is measured on eight specific metrics, averaged together**, not vibes: education, inventiveness and technology development, competitiveness in global markets, economic output, share of world trade, military strength, the power of financial centers for capital markets, and strength of the currency as a reserve currency.
- **The rise phase runs on a specific causal chain**, in his stated order: strong education (not just skills — "character, civility and work ethic") leads to innovation, which leads to competitiveness and rising trade share, which requires military strength to protect trade routes, which (combined with strong capital markets and government-business-military coordination) produces a reserve currency — and "having a reserve currency enables the empire to borrow more than other countries," which he calls "the exorbitant privilege."
- **The top phase is described as carrying the seeds of decline inside its own success.** Rising costs make the leading power less competitive as others copy its methods ("British ship builders had less expensive workers than Dutch ship builders, so they hired Dutch designers to design better ships"); people who inherited wealth become "less battle hardened, steeped in luxuries"; borrowing against reserve-currency status quietly weakens the currency even as spending power looks strong: "when borrowing and spending are strong, the empire appears very strong, but its finances are in fact being weakened."
- **Decline follows a specific, named sequence, not a vague collapse:** debt becomes unpayable, the country is forced to choose between default and printing money ("it always chooses to print a lot of new money"), which devalues the currency, which widens wealth and values gaps, which produces populism of the left (redistribute) and right (defend existing wealth), which produces internal conflict, which invites external challenge from a rising rival — "typically, the decline comes gradually and then very suddenly."
- **He explicitly declines to predict a US collapse, and states where the US actually is in the cycle at time of recording.** "The United States hasn't yet reached this point... the big sell off in dollars and dollar debt hasn't yet begun... they've not yet crossed the line to become wars" — the value of the model is diagnostic (where are we, what stage), not fatalistic.
- **The prescription he lands on is deliberately reductive**, after 40+ minutes of mechanism: "it comes down to just two things: earn more than we spend, and treat each other well. All other things I mentioned... are just ways of getting at these two things."
