# How to have an idea meritocracy in any organization

> **Key principle — Ray Dalio:** Despite the title, this specific video is about the precondition for an idea meritocracy, not the mechanics of running one: you have to split yourself into "the designer/manager of your machine" and "the worker within your machine," and let the designer/manager evaluate the worker with total objectivity. YouTube's own description points to this being drawn from the "Make Your Idea Meritocracy Work in a Way That Suits You" section of *Principles* (pg 320-321) — self-objectivity here is presented as the load-bearing skill that a working idea meritocracy depends on.

*Ray Dalio, Principles. [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=S7hNda9DVxo) · 7 min · 77K views.*

## Key lessons

- **Two roles, one person, and most people never leave the second one.** "Most people remain stuck in the perspective of being a worker within their machine," reacting emotionally moment to moment, instead of also stepping back to design and manage the whole system their life runs on.
- **The designer/manager's job is to see the worker honestly, without inflating or protecting them.** "Not believing in him more than he deserves or putting him in jobs that he shouldn't be in" — the same clear-eyed evaluation you'd apply to hiring anyone else, applied to yourself.
- **The Einstein-on-a-basketball-team example makes the reframe concrete.** "Would you want to have Einstein on your basketball team when he fails to dribble and shoot well? Would you think badly of him?" The move is to fire yourself from roles you're bad at while staying in the designer/manager role, not to feel obligated to be competent at everything.
- **He explicitly reframes discovering a weakness as good news, not failure.** "You shouldn't feel upset at yourself if you find out that you're bad at something. You should be happy you found out because knowing that and dealing with it will improve your chances of getting what you want."
- **Self-objectivity requires outside input, even for him.** "It is difficult to see oneself objectively, you need to rely [on] the input of others... I still know I can't see myself objectively, which is why I continue to rely as much on others' input" — framed as a permanent condition, not something he eventually solved alone.
- **The episode closes with five compressed decision rules:** don't confuse what you wish were true with what's really true; don't worry about looking good, worry about achieving your goals; don't overweight first-order consequences relative to second- and third-order ones; don't let pain stand in the way of your progress; don't blame bad outcomes on anyone but yourself.
