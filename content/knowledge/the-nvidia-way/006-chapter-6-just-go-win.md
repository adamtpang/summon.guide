# Chapter 6: Just Go Win

> **Key principle:** "Three Teams, Two Seasons" let Nvidia outpace 3dfx, whose perfectionism and a distracting acquisition of board-maker STB Systems slowed it to the point of bankruptcy; Nvidia bought its patents and hired its engineers, who were shocked to learn there was "no secret sauce," just relentless execution. The same period saw Nvidia go public, win the Xbox contract from Microsoft after a rival had already been publicly announced as the winner, and lose cofounder Curtis Priem, whose insistence on individual ownership of chip architecture clashed with Jensen's demand that all credit belong to "we."

*Synthesized from Chapter 6 of The Nvidia Way by Tae Kim.*

## Key lessons

- 3dfx, once confident enough in Nvidia's imminent bankruptcy to skip acquiring it, instead chased "feature creep" perfectionism and bought board-maker STB Systems for $141 million, which backfired when other board partners fled to Nvidia and STB's distribution problems consumed 3dfx's management attention; 3dfx filed for bankruptcy in 2002, and former engineer reactions captured the lesson: "we thought there was going to be a secret sauce... it's just really hard work and intense execution on schedules."
- Jensen personally recruited SGI's star engineer John Montrym after three failed pitches by Priem, Malachowsky, and Kirk, finally winning him over not with words but by building a demo in weeks that mimicked SGI's flagship technology, proving Nvidia's execution speed outmatched what Montrym's own six-person-year SGI project could do.
- Nvidia's TSMC relationship, initiated by a personal letter to CEO Morris Chang after cold calls failed, became central to production; Chang described Jensen's own philosophy, "rough justice," a partnership expected to net out roughly 50-50 over years rather than every single deal.
- Nvidia went public on January 22, 1999, raising $42 million and closing up 64% at $19.69/share; executives made joking pledges (tattoos, a nipple piercing, a mohawk) for if the stock ever hit $100, and had to follow through within the year when Xbox news sent the stock soaring past that mark.
- Microsoft had already publicly committed the original Xbox graphics contract to start-up Gigapixel, with a Bill Gates GDC announcement drafted, when Nvidia's persistent pitching (led by Jensen and Chris Diskin) reopened the deal one week before the announcement; Nvidia won the $200 million contract instead, leaving Gigapixel's founder to say, "Today, I would be running a trillion-dollar company, not Jensen, I presume."
- Cofounder Curtis Priem increasingly clashed with Jensen's collectivist culture, insisting on individual ownership of "his" chip architecture even after unilaterally deleting outdated documentation the software team was still using; Jensen overruled him and insisted on plural ownership, "No, it's our architecture. You didn't do it. We did it." Priem was moved off engineering into IP work, took a leave of absence in 2003, and ultimately resigned, saying, "I was tired, beaten up, and demoralized... I always wish I could have stayed."
- On a 2000 investor road show plagued by skeptical questions ("Why would we invest in a graphics company?"), Jensen compared the pessimism to the "Bring out your dead" scene from Monty Python and the Holy Grail; Morgan Stanley raised $387 million for Nvidia and gifted Michael Hara a custom illustration casting Jensen as King Arthur defeating a black knight.
