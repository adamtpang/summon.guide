# Chapter 2: The Graphics Revolution

> **Key principle:** Priem and Malachowsky, both engineers with unconventional paths into graphics, teamed up at Sun Microsystems in the late 1980s to build the GX graphics accelerator, in defiance of a direct order from their boss's boss not to touch graphics at all. GX's success made both men's careers, but Sun's shift toward political, risk-averse decision-making in the early 1990s convinced them their future lay outside the company, with a young LSI Logic account manager named Jensen Huang already embedded in their plans.

*Synthesized from Chapter 2 of The Nvidia Way by Tae Kim.*

## Key lessons

- Curtis Priem taught himself programming on a Teletype terminal in high school outside Cleveland, won a science fair with a text-based billiards simulation, and chose RPI over MIT because RPI let professors, not TAs, teach freshmen and had just acquired a new mainframe.
- After designing the IBM Professional Graphics Controller (PGC) at Vermont Microsystems at age twenty-three, a genuine advance in PC graphics, Priem left for Silicon Valley when the company refused to offer equity, eventually landing at Sun Microsystems under Wayne Rosing, a former Apple engineer who coveted Priem's PGC design.
- Rosing wanted fast dedicated graphics chips for Sun's new SPARCstation line, directly against his boss Bernie Lacroute's order to leave graphics alone; he told Priem, "do anything you want," as long as the design fit inside the existing frame-buffer footprint, effectively unlimited creative license.
- Chris Malachowsky, raised for a medical career by his parents, decided against becoming a doctor while lying on a picnic table during his MCAT lunch break, telling his relieved parents, "I don't want to be a doctor anymore," and moved into engineering and manufacturing at Hewlett-Packard before a practice job interview at Sun turned into his life's work.
- Priem and Malachowsky became the "closet graphics" team, working in secret on what their boss's boss had forbidden; their design used two dedicated chips (the FBC and TEC) to offload rendering from the CPU, a partnership marked by loud creative arguments Jensen later called "honing the sword."
- LSI Logic, assigned to fabricate the resulting GX chip, put a young account manager named Jensen Huang on the Sun relationship; the three men worked out manufacturing details together, planting the seed of their eventual partnership years before Nvidia existed.
- GX became a commercial hit (Priem's side project, the flight-sim game Aviator, became Sun's signature sales demo), but by the early 1990s Sun had turned bureaucratic and political, killing Priem's next-generation graphics proposal in favor of a rival's idea with better internal support; both men decided their time at Sun was over and reached out to their old LSI contact, Jensen, for help structuring a possible consulting deal, a conversation that would turn into something much bigger.
