# Conclusion: The Nvidia Way

> **Key principle:** After thirty-one years, Nvidia is described as an extension of Jensen Huang's own mind and habits: relentless recruiting of top talent, equity-driven retention paired with genuine personal care for employees, and a demanding culture of transparency, extreme work hours, and directness, all resting on Jensen's belief that resilience and "sheer will," not raw genius, built the company.

*Synthesized from the Conclusion of The Nvidia Way by Tae Kim.*

## Key lessons

- Jensen still refuses a private office, running meetings from a conference room called Metropolis and smaller ones from "Mind Meld"; former colleagues note that Nvidia employees, unusually, never contradict each other in front of outsiders because Jensen communicates strategy so relentlessly and consistently.
- Hiring talent in bulk and fast is treated as core strategy: when Creative Labs shut down its Huntsville, Alabama office, Nvidia beat Intel for the engineers by opening a permanent Huntsville office with no relocation required; in Pune, India, Dwight Diercks pivoted an aborted acquisition into a same-day hiring blitz, printing 50 offer packages overnight and landing 51 of 54 employees, seeding what grew into a 1,400-plus person office.
- David Kirk became known for direct poaching calls to rival architects, explicitly pitching Nvidia's scale ("we have twenty architects... you guys have one or two"); marquee hires like SGI's John Montrym and Rendition's Walt Donovan were chosen for complementary skills, not just prestige, building what Kirk called "the all-star team of architects."
- Retention runs on structured RSU grants (quarter-vesting over four years, "refresher" grants, and special "Top Contributor" awards personally reviewed by Jensen) alongside personal gestures, such as keeping an employee on payroll during a family health crisis or covering costs after an employee's house burned down; Nvidia's fiscal 2024 turnover was under 3 percent versus a 13 percent industry average.
- The culture explicitly rejects internal politics in favor of "no one loses alone," where employees are expected to flag when they're falling behind so others (including Jensen) can help, a contrast former Sun Microsystems employees describe as being expected to sink or swim alone elsewhere.
- Extreme commitment is baked in: 60-hour work weeks are treated as a floor, stretching to 80-plus during major chip-development crunches or strategic pivots; transparency includes direct, unscheduled lines to Jensen, who is known for detailed personal recall of employees and for the "LUA" (Listen, Understand, Answer) rebuke when someone rambles instead of answering directly.
- The book credits Jensen personally, not luck, for Nvidia's survival at several near-death junctures (fundraising to save the company, licensing the VGA core to ship the RIVA 128 on time, holding the CUDA course against Wall Street pressure), while acknowledging genuine serendipity too, quoting Curtis Priem: "Nvidia would have been a failure if NV1 had not failed."
- The Conclusion ends on Jensen's own framing of the company's success as resilience rather than intelligence, closing on the line he says "without any trace of hesitation": "I love Nvidia."
