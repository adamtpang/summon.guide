# Chapter 3: The Birth of Nvidia

> **Key principle:** Over months of "bottomless cup of coffee" meetings at a Denny's in East San Jose, Curtis Priem, Chris Malachowsky, and Jensen Huang planned a graphics-chip start-up for the emerging PC gaming market. Jensen only agreed to join once he believed the company could hit $50 million in revenue. They founded Nvidia (from "invidia," Latin for envy) on April 5, 1993, for $200 apiece, and secured $2 million in Series A funding despite a chaotic pitch to Sequoia's famously blunt Don Valentine.

*Synthesized from Chapter 3 of The Nvidia Way by Tae Kim.*

## Key lessons

- Two 1992 developments, the PCI bus and Microsoft's graphically showy Windows 3.1, convinced Priem and Malachowsky that the PC, not the workstation, was the real opportunity; they began meeting Jensen for hours at a Denny's at Capitol and Berryessa, ordering one bottomless coffee and working for four hours at a stretch.
- Jensen needed convincing: he pressed Priem and Malachowsky on market size until he was satisfied the business could reach $50 million in sales, and the three men's wives insisted on a synchronized resignation so no one household bore the risk alone.
- Priem forced the issue by resigning from Sun on December 31, 1992, and literally founding the company "by declaring that this was started" the next day, with no name, no funding, and no other employees yet committed.
- The company's name went through failed options (Primal Graphics, Huaprimal, iNVision) before Priem found "Invidia," Latin for envy, related to their belief that rivals envied their GX work at Sun; they dropped the "I" for NVidia, honoring their planned first chip, the NV1.
- Lawyer James Gaither asked Jensen how much money he had on him ("$200"), took it as payment for a large equity stake, and each cofounder then "bought" his own shares for $200; Nvidia was incorporated on April 5, 1993, and Priem drove straight to the DMV for a vanity plate reading NVIDIA.
- Wayne Rosing, Priem's former Sun boss, tried twice to keep the trio close to Sun, first courting Priem alone for what became Java's acceleration chip, then offering to license Sun's whole GX patent portfolio to the new company; the three declined both offers, choosing full independence over a safer partnership.
- Their Sequoia Capital pitch to Don Valentine was a near-disaster: Valentine, who despised product demos, grilled them with rapid questions ("What are you?"), and Priem's engineer-brained answers ("We're all of them"; "We're going to own I/O architecture") baffled the room. Sequoia invested anyway, on the strength of Jensen's reputation via an LSI Logic referral, giving Nvidia $2 million alongside Sutter Hill Ventures. Jensen took the lesson to heart: "Your reputation will precede you even if your business plan writing skills are inadequate."
