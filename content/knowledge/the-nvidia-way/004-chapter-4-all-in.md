# Chapter 4: All In

> **Key principle:** The NV1 tried to do everything at once: proprietary quadrilateral texture mapping instead of the industry-standard triangle approach, and integrated audio to compete with SoundBlaster. It flopped commercially because it couldn't run DOOM properly, and nearly bankrupted Nvidia. Reading Positioning by Al Ries and Jack Trout taught Jensen that Nvidia's real failure was message, not technology; the company rebuilt itself around a single, unmissable goal for its next chip, the RIVA 128, and shipped it in nine months using an emulator instead of physical prototypes, saving the company just in time.

*Synthesized from Chapter 4 of The Nvidia Way by Tae Kim.*

## Key lessons

- Priem designed the NV1 around forward (quadrilateral) texture mapping instead of standard inverse (triangle) texturing to save on then-expensive memory, and bundled in proprietary high-quality audio to beat SoundBlaster, both bets requiring developers to rewrite their games specifically for Nvidia's chip.
- Memory prices collapsed from $50/MB to $5/MB between the NV1's design and launch, erasing its main advantage, and it could not properly run DOOM, the most popular game in the world, because of its non-standard texturing and audio incompatibility with the SoundBlaster standard PC makers required.
- Malachowsky's postmortem: "We thought we had built great technology and a great product. It turns out we only built great technology. It wasn't a great product." Diamond Multimedia returned nearly all 250,000 ordered chips; Nvidia had spent $15 million developing the NV1 and now faced a cash crisis, cutting staff from over 100 to 40.
- A board member's simple question at an early meeting, "How would you position this?", stumped Jensen and sent him to Positioning by Al Ries and Jack Trout, which argues that customers don't want to be persuaded with facts, they want a simple, emotionally resonant message; the NV1's complexity had none.
- Sega's NV2 contract, meant to follow the NV1, was quietly abandoned by Sega, but Jensen had built in a $1 million contract clause for a working prototype; engineer Wayne Kogachi delivered one after a year of isolated work, and the payout became a critical lifeline during the crisis.
- With $3 million left in the bank (nine months of runway), Jensen forced Nvidia to abandon its NV1-era instincts, told his team to "quit polishing the turd," and committed the company to a market-standard approach for the next chip, code-named NV3 and branded RIVA 128 (Real-time Interactive Video and Animation Accelerator, "128" for its 128-bit memory bus), the largest chip attempted in the industry at the time.
- To beat the standard two-year chip cycle, Jensen bought a $1 million Ikos chip emulator, allowing software drivers to be built and debugged before physical silicon existed, reversing the normal sequence; testers like Henry Levin endured 15-minute mouse-click delays on the emulator to find bugs before tape-out.
- At the 1997 Computer Game Developers Conference, the RIVA 128 stunned rival 3dfx's own engineers with its benchmark results; within four months Nvidia shipped over a million units, captured a fifth of the PC graphics market, and posted its first profitable quarter. Jensen handed every employee a single dollar bill from a thick envelope as a symbol of the company's survival, giving a double bonus to the operations worker who packed and shipped the chips.
