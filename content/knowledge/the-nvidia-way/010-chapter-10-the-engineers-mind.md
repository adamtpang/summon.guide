# Chapter 10: The Engineer's Mind

> **Key principle:** Drawing on Carl Icahn's theory that corporate succession is "anti-Darwinian," favoring likeable non-technical executives over more capable ones, Kim argues Jensen's rare combination of deep technical mastery and business command let Nvidia repeatedly out-navigate technically shallow rivals like Microsoft under Ballmer, Apple under Sculley, and Intel under Swan.

*Synthesized from Chapter 10 of The Nvidia Way by Tae Kim.*

## Key lessons

- Kim opens with his own memory of AOL Time Warner CEO Gerald Levin fumbling a basic technical question, then invokes activist investor Carl Icahn's "anti-Darwinian" theory of CEO succession: boards tend to promote affable, unthreatening executives over more capable but less agreeable ones, producing a "survival of the un-fittest" cycle.
- Steve Ballmer (marketing background, Microsoft CEO for 14 years) is cited as missing mobile computing and making costly acquisitions like aQuantive and Nokia, with Microsoft's stock falling over 30% under his tenure; Steve Jobs reportedly cheered on hearing Ballmer remained in charge, since he thought little of Ballmer's technical judgment.
- John Sculley, a former PepsiCo marketing executive who ousted Jobs from Apple in 1985, is credited with selling incrementally pricier computers but blamed for technical missteps (the Newton, PowerPC chip choice) that nearly bankrupted Apple before it acquired Jobs's NeXT.
- Intel's Bob Swan, a CFO-turned-CEO with no engineering background, is portrayed as prioritizing stock buybacks over R&D, mismanaging AI strategy by shutting down the promising Nervana Systems acquisition in favor of the later Habana Labs deal; Nvidia's Jonah Alben likened Intel's AI approach to "throwing darts."
- By contrast, sources describe Jensen as exhaustively technical: reviewing Wi-Fi standards in depth for a product he ultimately didn't ship, attending the hardcore NeurIPS AI conference in person just "to learn," and following internal deep-learning email lists closely enough that colleagues could track his interests in real time.
- Jensen practices deliberately imprecise "CEO math," rounding numbers to move faster on big-picture strategic calls, a habit he traces to an Oregon State professor, Donald Amort, who taught him that "false accuracy is pointless"; his terse, haiku-like emails are described as intentionally minimal to respect colleagues' limited attention.
- Jensen's work ethic is depicted as extreme and self-imposed: he reportedly writes emails throughout vacations, refused his team's intervention to "go play with your kids," and told 60 Minutes in 2024 that Nvidia's demanding culture "should be like that. If you want to do extraordinary things, it shouldn't be easy."
- The chapter closes on Intel's cautionary history (declining to supply the original iPhone chip, selling its ARM-based XScale unit, falling behind TSMC on EUV lithography) as the "WinTel lock-in" playbook Nvidia is positioned to repeat with CUDA in the AI era, with Jensen's insistence on continued R&D investment framed as the opposite of Intel's late-stage financial-engineering focus.
