# Chapter 5: Ultra-Aggressive

> **Key principle:** Following the RIVA 128's success, Jensen formalized the intensity that had saved the company into permanent doctrine: employees should measure performance against the theoretical physical maximum ("Speed of Light"), not against competitors or Nvidia's own past. He restructured engineering into three parallel teams releasing on a six-month cadence ("Three Teams, Two Seasons") so Nvidia would always have a newer chip than any rival, and survived a second production near-death (defective TSMC chips) by testing every unit with an army of contract workers nicknamed "bluecoats."

*Synthesized from Chapter 5 of The Nvidia Way by Tae Kim.*

## Key lessons

- New chip designer Caroline Landry, Nvidia's first female engineer, adjusted to a culture of working past 11 p.m. on weeknights and most weekends; when employees complained about hours, Jensen replied, "People who train for the Olympics grumble about training early in the morning, too."
- Marketing director Michael Hara's standard new-hire orientation speech set the tone bluntly: "If you came here thinking you can just hide in the back, collect your paycheck, and go home at five, you're mistaken... you should resign today." Nvidia hired fast and fired fast rather than let a bad fit fester.
- "Speed of Light" meant breaking every project into tasks with a theoretical minimum completion time assuming zero delays, and judging employees against that ceiling, not against competitors or Nvidia's own past performance; Jensen would reprimand anyone who benchmarked against what had already been done.
- To hit an impossibly compressed nine-month RIVA 128 schedule, Jensen reversed the normal chip-development order, building software drivers before the physical chip existed via a $1 million Ikos emulator, a decision that became permanent Nvidia practice: drivers ready and tested before any new chip ships.
- Jensen's monthly refrain, "We're thirty days from going out of business," was deliberate psychological pressure, meant to prevent complacency even during good quarters; Dwight Diercks recalled Jensen enumerating exactly which three bad events could zero out the bank account at any time.
- A second existential crisis hit when new manufacturer TSMC produced defective RIVA 128ZX chips (titanium stringer contamination); rather than scrap the batch, Malachowsky proposed testing every single chip by hand, and Jensen hired hundreds of contract laborers nicknamed "bluecoats" who eventually outnumbered engineers on the testing floor, saving the batch and prompting Jensen's companywide email urging generosity toward them: "Give a bluecoat your pork chop."
- Jensen diagnosed the industry's real problem, that no graphics-chip company had ever held a lead more than two years, and solved it by splitting engineering into three parallel teams releasing derivative chips every six months ("Three Teams, Two Seasons"), enabled by Priem's earlier "virtualized objects" resource-manager architecture, which let the team emulate unfinished hardware features in software rather than delaying a launch: "the competition will always be shooting behind the duck."
