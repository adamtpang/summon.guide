# Postscript II: Why I Do All This Walking, or How Systems Become Fragile

> **Key principle:** Fitness researchers Art De Vany and Doug McGuff showed Taleb that the barbell logic of the book (extreme safety plus extreme, small-scale risk-taking, nothing in the middle) applies to the human body itself: organisms evolved for feast-and-famine, sprint-and-idle variability, and artificially smoothing out small stressors, whether in diet, exercise, forests, or economies, removes the practice a system needs to survive a genuinely large one.

*Synthesized from Part II of the Postscript Essay of The Black Swan by Nassim Nicholas Taleb.*

## Key lessons

- Taleb credits fitness practitioners Art De Vany (who also studied Hollywood's Extremistan box-office statistics, discussed earlier in the book) and physician Doug McGuff with showing him that he had missed something central despite a career studying randomness: living organisms, like economies, need Extremistan-style variability and extreme stressors to stay robust, and steady, moderate exercise and nutrition are themselves a Platonic illusion of health.
- He draws on predator-prey (Lotka-Volterra) population dynamics to argue humans evolved for cycles of extreme hunger and extreme abundance rather than three regular meals a day, and notes that religious traditions (Judaism, Islam, Orthodox Christianity) built in fast days long before modern fitness science caught up.
- Ancestral stressors were themselves fractal in size, mostly light everyday loads punctuated by rare, very heavy ones; nobody in the Pleistocene jogged on a fixed schedule, and Taleb calls marathon running (steady effort over a fixed long distance) a "modern abomination" compared to the ancestral pattern of sprinting when chasing or fleeing, then walking aimlessly the rest of the time.
- He describes his own adopted "barbell" regimen under De Vany's guidance: very long, slow, meditative walks combined with rare, random bursts of intense effort (short sprints, occasional stochastic weight-lifting sessions after semi-starvation, exposure to cold, irregular sleep from travel and jet lag, feasting followed by skipped meals), deliberately avoiding any fixed schedule.
- He frames this through "convexity to uncertainty" and against naive thermodynamic (calorie-in, calorie-out) models of diet: food and exercise act as informational signals that trigger metabolic cascades and epigenetic changes (genes up- or down-regulated by environmental contact), so a body deprived of stressors will not survive when it eventually encounters them.
- After roughly two and a half years on this regimen he reports concrete physical changes, loss of unnecessary body fat, blood pressure comparable to a much younger person, and a clearer mind, and frames the improvement as recreating most of the benefits of a hunter-gatherer lifestyle without abandoning an urban, modern life.
- "Beware Manufactured Stability": suppressing ordinary small-scale variability to create artificial regularity backfires across domains, preventing small forest fires sets up larger catastrophic ones, over-prescribing antibiotics breeds more dangerous resistant epidemics, and central banks (the Federal Reserve) trying to iron out the ordinary business cycle for years left the system far more exposed to the severe 2008 collapse.
- He generalizes the point: periods of low measured volatility, whether in markets or in a body kept artificially free of stress, are not evidence of low risk, they can instead signal a quiet shift into Extremistan-style tail risk that is being hidden rather than eliminated.
