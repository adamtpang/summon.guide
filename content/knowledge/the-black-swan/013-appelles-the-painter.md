# Chapter 13: Appelles the Painter, or What Do You Do if You Cannot Predict?

> **Key principle:** Taleb draws the practical playbook out of the preceding prediction chapters. On small, harmless matters, act like a normal confident human and keep forecasting for the picnic; on large, consequential matters, refuse to depend on forecasts at all, and instead position yourself so that unpredictable good outcomes (positive Black Swans) can find you cheaply while unpredictable bad outcomes are capped.

*Synthesized from Chapter 13 of The Black Swan by Nassim Nicholas Taleb.*

## Key lessons

- The chapter takes its title from the ancient Greek painter Apelles, retold by Sextus Empiricus: after struggling for hours to paint the realistic foam at a horse's mouth, Apelles gave up in frustration and hurled his paint-soaked sponge at the canvas, and the resulting accidental splatter created a perfect rendering of the foam. Taleb uses this as the emblem of the empirics' method: engineered accident and trial and error succeeding where deliberate effort failed.
- "Be a fool in the right places": Taleb's first piece of advice is not to try to suppress ordinary human overconfidence in everyday, low-stakes matters (predicting the weather for a picnic, holding opinions), but to sharply withhold trust in predictions on matters that could seriously harm you, such as government social-security forecasts decades out or economists' market calls. Rank beliefs by the harm they could cause if wrong, not by how plausible they sound.
- "The idea of positive accident": since serendipity cannot be aimed at directly, the practical response is to maximize your surface area for lucky accidents, take meetings, go to parties, live in big cities, say yes to unplanned opportunities, because positive Black Swans require first being exposed to them, and most people fail to even recognize a lucky break in the moment it appears.
- Taleb explicitly distinguishes positive-Black-Swan businesses (movies, some publishing, scientific research, venture capital, where the maximum loss is small and bounded but the maximum gain is open-ended) from negative-Black-Swan businesses (banking, insurance, military, homeland security, where the maximum gain is capped but a single rare event can wipe you out). The correct posture toward uncertainty is opposite in each case.
- The barbell strategy, introduced here by name: rather than putting money into a single "medium risk" position (which depends on someone's flawed risk model to even define what "medium" means), split your exposure into two extremes, roughly 85-90% in maximally safe instruments (Taleb names Treasury bills) that no Black Swan can meaningfully damage, and the remaining 10-15% in maximally speculative, highly leveraged bets (he mentions options and venture-capital-style investments) whose downside is capped at what you put in but whose upside is unbounded. The combined portfolio has a defined floor and open-ended upside, a "convex" combination, rather than an unknown, possibly catastrophic tail risk hiding inside a falsely comfortable "moderate" position.
- Taleb explicitly distinguishes the barbell from buying lottery tickets: lottery payoffs are capped by a known, finite jackpot, while positive-Black-Swan bets (a book, a startup, a research bet) have no known upper limit on how large the payoff can become, which is what makes small bounded losses worth risking repeatedly across many such bets rather than concentrating on one.
- He cites screenwriter William Goldman's line "Nobody knows anything" about predicting movie sales, arguing the most successful operators in unpredictable fields don't try to out-predict the field, they structure their exposure so that not knowing works in their favor (many small tries, each with capped downside and unbounded upside) rather than against them.
- Practical rules Taleb lists for exploiting unpredictability: distinguish positive from negative exposure before acting; don't hunt for specific, narrowly predicted Black Swans (this leaves you vulnerable to the ones you didn't predict) but instead invest generally in preparedness; seize opportunities immediately when they appear rather than assuming they'll recur; treat government and institutional predictions skeptically since officials are incentivized to appear competent rather than be accurate; and don't waste energy arguing with professional forecasters. The unifying idea he calls "the great asymmetry": arrange your affairs so that when you are wrong, the cost is small and known, and when you are right, the payoff can be very large, since you can rarely know the true odds of a rare event but you can usually know and control your exposure to its consequences.
