# Chapter 2: Yevgenia's Black Swan

> **Key principle:** Yevgenia Nikolayevna Krasnova, a fictional Franco-Russian neuroscientist-turned-novelist, is rejected by mainstream publishers because her genre-defying manuscript cannot be classified as fiction or nonfiction and has no identifiable target audience. A small publisher with nothing to lose picks her up on unfavorable terms; her book unexpectedly becomes one of literary history's biggest bestsellers, after which critics and scholars retroactively construct an entirely convincing account of why her success was inevitable all along.

*Synthesized from Chapter 2 of The Black Swan by Nassim Nicholas Taleb.*

## Key lessons

- Yevgenia is a neuroscientist with a philosophy interest (her first three husbands were philosophers) who tries to express her research ideas in literary form, refusing standard narrative conventions (she leaves foreign dialogue untranslated, like subtitles, rather than smoothing it into English).
- Mainstream publishers reject her repeatedly because she cannot answer basic gatekeeping questions: "Is this fiction or nonfiction?" and "Who is your audience?" A writing workshop instructor tells her flatly that her case is hopeless, since she refuses to imitate the kind of stories that had recently succeeded (e.g., typical New Yorker-style short fiction).
- She eventually posts her manuscript, "A Story of Recursion," online for free, where a small, unknown publisher (distinguished mainly by pink-rimmed glasses and broken Russian) discovers it and agrees to publish it completely unedited, in exchange for a below-standard royalty rate, since he has little to lose.
- The book slowly catches fire and becomes a massive, translated-into-forty-languages bestseller; the small publishing house grows into a large corporation, and Yevgenia becomes a celebrated pioneer of a supposed "Consilient School" blending science and literature.
- After the fact, literary scholars construct detailed, plausible-sounding intellectual lineages for her success (tracing her style to Milan Kundera, or to Gregory Bateson's autobiographical scholarly writing), even though Yevgenia had never read either author. Taleb uses this to dramatize the narrative fallacy: convincing causal stories get built backward from a known outcome, not forward from real foresight.
- Editors who had earlier rejected her later claim they would have immediately recognized her talent had she only approached them differently, another instance of the retrospective distortion introduced in Chapter 1: success looks obvious and foreseeable only once it has already happened.
- Taleb explicitly labels Yevgenia's rise a Black Swan and uses her case as the bridge into Chapter 3: this kind of extreme, disproportionate, winner-take-almost-all outcome is only possible in the province he is about to name Extremistan, as opposed to the tame, predictable province he calls Mediocristan.
- A footnote clarifies that Yevgenia Krasnova is a fictional character, underscoring that the chapter functions as an illustrative parable rather than a reported case study, deliberately built to be more vivid and memorable than an abstract statistical description of the same phenomenon.
