# Chapter 8: Giacomo Casanova's Unfailing Luck: The Problem of Silent Evidence

> **Key principle:** Taleb names "silent evidence" as the bias created whenever we only observe the survivors, winners, or published accounts of a process, and never the failures, drowned, or forgotten, and argues this single distortion secretly inflates our sense that success stories reveal skill, virtue, or destiny.

*Synthesized from Chapter 8 of The Black Swan by Nassim Nicholas Taleb.*

## Key lessons

- Taleb opens with Cicero's account of the philosopher Diagoras, shown paintings of worshippers who prayed and then survived a shipwreck as proof that prayer protects you; Diagoras's reply, asking where the paintings were of worshippers who prayed and then drowned, is the founding example of silent evidence: the dead cannot advertise their experience, so only the confirming cases survive to be seen.
- Taleb calls this the "cemetery of letters": the Phoenicians were long dismissed as having no literature, when in fact their writing simply perished on fragile papyrus, and Balzac's novel Lost Illusions is cited for coining the image of "nightingales," the vast unread manuscripts sitting forgotten on bookstore shelves, used to argue that celebrated authors like Balzac may be less uniquely gifted than lucky compared to the many comparably talented writers whose work never survived to be judged.
- Popular "how to become a millionaire" studies of successful people's traits (courage, optimism, risk-taking) are shown to be worthless on their own, because Taleb argues the graveyard of failed risk-takers is full of people who shared exactly the same traits; without comparing winners to the much larger, invisible population of people with identical qualities who failed, luck cannot be separated from skill.
- The chapter's central illustration of silent evidence is a thought experiment: a diverse cohort of rats is exposed to rising levels of radiation, and at each level only the naturally stronger rats survive, so the surviving group looks progressively stronger and fitter as a population, even though every individual rat, including the strongest survivor, is actually weaker after radiation than before. Taleb uses this to warn that the bias hides best exactly where the underlying risk is most lethal.
- In "What You See and What You Don't See," drawing on the economist Frederic Bastiat, Taleb contrasts the visible, praised consequences of an action (politicians funding visible Hurricane Katrina relief) against the invisible, unclaimed costs elsewhere (money diverted from cancer research, or the roughly one thousand Americans who died in extra car accidents after switching from flying to driving out of fear following September 11, 2001); the seen dominates our judgment even when the unseen cost is larger.
- Giacomo Casanova, the eighteenth-century adventurer, believed his repeated escapes from ruin proved he had a personal "lucky star" (etoile); Taleb reframes this as survivorship illusion, comparing Casanova to a simulated population of many adventurers, most of whom would have been permanently crushed by at least one setback, so that any single surviving adventurer will retroactively feel singled out by destiny purely because he is the one left to tell the story. The same illusion is applied to claims about New York City's supposed built-in "resilience" through repeated crises.
- The chapter closes with the "anthropic bias," under the heading "I Am a Black Swan": Taleb argues that our own existence, or any streak of survival, cannot be used as evidence of special protection or destiny, because we can only ever observe the branch of history in which we happened to survive to observe it; the correct reference class for judging the odds is the full cohort of everyone who started out, not just the lucky survivor doing the counting.
- Taleb generalizes the lesson as a warning against the word "because": whenever survival itself is the condition that allowed an observation to be made at all (why didn't the plague kill more people, why did Casanova keep bouncing back), reaching for a causal explanation is often just narrative fallacy in disguise, and the more honest response is to admit that randomness, not a hidden cause, may be doing the real work.
