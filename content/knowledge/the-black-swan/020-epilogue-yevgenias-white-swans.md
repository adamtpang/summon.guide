# Epilogue: Yevgenia's White Swans

> **Key principle:** Taleb closes the fictional Yevgenia Krasnova thread by having her second book, The Loop, receive glowing reviews yet sell almost no copies, an outcome nobody, including her publisher, could foresee, so that the very career of the woman whose debut was used earlier in the book to illustrate the Black Swan becomes, once again, an unpredictable and inexplicable-in-advance event.

*Synthesized from the Epilogue of The Black Swan by Nassim Nicholas Taleb.*

## Key lessons

- Yevgenia retreats into eight years of secluded, longhand writing in New York, dictating to a rotating cast of secretaries and growing dissatisfied with her own drafts even as she comes to see her acclaimed first book as shallow by comparison.
- She avoids the press and reviews entirely once her new novel, The Loop, is published, staying deliberately insulated from how it is received.
- The reviews are uniformly laudatory, exactly as her publisher expected, yet almost nobody actually buys the book; within a year and a half it is effectively out of print.
- The publisher, who had bet his own financial solvency on Yevgenia delivering another hit, cannot explain the gap between critical praise and commercial failure; he eventually settles on the theory that the book was simply "too long."
- After a tearful reckoning, Yevgenia reflects on the numbing, second-rate characters of novelists like Georges Simenon and Graham Greene, and decides she has always preferred charm to beauty, suggesting a private reconciliation with obscurity rather than despair over it.
- The chapter's final line makes the recursive point explicit: Yevgenia's second book, like her first, was itself a Black Swan, an outcome nobody, including the people whose job it is to predict such things (publishers, critics, the market), could have called in advance.
- The episode functions as a closing demonstration rather than a moral: even inside a life already defined by one Black Swan (the runaway, unexpected success of her debut), the same unpredictability strikes again, this time as failure instead of triumph, underscoring that Black Swans are not reserved for exceptional or first-time events.
