# Chapter 17: Locke's Madmen, or Bell Curves in the Wrong Places

> **Key principle:** John Locke defined a madman as someone "reasoning correctly from erroneous premises," and Taleb applies this directly to neoclassical financial economics: elegant, internally consistent, Nobel-winning mathematics built on the false premise that markets are Gaussian, which produced real institutional disasters once markets behaved like Extremistan.

*Synthesized from Chapter 17 of The Black Swan by Nassim Nicholas Taleb.*

## Key lessons

- Taleb states flatly that in his own large personal library of statistics books, not one deals properly with Extremistan; the field trains hundreds of thousands of business and social-science students each year in Gaussian methods and then turns them loose on real-world data, which he compares to developing a medicine for plants and administering it to humans.
- "Only Fifty Years" makes the empirical case concretely: over the prior fifty years of U.S. stock market history, the ten single most extreme trading days account for roughly half of all cumulative returns, evidence Taleb says should be more than enough (he calls invoking anything beyond "six sigma" absurd) to establish that markets belong to Extremistan, not Mediocristan.
- "The Clerks' Betrayal" recounts the 1987 crash, a move so large that under true Gaussian assumptions it would be expected roughly once every several billion lifetimes of the universe; practitioners briefly agreed publicly that sigma-based risk measures were fraudulent, then reverted to the same Gaussian tools back at the office the next day because, in Taleb's words, people wanted a number and had "nothing else" to anchor on.
- The chapter gives a short, pointed history of the economics Nobel: created by the Bank of Sweden (not part of Alfred Nobel's original bequest, a fact his own family has objected to), it went to Harry Markowitz and William Sharpe for Modern Portfolio Theory, a body of work Taleb argues collapses into "hot air" the moment you strip out its Gaussian assumptions and treat prices as scalable instead.
- Robert Merton and Myron Scholes received the prize in 1997 for an option-pricing formula that made earlier, less restrictive work (credited by Taleb to Ed Thorp and Louis Bachelier) dependent on Gaussian assumptions those originators had not needed; Taleb, who traded options professionally, argues the formula misses that a long-shot option benefits disproportionately from Black Swans.
- LTCM (Long-Term Capital Management), the hedge fund co-founded by Merton and Scholes, collapsed in 1998 after a Russian financial crisis produced a combination of events their risk models had treated as essentially impossible; because their models excluded large deviations, the firm had taken on exposures large enough that its failure nearly destabilized the broader financial system, and the aftermath simply produced the excuse "it was just a Black Swan" rather than a reckoning with the underlying methodology.
- Taleb recounts being personally attacked at conferences by prominent economists and mathematicians (including a heated exchange with Myron Scholes) whenever he presented empirical evidence of Extremistan in markets, and observes that critics almost always attacked a distorted version of his claim (that markets are "totally unpredictable") rather than his actual position (that they are largely random and fat-tailed).
- Table 4 in the chapter lays out two opposed postures toward randomness: "skeptical empiricism," bottom-up, comfortable saying "I don't know," assumes Extremistan, and prefers being broadly right, versus "the Platonic approach," top-down, built on elegant closed models like Monopoly, assumes Mediocristan, and prefers being precisely wrong; Taleb personifies the two sides as "Fat Tony" and "Dr. John."
- He closes on an asymmetry of evidence that runs through the whole argument: a single extreme observation is sufficient to disprove that a process is Gaussian, but no quantity of quiet, uneventful historical data can ever fully confirm that it is, since Extremistan tolerates long calm stretches just as easily as Mediocristan does; practitioners who cited quiet-period data as "proof" of Gaussian safety were, in his framing, confusing absence of evidence with evidence of absence.
