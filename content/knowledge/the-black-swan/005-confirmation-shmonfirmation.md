# Chapter 5: Confirmation Shmonfirmation!

> **Key principle:** Taleb names our natural habit of collecting only supporting evidence "naive empiricism," and contrasts it with Karl Popper's asymmetry: you can never be fully certain a general rule is true no matter how many confirming cases you pile up, but one clean counterexample can certify it false. Seeing a black swan proves that not all swans are white; no number of white swans proves the opposite.

*Synthesized from Chapter 5 of The Black Swan by Nassim Nicholas Taleb.*

## Key lessons

- Taleb names the "round-trip fallacy": confusing "there is no evidence of X" with "there is evidence of no X." He illustrates it with a mock claim that having breakfast with O. J. Simpson without being killed proves his innocence, and with the more serious case of turkey-style observation, where a thousand calm days tell you nothing about whether a catastrophic day is coming.
- The same fallacy shows up in real statistics: if 99 percent of terrorists are Muslim, that does not mean most Muslims are terrorists, since terrorists are a tiny fraction of over a billion Muslims. Taleb uses this to show how easily people convert "most A are B" into the false converse "most B are A."
- Doctors commit the same error with the acronym NED (No Evidence of Disease): a clean cancer scan means no cancer was found, not proof of no cancer, since it is impossible to examine every cell. Taleb ties this to historical medical mistakes, like dismissing mothers' milk and dietary fiber as useless simply because no benefit had yet been observed.
- Taleb calls our reasoning "domain-specific": the same person who can spot a logical trap on the SAT (all zoogles are boogles, therefore not all boogles are zoogles) will make elementary probability mistakes in daily life. He cites Kahneman and Tversky's experiment in which trained statisticians, informally asked which of two hospitals is more likely to have 60 percent boys born on a given day, wrongly picked the larger hospital, when smaller samples should fluctuate more from the 50/50 average.
- Karl Popper's idea of falsification is presented as the fix: you cannot prove a general theory true by accumulating confirming cases, but you can prove it false with one strong counterexample, so science should proceed by forming bold conjectures and actively hunting for the observation that would refute them, rather than the one that would support them.
- The psychologist P. C. Wason's 2-4-6 experiment showed that when subjects were told a rule generated the sequence 2, 4, 6, they overwhelmingly tested only sequences meant to confirm their guessed rule (like "even numbers going up by two") rather than sequences designed to break it, so almost none discovered the actual rule, "numbers in ascending order." Taleb contrasts this with chess grandmasters, who train themselves to look for the weakness in their own move rather than confirmation of its strength.
- Hempel's raven paradox, illustrated when Taleb's friend Bruno Dupire pointed at a red Mini Cooper and joked "Look, no black swan," shows the absurdity taken to its logical extreme: since "all swans are white" is logically equivalent to "all non-white things are non-swans," a naive confirmation-seeker would have to treat every non-white, non-swan object (a red car, a green shirt) as supporting evidence.
- Taleb argues our inductive instincts are themselves domain-specific and evolved for a different, more repeatable environment (what he elsewhere calls Mediocristan): children readily generalize a stranger's skin color to their whole tribe but not their body weight, showing built-in selective learning rather than blank-slate induction from experience alone. In today's world of "Extremistan," dominated by rare, high-impact events, this inherited instinct to trust a long run of calm, confirming days leaves us structurally unprepared for the black swan that a thousand white days cannot rule out.
