# Chapter 11: How to Look for Bird Poop

> **Key principle:** Taleb catalogs major discoveries, penicillin, the cosmic microwave background, the laser, Viagra, that were found by people looking for something else entirely, then argues that beyond human overconfidence there are genuine structural limits to prediction: Popper's regress problem (to predict a future discovery you would already need to possess it), Poincare's three-body problem showing how tiny imprecision explodes with each added interaction, and Hayek's critique of "scientism," the false belief that social systems can be centrally modeled the way physical ones can.

*Synthesized from Chapter 11 of The Black Swan by Nassim Nicholas Taleb.*

## Key lessons

- The chapter's title comes from Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson, two Bell Labs radio astronomers who in 1965 were trying to eliminate a persistent background hiss in their antenna, at first blaming it on bird droppings in the dish, before realizing they had stumbled onto the cosmic microwave background radiation, evidence for the big bang, a discovery neither man was looking for.
- Other examples of pure serendipity: Alexander Fleming discovered penicillin's antibacterial properties when mold accidentally contaminated a lab experiment he had abandoned; the laser was invented by Charles Townes with no application in mind and only later found uses in eye surgery, CDs, and data storage; Viagra began as a failed hypertension drug; a different hypertension drug became a hair-growth treatment. Taleb calls this pattern "a solution waiting for a problem."
- Taleb argues engineers and researchers largely build tools for the pleasure of building them, and knowledge often flows backward from tool to theory rather than forward from theory to tool; he cites the computer, built for other purposes, later enabling new visual/geometric mathematics nobody was seeking.
- "How to predict your predictions": drawing on Karl Popper's attack on historicism, Taleb explains the regress problem, to predict a future technological discovery (like the wheel) with any real content, you would already need to know how to build it, meaning genuine future knowledge cannot be predicted in advance without already possessing it. He generalizes this into a "law of iterated expectations": if you will certainly know something tomorrow, you already effectively know it today.
- "The Nth billiard ball": Henri Poincare's work on the three-body problem showed that adding just one small third object (like a comet) to a two-body orbital system makes long-run prediction explosively sensitive to tiny errors in initial conditions. Taleb extends this with mathematician Michael Berry's billiard-ball example: predicting the ninth collision on a pool table requires accounting for the gravitational pull of a person standing nearby, and predicting the fifty-sixth collision would require knowing the position of every particle in the universe. This illustrates that some systems are not just hard to predict but structurally, not just practically, resistant to it, a point later rediscovered independently by meteorologist Edward Lorenz as the "butterfly effect."
- Friedrich Hayek's 1974 Nobel address, "The Pretense of Knowledge," is presented as a parallel critique aimed at economics and central planning: no single planner or institution can aggregate the dispersed, tacit knowledge distributed across a whole society, so top-down modeling of social and economic systems (what Hayek called "scientism," borrowing the prestige of physics without its epistemic justification) is fundamentally misguided, not just difficult.
- Taleb links prediction to free will: fully predicting human behavior would require treating people as deterministic automatons, which conflicts with the intuitive sense that people choose. He criticizes how orthodox (Samuelson-style) economics sidesteps this by simply assuming humans are perfectly "rational" optimizers, a mathematically convenient but empirically false simplification that behavioral economists (citing Kahneman and Tversky's anchoring experiments) have repeatedly shown does not describe real decision-making.
- The chapter closes on the "riddle of induction" via philosopher Nelson Goodman's grue paradox: a series of past green-emerald observations is equally consistent with the emerald staying green forever or with it being "grue," green until an arbitrary future date and blue after. Taleb uses this to argue that a trend line fit to past data (his running Figures 2-6 of a fitted line that later diverges from the real generating process) never uniquely determines the future; there are infinitely many curves consistent with any past data set, so naive trend-extrapolation is itself a form of narrative fallacy.
