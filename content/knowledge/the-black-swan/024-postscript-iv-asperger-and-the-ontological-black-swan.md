# Postscript IV: Asperger and the Ontological Black Swan

> **Key principle:** Taleb argues that the confusion over whether the September 11 attacks or the 2008 crisis were "really" Black Swans stems from treating Black Swan status as objective rather than observer-dependent, an error he compares to the underdeveloped theory of mind seen in Asperger syndrome, and extends this into a broader claim that probability itself must be understood as a subjective degree of belief, since ontological and epistemic uncertainty can never be cleanly separated in practice.

*Synthesized from Part IV of the Postscript Essay of The Black Swan by Nassim Nicholas Taleb.*

## Key lessons

- A Black Swan is defined relative to a particular observer, not as an objective feature of an event: the September 11 attacks were a Black Swan to the victims who did not expect them, but not to the terrorists who planned them; Taleb sums up the idea in one recurring line: "a Black Swan for the turkey is not a Black Swan for the butcher." The 2008 crisis was a Black Swan to nearly every economist and financier but not to Taleb himself.
- He connects this observer-relativity to "theory of mind," the cognitive ability to recognize that other people can hold different knowledge or beliefs than you do, tested in children with the false-belief task (where a toy is hidden and the subject must predict what another child, who did not see it moved, will believe); people who struggle with this, associated with mild autism or Asperger syndrome, tend to treat their own knowledge as universal.
- Citing psychologist Simon Baron-Cohen's research on a spectrum between "systematizing" and "empathizing" minds, Taleb notes that systematizers (overrepresented in engineering, physics, and mathematical economics, and skewed male) are more prone to Black Swan blindness and highly averse to ambiguity, while empathizing, socially attuned minds are less prone to this trap.
- As informal evidence, he cites a calculation he and George Martin ran in 1998 finding that finance and economics professors who moved into hedge fund trading disproportionately made bets exposed to blowup risk, compared to non-academic traders, singling out Myron Scholes and Robert Merton (of Long-Term Capital Management) as illustrations.
- "Future Blindness Redux": Alan Greenspan told Congress the banking crisis could not have been foreseen because "it had never happened before," a justification Taleb mocks (Greenspan himself had never died in eighty years, which does not make him immortal), and generalizes into a critique of "stress tests" that anchor on the worst past deviation without recognizing that the same method would have failed to predict that very anchor event the day before it happened.
- He states his finding that ordinary, regular events can be predicted from other regular events, but extreme deviations essentially never have identifiable predecessors of similar scale (the Great War, the 1987 crash of nearly 23 percent in a single day), which undercuts forecasting approaches that extrapolate from history's worst prior case.
- In "Probability Has to Be Subjective," he traces subjective probability to Frank Plumpton Ramsey (1925) and Bruno de Finetti (1937): probability as a quantified degree of belief, constrained mainly by consistency (the "Dutch book" rule against holding beliefs that guarantee you a loss), rather than a fixed, objectively measurable property like temperature.
- He distinguishes ontological/ontic uncertainty (genuine randomness that no amount of prior information could have let you derive in advance, associated with "nonergodic" systems that have no stable long-run average and are prone to path dependency) from epistemic uncertainty (randomness that only exists because our own knowledge is incomplete), but argues the distinction, while philosophically real, is practically unusable, since epistemic limitations are so hard to disentangle from any deeper "true" randomness that insisting on the difference mostly distracts from the real, practical problem of limited knowledge.
- He also critiques the economic notion of "rational expectations," where observers are assumed to converge on the same beliefs given the same data, arguing that people who assume Mediocristan-style randomness and people who assume Extremistan-style randomness will never converge, because Extremistan assumptions resist being revised quickly by ordinary evidence.
