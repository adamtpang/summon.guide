# Creation

> **Key principle:** Genes and explanatory ideas are both abstract replicators, information that, once embodied in a suitable medium, tends to cause itself to persist there. Neo-Darwinian evolution (random variation of genes plus selection by differential replication) explains how the knowledge in biological adaptations is created without any designer, exactly as conjecture-and-criticism explains how human knowledge is created without deriving it from experience. Creationism, spontaneous generation, and Lamarckism all fail for the same reason: each denies, rather than explains, how new knowledge could possibly come into existence.

*Synthesized from Chapter 4 of The Beginning of Infinity by David Deutsch.*

## Key lessons

- Creationism ("the gods did it") is a bad explanation in the Chapter 1 sense: an undesigned or supernatural designer either had to have the relevant knowledge "just there" (in which case positing the designer explains nothing, since the biosphere itself could equally have "just happened" with that knowledge already embodied), or the designer's own origin needs explaining, regressing endlessly. Deutsch also notes many adaptations are actively bad design (the vertebrate eye's backwards wiring and blind spot, versus the squid eye's better layout; the vestigial, non-functional vitamin-C gene in primates), which is hard to reconcile with a benevolent, competent designer.
- William Paley's watch-on-the-heath argument (1802) is treated seriously and praised for correctly identifying what needs explaining: a watch, unlike a stone, has parts arranged so that if they were slightly different it would no longer work, exactly the "hard to vary" signature of a good explanation or genuine design. Paley's error was concluding that only a conscious designer could produce this; his own argument, applied to "the ultimate designer," would force that designer to need a designer too, which is a contradiction, so it actually rules out a designer, not proves one.
- Spontaneous generation (mice forming from rags, refuted experimentally by Louis Pasteur in 1859, the same year Darwin published) and Lamarckism (Jean-Baptiste Lamarck's theory that an organism's acquired traits during its lifetime, like a giraffe stretching its neck, are inherited by its offspring) are both diagnosed as variants of the same error as inductivism: they assume new knowledge can be present in, or derived directly from, experience, rather than requiring conjecture followed by testing.
- Deutsch corrects two popular misreadings of Darwinism: evolution does not maximize "the good of the species" (illustrated by a thought experiment about birds evolving to nest ever earlier for the best nesting sites, which lowers the total population even as it "wins" for the genes involved), and it is not "survival of the fittest" in the sense of always improving functionality (the peacock's tail, which actively endangers the bird, spread purely because peahens preferred it). What evolution actually optimizes is a gene's relative ability to get itself replicated more than rival variants, nothing else.
- Neo-Darwinism, at its most general, is a theory about replicators (anything that causally contributes to its own copying), not specifically about biology. Genes are replicators; so are memes, Richard Dawkins's term (credited here) for ideas that are replicators, such as a joke that spreads because people who hear it tend to retell it. Both knowledge and adaptations are "abstract replicators": what actually persists and gets copied is the information itself, which is why the same gene or idea can be stored in different physical media (DNA, a computer file, a different language) and still count as the same replicator.
- Deutsch addresses the fine-tuning of physical constants (first calculated by Brandon Carter in 1974: if certain force strengths or the Big Bang's expansion rate had been slightly different, no stars, planets, or life could exist) and rejects both the supernatural-designer reading and pure "anthropic" multiverse explanations. Citing physicist Dennis Sciama's argument, he shows that with many fine-tuned constants, an anthropic explanation actually predicts our universe should sit near the edge of life-permitting values, not comfortably in the middle, and citing a Feynman-style argument, that "all possible laws are instantiated" theories predict we are far more likely to be in a chaotic, about-to-be-destroyed universe than a stable one, making both bad, easily-variable explanations that fail to do the specific explanatory work needed.
- The chapter's summary judgment: every false theory it examines, creationism, spontaneous generation, Lamarckism, and unsupported anthropic fine-tuning arguments, is at root a form of "creation denial": each one fails to explain, or actively denies, how new knowledge (in a gene or in a scientific discovery) could come into existence in the first place, whereas neo-Darwinism and the Popperian theory of knowledge are the two theories that actually describe real creation, calling this book's recurring idea of "unpredictable creation ex nihilo," since no predictive process can reveal a discovery's content before it is made.
