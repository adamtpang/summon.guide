# Closer to Reality

> **Key principle:** Deutsch's own experience cataloguing galaxies through a microscope aimed at photographic plates is used to show that we never observe anything directly: all observation is theory-laden, and instruments work by feeding our senses interpretations, not raw contact with the world. Paradoxically, the more machinery (telescopes, cameras, computers) stands between us and a star or a subatomic particle, the more accurately we can know it, because each layer embeds more corrective theory.

*Synthesized from Chapter 2 of The Beginning of Infinity by David Deutsch.*

## Key lessons

- As a graduate student, Deutsch catalogued galaxy clusters by examining Palomar Sky Survey photographic plates under a microscope, clicking cross-hairs onto each galaxy's estimated center; the task looked mindless but actually required constant judgment about which faint, fuzzy shapes were galaxies versus stars or plate defects.
- Feeling insignificant (or feeling relieved at insignificance) in the face of the universe's scale is a mistake in both directions: Deutsch argues the universe is not something that should overwhelm us, since it is our home and our resource. The bigness of the cosmos carries no implication about human importance.
- At one point Deutsch misidentified an object and asked his hosts whether it was a galaxy or a star; the answer was that it was merely a flaw in the photographic emulsion, a concrete demonstration that even careful, trained observation is an interpretive act that can be wrong, and that an error is always an error in an explanation of something, not a failure of raw perception.
- Every scientific instrument (telescope, particle accelerator, electron microscope) works by physically separating the observer further from the object of study, yet subjectively and epistemically it brings them "closer to reality." Astronomers today mostly never look through eyepieces at all; they look at pixels, numbers, and graphs, and thereby genuinely observe galaxies, because their theories tell them how to correctly attribute that local evidence to the distant reality.
- Jocelyn Bell's discovery of the first pulsar is presented as the clearest case: what she actually perceived was a shaky line of ink on paper from a radio telescope's chart recorder, and only a long chain of theoretical interpretation let her recognize it as a new type of dense, rapidly spinning star. The longer and better that interpretive chain, the more theory each additional link requires, and a single unnoticed error anywhere in the chain can make the resulting "observation" arbitrarily misleading.
- The empiricist ideal of "pure," theory-free observation is exactly backwards: truly accurate observation is always hugely indirect and requires applying more explanatory knowledge in advance, not less. Even a telescope's mechanical tracking and adaptive-optics corrections exist to strip away appearances (twinkling, apparent motion of stars) that are mere artifacts of the Earth's atmosphere and rotation, not properties of the stars themselves.
- Instruments work by placing rare, fragile, highly specific configurations of ordinary matter (iron, sand, silicon) into forms that, when observed, take on the appearance of something else entirely elsewhere in the universe; alter that configuration even slightly (press the wrong button) and it reverts to showing nothing but itself. The chapter's implicit criterion, carried over from Chapter 1, is that something is real if and only if it figures in our best explanation of what we experience — which is why the specks of silver on Deutsch's photographic plate really were, in that sense, galaxies.
- The chapter closes by rejecting Edison's "one per cent inspiration, ninety-nine per cent perspiration" framing of research as mostly mindless toil: a repetitive experiment is not repetitive if the experimenter is thinking about the ideas it tests and the reality it investigates, so science is not drudgery redeemed by rare discoveries but can be creative and enjoyable throughout.
