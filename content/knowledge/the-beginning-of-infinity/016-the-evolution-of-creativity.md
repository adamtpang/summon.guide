# The Evolution of Creativity

> **Key principle:** Two puzzles are solved by the same insight. First: human creativity could not have evolved for its power to innovate, because for hundreds of thousands of years our ancestors innovated on a timescale of many millennia, far too slowly to give more creative individuals any measurable reproductive advantage. Second: human memes (unlike simple ape or parrot "memes") contain knowledge the recipient never directly observes, so how can they possibly be copied? The answer to both: creativity did not evolve to invent new ideas, it evolved to replicate existing ideas at all, since acquiring a genuine human meme requires the same act of conjecture and criticism as creating a new one. Only in static societies, which reward faithful replication of existing memes above all else, was this creativity prevented from also producing visible innovation.

*Synthesized from Chapter 16 of The Beginning of Infinity by David Deutsch.*

## Key lessons

- The chapter opens with the paradox: the fossil and archaeological record shows innovation (new tool designs, technologies) happening on a timescale of many thousands of years throughout most of human prehistory, yet the underlying capacity for creativity must have been evolving rapidly (by the standards of gene evolution) for ape-like ancestors to become modern humans so quickly. If more-creative individuals were only rarely able to actually innovate, there could have been no consistent selection pressure favoring creativity, so what was creativity actually being used for?
- Deutsch rejects sexual-selection theories (creativity evolved as a mating display, like the peacock's tail) as implausible: creativity is a far more sophisticated adaptation than coloration or body shape (we still can't replicate it artificially), and if it were being intensively used for any purpose, including displays, it's unclear why it would never spill over into functional, practical innovation as well.
- The second puzzle: meme replication is often described as "imitation," but Deutsch argues true imitation of complex behavior is logically impossible without already knowing what aspect of the behavior matters, using Popper's own habit of opening his philosophy-of-science lectures by asking students simply to "observe," then waiting for someone to ask what they're supposed to observe, to illustrate that there is no such thing as observation, or imitation, without prior theory. "Imitate Popper" has infinitely many possible interpretations (his accent, his posture, facing where he faced); one must already know the relevant idea in his mind to select the right one to imitate.
- Deutsch draws the crucial contrast with real animal "imitation": parrots parrot and apes ape using a fundamentally different, non-creative mechanism, an inborn, genetically fixed repertoire of copiable behaviors (identified partly via mirror neurons) combined, in apes, with "behaviour parsing" (a term from researcher Richard Byrne): breaking an observed action sequence into pre-known elementary units and statistically inferring their order, without ever understanding why the elements fit together. This lets apes copy simple multi-step tricks like cracking nuts with rocks, but only because every element and its meaning is already genetically pre-specified; nothing genuinely new or unbounded can be transmitted this way, and apes can't even parrot novel sounds because that translation pathway never evolved in them.
- Human meme acquisition is categorically different: a person hearing a lecture is not trying to reproduce the lecturer's exact words or gestures (most of which are forgotten immediately) but is trying to discover the meaning, the underlying idea, using conjecture and criticism to explain the lecturer's behavior, exactly the way a scientist explains a natural phenomenon. This is why a human listener can often correctly infer an idea even from an inconsistent, error-ridden, or garbled account of it, something no parrot or ape could ever do.
- Deutsch's unifying answer to both puzzles: what replicates human memes is the very same creativity used to create new knowledge, since acquiring a meme (discovering a hidden explanation, another person's idea) faces the identical logical challenge as scientific discovery (discovering a hidden law of nature): both require guessing an explanation from indirect evidence, since neither the idea nor the law can be directly observed. Creativity therefore evolved specifically to acquire existing knowledge more efficiently, not to generate new knowledge, but because the underlying mechanism (conjecture and criticism) is identical for both tasks, gaining the ability to do the former automatically conferred the latter as well, an instance of unplanned reach exactly like the jumps to universality in Chapter 6.
- This resolves the innovation puzzle: our ancestors' societies were static societies (Chapter 15), where status and reproductive success came from enacting existing memes with exceptional fidelity, not from innovating. So there was, paradoxically, strong selection pressure for ever-greater creativity used specifically to become a better replicator of existing tribal, parental, and priestly expectations: paradoxically, it takes creativity to thrive in a static society, precisely the kind of creativity that lets someone appear less innovative than everyone around them. The huge reservoir of practical, world-transforming creative power was there all along but was systematically suppressed by the very static-society dynamics it helped sustain.
- Deutsch credits Susan Blackmore's core "meme machine" idea (that the human brain evolved specifically to replicate memes) as correct, while rejecting her downplaying of creativity, since human-type memes are impossible without creativity (both necessary and, per evidence like deaf, blind, or paralyzed people acquiring full human culture despite lacking specific sensory/motor adaptations, sufficient), which is why other proposed drivers of human evolution (upright walking, tool-use, sexual selection, the "Machiavellian hypothesis" of intelligence evolving to predict and deceive others) are not philosophically fundamental to what makes humans people: creative, universal explainers. The chapter closes by describing the long era of static, prehistoric societies as an almost cruel cosmic joke, in which creativity, which could have produced endless useful innovation from the start, was instead turned by memetic evolution into a tool for its own suppression, until the Enlightenment finally broke the pattern.
