# The Beginning

> **Key principle:** The book's closing argument is that we are never "nearly there" in knowledge, and both ways of believing otherwise, the dismal view that fixed limits exist, and the falsely cheerful view that only insignificant details remain (exemplified by John Horgan's The End of Science), are prophetic fallacies. Real, deep, currently unsolved problems (the inconsistency between quantum theory and general relativity, the discovery of dark energy, the nature of qualia and creativity, why the genetic code has the reach it has) prove ignorance still vastly outweighs knowledge. Growth of knowledge should be understood not as solutions replacing problems, but as problems being replaced by better problems. Since the content of future knowledge is fundamentally unpredictable, the one thing still within human control is which direction that unbounded future takes: toward deepening error and stagnation, or toward deepening understanding and life.

*Synthesized from Chapter 18 of The Beginning of Infinity by David Deutsch.*

## Key lessons

- Deutsch opens by noting that even Richard Feynman, one of the 20th century's greatest physicists, fell into the prophetic fallacy, writing in 1965 that fundamental physics would see "certainly not... novelty, say for a thousand years," comparing further discovery to "the discovery of America, you only discover it once." Deutsch's rebuttal: even the concept of a "law" of nature, and of "levels of explanation," are historically contingent ideas that have changed before and will change again, so no one can know in advance whether a field's foundational concepts themselves are about to be superseded.
- Deutsch distinguishes two false versions of believing we are "nearly there": the "dismal" version (knowledge is bounded by nature or supernatural decree, so progress is temporary, sometimes even called "optimism" historically) and the falsely "cheerful" version (all remaining ignorance will soon be mopped up), and argues both share the same defect as Lagrange's claim that Newton was uniquely lucky because "the system of the world can be discovered only once," or Michelson's 1894 claim that physics remained only to be filled in "in the sixth place of decimals": both would have made the entire subsequent revolutions of relativity, quantum theory, and cosmology permanently unimaginable, "a tiny, frozen island of explanation in an ocean of incomprehensibility."
- Deutsch proposes reframing all scientific theories as "misconceptions" from the outset (Einstein's Misconception of Gravity improving on Newton's Misconception, improving on Kepler's) to make clear that science claims neither infallibility nor finality, and reframes the growth of knowledge generally as a transition from problems to better problems (not from problems to solutions, nor from theories to better theories): today's physics problems, rooted in twentieth-century discoveries, embody more knowledge than Einstein's own problems did, which were rooted in Newton and Euclid.
- Deutsch directly rebuts John Horgan's 1996 book The End of Science, which argued fundamental science had essentially already been completed. Deutsch traces Horgan's error to empiricism (wrongly believing science alone can "resolve questions" objectively while philosophy and art produce only irreducibly "ironic," ambiguous interpretations) and to conflating two different senses of "ambiguity," multiple true meanings in a deep idea, versus vagueness or self-contradiction, a confusion borrowed from bad postmodernist philosophy (Chapter 12). Deutsch argues Horgan's method can pre-emptively redefine any future discovery as either "a new equation" (not fundamental) or "a new interpretation" (dismissed as mere "ironic," non-objective philosophy), guaranteeing the conclusion in advance.
- As concrete evidence that deep, fundamental problems remain wide open, Deutsch cites the unresolved inconsistency between quantum theory and general relativity (the "problem of quantum gravity") and the discovery of dark energy: cosmological models assumed the universe's expansion was slowing under gravity until supernova observations showed it is instead accelerating, forcing physicists to posit an unexplained "dark energy" with no adequate theory yet for its magnitude or nature, decisively refuting the idea that cosmology is a "fundamentally completed science."
- Deutsch surveys and largely rejects a cluster of speculative anthropic-style arguments as either unfounded or self-canceling: the multiverse-based "quantum suicide" argument and the "simulation argument" (both require an unjustified assumption about how to count or weight multiple instances of oneself, which Deutsch illustrates with a "puff pastry" multi-layered-space thought experiment showing that raw instance-counting is not a valid guide to probability), and the "doomsday argument" (estimating humanity's remaining lifespan from assuming we're at a "typical" position in the sequence of all humans, which fails once effective immortality via brain-backup technology is considered, and which, if humanity turns out to be infinite, actually suggests we are at the very beginning).
- On Vernor Vinge's 1993 concept of "the Singularity" (the point past which AI-driven change becomes unpredictable, sometimes framed as "the end of the human era"), Deutsch argues this is a parochial misconception: because humans are already universal explainers and constructors (Chapter 3), there can be no such thing as a "superhuman mind" in any meaningful sense, only faster and more automated versions of the same universal human-type thinking, and he predicts humans' capacity to cope with accelerating change will keep pace with the acceleration itself: even if everyone could suddenly think a million times faster, no one would feel any more rushed than they do now, since the pace of life would speed up right along with the pace of thought.
- The chapter, and the book, closes on Deutsch's central image: comparing our present understanding of the physical universe to Eratosthenes's accurate but radically incomplete third-century-BCE measurement of the Earth's circumference, we know a great deal, yet remain deeply ignorant of AI, qualia, creativity, why the genetic code has the reach it has, and know that both of physics's deepest current theories must be false somewhere. The book's closing argument is that since the future cannot be predicted, only chosen, the single mode of thought capable of sustaining progress, or even survival, over the long run is the pursuit of good explanations through creativity and criticism. Infinity, in some form, lies ahead regardless; the only real choice left to us, Deutsch concludes, is what kind of infinity it will be, one of ignorance or of knowledge, of error or of truth, of death or of life.
