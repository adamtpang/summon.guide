# Optimism

> **Key principle:** Because future knowledge cannot be predicted in advance (no explanation has enough reach to foresee its own successors), both blind optimism and blind pessimism, including the "precautionary principle," are prophecy in disguise and both are self-undermining. Deutsch's Principle of Optimism states that all evils and failures are caused by insufficient knowledge, not by any inherent limit on what problems can be solved, which is why rational policy for the unknown future is to remain open to inconceivable solutions rather than trying to derive safety from existing knowledge alone. Historically this optimistic stance has flared briefly in mini-enlightenments (Periclean Athens, Medici Florence) before being extinguished; only the modern Enlightenment has, so far, sustained it.

*Synthesized from Chapter 9 of The Beginning of Infinity by David Deutsch.*

## Key lessons

- Deutsch distinguishes prediction (a conclusion that follows from a good explanation) from prophecy (claiming to know what is not yet knowable), following Popper. Martin Rees's Our Final Century (2003), which puts civilization's odds of surviving the 21st century at 50 percent, and Thomas Malthus's 1798 essay predicting imminent, permanent famine from exponential population growth, are both treated as respected but ultimately unreliable prophecies: both ignored that solving future problems depends on knowledge not yet created, which by definition cannot be foreseen or assigned a probability, unlike the genuinely random outcome of a game like Russian roulette.
- Physicist Albert Michelson's 1894 declaration that physics was nearly complete, "our future discoveries must be looked for in the sixth place of decimals," is used as the paradigm case of prophecy dressed as prediction: Michelson (and, earlier, Lagrange praising Newton as uniquely "lucky" because "the system of the world can be discovered only once") could not conceive of relativity or quantum theory because their own best available knowledge was the very thing about to be overturned, and Michelson's own experiment with Edward Morley unknowingly supplied a key piece of evidence for Einstein's relativity.
- Blind optimism (proceeding as if bad outcomes cannot happen, exemplified by the "unsinkable" Titanic) and blind pessimism (the precautionary principle: avoid anything not known to be safe) are shown to be mirror images that share the same underlying mistake, both try to derive policy from existing knowledge by fiat rather than treating policies as fallible conjectures to be criticized. Deutsch's key counter to blind pessimism: the harm from any innovation that doesn't destroy the growth of knowledge is always finite, but the potential good is unlimited, and virtually every fallen historical civilization could have been saved by more knowledge, not more caution, since most enthusiastically practiced the precautionary principle already.
- Deutsch rebuts Stephen Hawking's suggestion that humanity should hide from potential hostile extraterrestrials (comparing contact to Columbus's arrival in the Americas): this both misapplies the Spaceship Earth idea at galactic scale (assuming an advanced civilization would need Earth's raw materials rather than being able to transmute matter, per Chapter 3) and ignores that there can be only one type of person, universal explainers and constructors, so an advanced alien civilization would still be bound by the same rational, critical methods of progress, not by conquistador-style predation.
- The Principle of Optimism is stated plainly: "All evils are caused by insufficient knowledge." It is primarily a way of explaining failure (there is no fundamental barrier, supernatural decree, or inherent limit on reason preventing progress, only not-yet-created knowledge), not a claim that success is guaranteed. Deutsch extends this explicitly to death and aging: since a sick or aging person is a physical object, and no law of physics forbids transforming it into the same person in good health, the only thing standing between us and a cure is not knowing how, making death, like any other evil, in principle soluble.
- Deutsch reframes "hard" problems: a hard problem is not one unlikely to be solved, difficulty is often exactly what motivates people to solve it, illustrated by JFK's 1962 "we choose to go to the moon... not because it is easy, but because it is hard" speech, where success required knowledge (new metal alloys, guidance systems) that did not yet exist and whose creation could not be assigned a probability in advance.
- Deutsch reframes political philosophy through Popper: the traditional "who should rule?" question (monarch, aristocrats, "the people") is diagnosed as the political analogue of empiricism's mistake, seeking to derive or justify the right rulers from some authoritative source. The right question instead is "how can we get rid of bad rulers and policies without violence?" A rational political system, like science, doesn't try to guarantee good outcomes upfront but makes it as easy as possible to detect and remove bad ones, embodying fallibilism (problems are inevitable) alongside optimism (problems are soluble).
- The chapter closes with case studies of short-lived "mini-enlightenments": Periclean Athens (Pericles' Funeral Oration credited democracy, open discussion, and openness to foreigners with Athens's success, contrasted with militarized, conformist Sparta, which defeated Athens in 404 BCE) and Medici Florence (patronage of Leonardo, Michelangelo, and humanism, ended when the monk Savonarola seized power in 1494 and staged the "Bonfire of the Vanities," burning art and books). Both flared briefly and were snuffed out; Deutsch's guess is that many such mini-enlightenments have recurred throughout history, invisibly, and that only the modern Enlightenment has, so far, been the single exception that did not extinguish itself.
