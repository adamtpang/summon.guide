# Choices

> **Key principle:** The US apportionment problem (how to allocate House seats fairly among states) turns out to be mathematically unsolvable in the way everyone hoped: Balinski and Young proved in 1975 that any apportionment rule that stays within quota is subject to the population paradox. Kenneth Arrow's 1951 impossibility theorem generalizes this into social-choice theory at large: five individually reasonable axioms for combining individual preferences into a group "will of the people" are jointly inconsistent. Deutsch's resolution is not a better voting formula but a reframing: decision-making, individual or collective, is not a process of weighing fixed preferences among fixed options, but a creative process of generating and criticizing explanations, so political institutions should be judged by Popper's criterion, how well they let bad rulers and policies be removed without violence, not by how proportionately they mirror existing opinion.

*Synthesized from Chapter 13 of The Beginning of Infinity by David Deutsch.*

## Key lessons

- The chapter opens with the US apportionment problem: the Constitution requires House seats be allocated to states in proportion to population, but rounding fractional "quotas" to whole numbers keeps producing paradoxes. George Washington's first-ever presidential veto (1792) blocked a reallocation-based rule proposed by Alexander Hamilton; subsequent rules by Jefferson, Webster, and Hamilton were adopted and discarded repeatedly through the 19th and 20th centuries, each eventually producing some "apportionment paradox" (like the Alabama paradox, where adding seats to the House can cause a state to lose one).
- Balinski and Young's Theorem (1975) proved that every apportionment rule that "stays within quota" (never differs from a state's exact proportional share by more than one seat) is necessarily subject to the population paradox (a state whose population grew can lose a seat to one whose population shrank). This is a genuine mathematical impossibility result, not evidence of incompetence or corruption, which is why "eminent mathematicians," including panels that included John von Neumann, gave different, mutually incompatible recommendations across decades without ever accusing each other of mathematical error.
- Kenneth Arrow's impossibility theorem (1951, Nobel Prize 1972) generalizes the problem to all of social-choice theory: five seemingly self-evident axioms for defining a group's "will" from its members' individual preferences (the rule depends only on group members' preferences; no single member's preference automatically becomes the group's regardless of others, the "no-dictator" axiom; unanimous individual preference becomes group preference; a preference doesn't reverse if more members come to agree with it; a group's preference on one issue doesn't change just because members change their minds on an unrelated issue) are jointly inconsistent. No rule can satisfy all five, meaning there is, strictly speaking, no such thing as a coherent "will of the people."
- Deutsch's central philosophical move: Arrow's theorem doesn't refute rational politics, it refutes a mistaken model of decision-making as choosing from a fixed menu of options via a fixed formula (a "weighing" process, the political analogue of inductivism's mistaken picture of theories being "weighed" by evidence). Real decision-making, individual or collective, is a creative process: to choose an option rationally is to choose its explanation, and because good explanations are hard to vary, they can't be smoothly blended, "something halfway between them is usually worse than either of them separately," which is why compromise policies are generally bad policies: nobody actually believes in them, so when they fail, no one learns anything and no explanation gets criticized or abandoned.
- Deutsch reinterprets Arrow's technical term "dictator" (any agent whose preference alone determines an outcome regardless of others) to show it isn't sinister: laws requiring individual consent (against rape, against involuntary surgery, a secret ballot) are all "dictatorships" in Arrow's sense, and are exactly the individual freedoms that make rational decision-making possible. The mistake critics make is wishing that logically impossible conceptions of "fairness" were achievable; Deutsch's reply is that a conception of justice which conflicts with the demands of logic or rationality is, for that very reason, unjust.
- The chapter argues for judging political institutions not by "who should rule?" or by proportional representation, but by Popper's criterion (introduced in Chapter 9): how easily a system lets voters detect and remove bad rulers and policies without violence. On this basis, Deutsch defends plurality ("first-past-the-post") voting over proportional representation, using West Germany's Free Democratic Party (which, despite never exceeding 12.8 percent of the vote, held the "balance of power" and controlled the Foreign Ministry for 29 of 31 years between 1949-1998) and Israel's highly proportional system (where in 1992 right-wing parties won the popular vote but left-wing parties won more seats) as cautionary examples of proportional systems shielding incumbents and small kingmaker parties from real accountability, versus a 1926 Canadian election where the plurality system corrected a seat-vote mismatch within a single subsequent election cycle.
- Deutsch closes by reframing elections themselves: not as a way of "consulting" a wise electorate to discover the correct policy, but as one of many institutions (alongside a free press, courts, and open debate) through which explanations are proposed, tested, and abandoned between elections: for Deutsch, the real substance of democratic decision-making lies not in the checkbox chosen on election day but in the ideas generated in the intervals between elections. He points to genuine historical convergence on moral truths once fiercely contested (that slavery is wrong, that women should have equal rights) as evidence that political disagreement, like scientific disagreement, can genuinely converge toward objective truth over time, even though new disagreements will always arise as knowledge grows, which Deutsch treats as a permanently good thing rather than a problem to be solved away.
