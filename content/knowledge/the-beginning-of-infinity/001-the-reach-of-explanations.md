# The Reach of Explanations

> **Key principle:** Empiricism and inductivism are both false: theories are never read off nature or derived from repeated experience, they are guessed, then tested. What makes an explanation good is not that it is testable but that it is hard to vary without destroying its ability to account for what it explains; because a good explanation's details are locked together by their function, it applies well beyond the observations that prompted it. That extra applicability is "reach," and the unbounded growth of reach made possible by tradition of criticism is one meaning of "the beginning of infinity."

*Synthesized from Chapter 1 of The Beginning of Infinity by David Deutsch.*

## Key lessons

- Empiricism (the idea that knowledge is "derived" from sensory experience, per Locke's "white paper" mind) and inductivism (the idea that theories are obtained by extrapolating repeated observations) are both false. There is no logical process that can derive a general theory from a finite set of experiences; the "principle of induction" that the future resembles the past has never been stated in any usable form, and most scientific knowledge (about stars, the Big Bang, subatomic particles) isn't even about anyone's experiences to extrapolate from.
- Knowledge is created by conjecture: bold guesses formed by rearranging, combining, and criticizing existing ideas, then tested against experience. Experience's role is to help choose between theories that have already been guessed, not to generate them. This two-step process (conjecture, then criticism/testing) was properly understood only with Karl Popper in the mid-twentieth century.
- All observation is theory-laden (a term Deutsch credits to Norwood Russell Hanson): there is no raw, uninterpreted perception. The ancient geocentric theory of a celestial sphere felt directly observed and was repeatedly "confirmed," yet it was false; the senses themselves say nothing, only our fallible interpretations of them do.
- A good explanation is one that is hard to vary while still accounting for what it purports to account for; a bad explanation is one whose details could be swapped for many other details without changing its predictions. Deutsch's running example: the true axis-tilt explanation of the seasons versus the Greek myth of Persephone and Hades. In the myth, the marriage contract, the magic seed, and the identity of the gods could all be replaced by countless equally-good-fitting variants (he constructs an alternative Persephone myth on the spot to prove it), so the myth explains nothing. The axis-tilt theory's details are all load-bearing and interlock with independently testable facts about geometry, heat, and spinning bodies.
- Testability alone cannot be the mark of science, because bad explanations (a prophet's doomsday date, a gambler's lucky-night hunch, easily-varied myths) are often testable too, and when refuted their holders just swap in another equally bad, equally arbitrary explanation without ever making progress. It is only once a theory is a good explanation that testability starts to matter.
- Reach is the ability of an explanation to solve problems beyond the ones it was created to solve. The axis-tilt theory of seasons was devised to explain the sun's changing elevation, yet without modification it also explains why seasons run in antiphase between hemispheres, why the tropics lack seasons, and why the midnight sun occurs near the poles. Reach is not extrapolation and not part of the creative act; it is an intrinsic property of the explanation's content, discovered only after the fact, and the harder an explanation is to vary, the more rigidly its reach is fixed.
- Instrumentalism (the view that science can only predict observations, never describe underlying reality) and relativism (the view that truth is only ever relative to a culture or standard) are both diagnosed as consequences of taking empiricism to its logical end; Deutsch rejects both in favor of realism, the position that the physical world genuinely exists and is knowable.
- The Enlightenment (as Deutsch uses the term through the book) was not merely a rejection of authority, which has happened many times in history with little lasting benefit; what made it different, and what powered the scientific revolution, was that it combined that rejection with a sustained tradition of criticism, in which conjectures could be inherited and improved upon by others rather than dying with their originator. Fallibilism, the recognition that no source of knowledge is authoritative and that even our best current explanations likely contain undetected errors, is what makes open-ended progress possible rather than something to despair over.
