# Artificial Creativity

> **Key principle:** Alan Turing correctly argued AI must in principle be possible, since a universal computer is a universal simulator, and proposed the Turing test as a way to detect it. But the test is flawed because it is rooted in the same empiricist, behaviourist mistake as instrumentalism: judging only outward behavior rather than requiring an explanation of how the candidate's utterances were actually produced. Fifty-eight years of chatbot research produced no genuine progress toward thinking machines because the field lacks a theory of creativity, and so-called "artificial evolution" experiments likely just relocate the programmer's own creativity rather than generating new knowledge.

*Synthesized from Chapter 7 of The Beginning of Infinity by David Deutsch.*

## Key lessons

- Alan Turing (1950 paper "Computing Machinery and Intelligence") proposed that a machine's thinking ability could be tested by having a human judge fail to distinguish it from a real human in text conversation, the Turing test, and predicted machines would be thinking by the year 2000. Deutsch credits Turing as uniquely understanding, unlike Babbage and Lovelace, that AI must in principle be achievable because a universal computer is a universal simulator of any physical process, including a brain.
- Joseph Weizenbaum's 1966 program Eliza (simulating a psychotherapist by pattern-matching keywords into templates) fooled many users despite being simple, and even after Douglas Hofstadter was hoaxed in 1983 into believing a student pretending to be a chatbot was a real AI program, he later admitted astonishment at how readily he had been willing to believe real intelligence had been built into the program. Deutsch's diagnosis: fifty-eight years after Eliza (including Loebner Prize-winning entries like "Elbot"), chatbot technology had made literally zero progress toward genuine thought, only better keyword-matching tricks, while every other area of computing advanced enormously in the same period.
- Deutsch argues the lack of progress is not a hardware problem: today's computers vastly exceed the memory and speed Turing estimated would suffice, yet "it can no more think than Turing's slide rule could." If anyone actually knew how to write a thinking program, there would be no need to wait for faster hardware to test it, because a good explanation of how the program creates knowledge would itself be sufficient evidence that it is a genuine AI, no Turing test needed.
- The chapter uses qualia (the subjective character of a sensation, such as what "seeing blue" is actually like) as an example of a currently unsolved, undescribable, unpredictable problem that a full theory of mind will eventually have to explain. Deutsch criticizes Daniel Dennett's view (in Consciousness Explained) that qualia don't exist as merely a mistaken belief in disguise, calling it a bad explanation on the same logic as denying anything else inconvenient, "anything at all could be denied by that method."
- Deutsch's core diagnosis of the Turing test's flaw: it is rooted in behaviourism (which he calls "instrumentalism applied to psychology," judging only stimulus-response behavior, never underlying explanation). What actually distinguishes an AI from a clever trick is not its outward behavior but who created the knowledge behind its utterances, the programmer in advance, or the program itself in real time. A joke from a chatbot is evidence of thought only if there's a good explanation of how the program itself, not the programmer, generated it.
- Because Deutsch expects human-level abilities (intelligence, consciousness, qualia) to have arrived together "in one jump to universality," he predicts genuine AI, when achieved, will be a universal explainer, essentially a person, not a specialized behavior-imitator; getting better at faking thought (better chatbots) is not progress toward actual thought, any more than a more convincing forgery is progress toward the real painting.
- "Artificial evolution" experiments (evolutionary algorithms that iteratively improve, say, a walking robot's control program by random variation and selection against a fitness test) are argued to likely not be creating new knowledge at all, only re-expressing knowledge the programmer already built into the high-level language/subroutines they designed; Deutsch's proposed decisive test would be to strip out that human-designed language entirely (replace a working robot's program with literal random bit-mutations) and see whether performance ever improves through mutation and selection alone, predicting it would not.
- The chapter's closing diagnosis: both AI and artificial evolution have failed not from lack of trying or lack of computing power, but because of two genuinely unsolved scientific/philosophical problems, we don't understand how creativity works, and we don't understand what kind of universality the DNA replication system achieved, that let it go from specifying only early bacteria to specifying dinosaurs and human brains. Once creativity is understood, Deutsch predicts, programming it will not be difficult.
