# A Dream of Socrates

> **Key principle:** In an invented dialogue (explicitly not a historical reconstruction), the god Hermes appears to a dreaming Socrates and leads him, mostly by questioning, to twentieth-century Popperian epistemology dressed in ancient language: certainty ("justified belief") is unattainable and not even desirable, while objective knowledge is attainable through the same single process in every case, guesswork (conjecture) followed by criticism, whether the guess comes from the senses, from another person, from a dream, or supposedly from a god. Along the way the dialogue identifies the deep difference between Athens and Sparta: Athens seeks improvement, Sparta seeks stasis, and Sparta's traditions of holding its own ways immune from criticism form an inescapable trap, since criticism is the only path to self-correction.

*Synthesized from Chapter 10 of The Beginning of Infinity by David Deutsch.*

## Key lessons

- The setup: Socrates and Chaerephon have asked the Oracle at Delphi who the wisest man in the world is, and received only the answer "no one is wiser than Socrates." Disappointed, Socrates falls asleep and is visited in a dream by a god (whom he first guesses is Apollo, then correctly infers is Hermes, "god of knowledge, and of messages, and of information flow," since Hermes explicitly "reveals no facts," only makes arguments).
- The dialogue's central move is to show that "justified belief," certainty backed by some authoritative source, is a chimera: unattainable except through self-deception, unnecessary, and not even desired by the wisest people. Socrates already implicitly knows this (he says he can be sure of nothing and finds the idea of being "perfectly secure in one's beliefs" boring), and Hermes's method throughout is to draw out what Socrates already half-knows rather than hand down new facts, illustrating that a real revelation from an authority is worthless if it only tells you what you could reason out yourself.
- Hermes leads Socrates to see that even sensory experience is not a privileged, "direct" channel to reality: what is "easy to see" turns out to require prior knowledge and interpretation at every step (Socrates, pressed, admits he cannot even be sure whether he sees the inside of his own closed eyelids). The conclusion is that all knowledge, whether from the senses, from a conversation, from a dream, or purportedly from a god, comes from within, via guesswork, and is only ever provisionally accepted after surviving criticism; this is why it "makes no difference" to Socrates's new understanding whether Hermes is real or only a dream.
- The dialogue quotes the real historical philosopher Xenophanes (translated by Popper): "for certain truth, no man has known it... For all is but a woven web of guesses," used to state, in ancient language, exactly Deutsch's Chapter 1 fallibilism: objective knowledge is attainable, but certainty is not, and all human knowledge is conjecture refined by criticism.
- Hermes prompts Socrates to imagine a hypothetical "Spartan Socrates" who considers Athens decadent and Sparta virtuous, and shows that this comparison cannot be resolved by mere difference of perspective: since Sparta's core value is to keep its ways unchanged, if the Spartan view were right, Sparta's original way of life would have had to already be perfect at its founding, an absurd claim given how little Sparta has achieved outside warfare, whereas Athens's belief that improvement is possible carries no such requirement of prior perfection.
- The dialogue names the deepest difference between the two cities: Athens's overarching concern is improvement, Sparta's is stasis. This single distinction is shown to explain many surface differences (Sparta has no philosophers or living poets it honors, since both represent change; its harsh education exists to prevent children from questioning anything). Critically, Sparta's ban on debate, philosophy, and persuasion is diagnosed as an unusually self-perpetuating kind of mistake, one that guarantees it can never be corrected, because it disables the very mechanism, criticism and persuasion, through which any society could correct its own mistakes (Athens, by contrast, retains that mechanism even when individual Athenians, like politicians or sophists who think they already have all the answers, get things wrong).
- Socrates speculates that "the moral imperative not to destroy the means of correcting mistakes" might be the one moral imperative from which all others follow, a line Deutsch has Hermes decline to confirm or deny (since Hermes "reveals no facts"), but which functions as the chapter's clearest thesis statement, and previews Deutsch's later distinction (developed in Chapter 15) between static and dynamic societies.
- Deutsch closes the chapter (outside the dialogue) by explaining that this is deliberate historical fiction, not a claim about what Socrates actually believed, and uses the real "Socratic problem" (we only know Socrates through Plato's unreliable retelling, dramatized in the dialogue itself as the young Plato character increasingly misinterprets what Socrates says) to make a broader point: communication of ideas is always fallible guesswork on both ends, yet scientific theories are nonetheless reliably transmitted across generations, not because of careful fidelity to original sources (physicists rarely read Einstein's own papers), but because a good explanation is hard to vary, so independent learners who seek understanding through criticism converge on the same theory regardless of the messenger, because the way people converge with each other is by each independently converging on the truth.
