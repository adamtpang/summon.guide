# Why are Flowers Beautiful?

> **Key principle:** Just as Chapter 5 argued that moral right and wrong are objectively real because they appear ineliminably in our best explanations, this chapter argues the same for beauty. The evidence is flowers: because flower genes had to signal reliably to insect genes across a gap with no shared evolutionary history to draw on (unlike mate-signaling within a species), the only workable solution was a hard-to-forge, hard-to-vary design, which is to say, genuinely objective beauty, not a parochial code. That is why flowers, uniquely among the products of biological evolution (aside from rare accidents like the peacock's tail), reliably strike humans as beautiful even though humans never co-evolved with them at all.

*Synthesized from Chapter 14 of The Beginning of Infinity by David Deutsch.*

## Key lessons

- The chapter opens by connecting a line from Peter Shaffer's play Amadeus about Mozart's music ("displace one note and there would be diminishment") to John Archibald Wheeler's description (quoted at the very start of the book) of a hoped-for unified physics theory as "so simple, so beautiful... how could it have been otherwise." Both describe the same attribute Deutsch has used throughout the book for good explanations: being hard to vary while still doing the job. Wheeler explicitly calls this attribute beautiful.
- Deutsch rejects aesthetic relativism ("beauty is in the eye of the beholder") as a relic of empiricism, the same philosophical move that wrongly denies objective moral or philosophical knowledge can exist (Chapter 5). He distinguishes elegance, the specific form of beauty found in explanations, mathematical proofs, and theories, from beauty generally, and notes elegance is not identical to truth or goodness of an explanation (citing Thomas Huxley's remark about "the great tragedy of Science, the slaying of a beautiful hypothesis by an ugly fact," referring to the refutation of spontaneous generation), but that deep truths are so often elegant that elegance is a useful heuristic in the search for them.
- Deutsch draws a parallel between scientific and artistic creativity: both Beethoven's manuscripts (full of discarded drafts) and a physicist's wastebasket full of failed derivations reflect the same underlying process of conjecture and criticism, and prodigies who seem to create without visible effort (Mozart, the mathematician Srinivasa Ramanujan) must, given what we know about knowledge-creation, still be doing the trial and error, just invisibly, inside their own minds.
- The chapter's central argument is the flower case study. Flowering plants had to solve a communication problem: signal insects reliably enough to be recognized, while being hard for other, non-nectar-producing flower species to counterfeit. Because flowers and insects are different species with no shared genetic starting point to build codes on (unlike within-species mate selection, which can build on existing shared genetic knowledge, as with giraffe neck length), Deutsch argues the only viable solution to that cross-species signaling problem was to converge on objective, hard-to-vary beauty, standards recognizable independent of any particular species' parochial preferences.
- This explains why humans, despite never having co-evolved with flowers at all, still reliably find flowers beautiful (unlike almost every other product of biological evolution, where we typically don't share another species' criteria of attractiveness; a beetle attractive to another beetle usually looks nondescript or hideous to us). Deutsch rules out simpler explanations, that flowers are just symmetrical, colorful, or high-contrast, by noting white and asymmetrical flowers (like orchids) are still perceived as beautiful, while symmetrical, high-contrast things like spiders are not.
- Deutsch extends the theory to explain why humans create art at all: a human mind carries far more unique, individually-created knowledge than the genome of any species, so communicating something genuinely new between two humans is structurally like the flower-insect problem, signaling across a gap with no shared code to fall back on. This is why human artists, like flower genes, are pushed toward objective beauty rather than merely parochial, cultural signaling when they want to communicate something truly new.
- The chapter distinguishes two kinds of beauty and two kinds of artistic problem: "applied" beauty (parochial, solves a signaling or comfort problem, like a computer's user-interface design or an advertising jingle) versus "pure" beauical (created for its own sake, analogous to pure scientific research, seeking objective, universal truth). Deutsch rejects both the "art as skill" and "art as self-expression" theories of art on the same grounds he rejects instrumentalism and relativism in science: they misconceive artistic progress as either a finite, perfectible technical skill or as merely expressing something already present, rather than as the creation of genuinely new objective knowledge.
- The chapter closes with optimism about art's future paralleling the book's argument about science: since beauty is objective and the growth of aesthetic knowledge is unbounded, future art (and future senses and qualia, designed rather than merely evolved) could create kinds of beauty as inconceivable to us now as our art would be to a Stone Age ancestor, extending philosopher Thomas Nagel's question "what is it like to be a bat?" into a prediction that future technological art, not philosophy, may eventually let people directly experience such alien qualia.
