# The Reality of Abstractions

> **Key principle:** Modern physics denies the existence of everyday entities like the "force" of gravity, showing that reality is stranger than intuition, yet at the same time high-level, emergent phenomena, and even purely abstract ones like prime numbers, causation, and moral right and wrong, are genuinely real, because they are indispensable to our best explanations of the world. Reductionism (the view that only microphysical explanations are truly fundamental) and holism (its mirror-image, that only wholes explain parts) are both bad philosophy: any level of emergence can be where the real explanatory work happens.

*Synthesized from Chapter 5 of The Beginning of Infinity by David Deutsch.*

## Key lessons

- Einstein's general relativity denies that a "force" of gravity exists at all (an object in a gravitational field is simply following the straightest path through curved spacetime), illustrating Deutsch's criterion for reality: if an entity is invoked by our best available explanation, we must regard it as real, and if our best explanation denies it, we must stop assuming it exists, no matter how intuitive it feels.
- Emergence is the phenomenon by which vast numbers of intractable low-level interactions (e.g., every water molecule in a boiling kettle) resolve into a smaller set of high-level regularities (boiling point, volume) that can be explained almost entirely in terms of each other, without reference to the atomic level. Deutsch calls this "quasi-autonomous" and argues emergent levels are not a lesser or approximate form of explanation, they are often where genuine, irreducible explanatory content lives.
- Reductionism (science only ever properly explains by breaking things into components) and holism (only wholes properly explain parts) are both rejected as bad philosophy. Deutsch's own worked example, reused from The Fabric of Reality: explaining why a specific copper atom sits at the tip of Winston Churchill's nose on his Parliament Square statue requires citing his leadership, the Allied victory, and the custom of erecting statues, not a particle-by-particle physical trajectory, which would predict the atom's location without ever explaining it.
- Douglas Hofstadter's domino-computer thought experiment (from I Am a Strange Loop) is presented as a decisive argument against reductionism: a network of spring-loaded dominoes computing whether 641 is prime has one domino that never falls, and the true explanation for why it never falls is "because 641 is a prime number," not an exhaustive chain of "because its neighbor never fell." Deutsch credits Hofstadter's own argument but criticizes Hofstadter for abandoning it later in the same book by concluding, following Daniel Dennett, that the conscious "I" is an illusion because physics alone determines behavior; Deutsch counters that physical laws don't "push" anything either, they only explain and predict, and multiple true explanations can coexist at different levels of emergence without contradiction.
- Successive scientific theories of the same phenomenon (Kepler's ellipses, Newton's gravitational force, Einstein's curved spacetime) often deny the very entities their predecessor used to explain planetary motion, which some cite as evidence for instrumentalism (successive theories aren't cumulative truth, just improving predictions). Deutsch rejects this: each theory captured something real about the previous one (Newton's force approximated Einstein's spacetime curvature to high accuracy), so a theory's truth "lives on" in its successor even when its ontology is swept away, in Einstein's own words theories can "live on as a limiting case."
- Abstract objects like numbers and the infinite set of natural numbers are argued to be genuinely real by the same criterion as everything else: they are indispensable to our best explanations (the prime-number explanation of the domino network), and there is no mystery about how we can know about them, since our knowledge of abstractions comes from conjecture and criticism just like all other knowledge, not from any special faculty or supernatural source (contra Plato, who concluded knowledge of perfect geometric forms had to be supernatural because it plainly wasn't derived from observation).
- The chapter extends this same argument to morality: the empiricist maxim "you can't derive an ought from an is" (a paraphrase of David Hume) is true but is used to wrongly conclude morality can't be a matter of reason at all. Deutsch's counter is that you can't derive a factual scientific theory from an "is" either, since science doesn't work by derivation, it works by conjecture and criticism, and moral explanations are open to exactly the same kind of critical, explanatory reasoning. Because the distinction between right and wrong appears ineliminably in our best explanations of what to do, it must be regarded as objectively real, just as beauty is (a claim developed further in Chapter 14).
- The chapter closes by tying reach (Chapter 1) to abstraction: a theory's reach into the world of abstractions is a property of the knowledge itself, not of the brain that happens to hold it, and people themselves are described as abstractions with a unique kind of infinite reach, the ability to understand explanations, which sets up the chapter's transition into the next chapter's topic of universality.
