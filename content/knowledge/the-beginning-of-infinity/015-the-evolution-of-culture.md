# The Evolution of Culture

> **Key principle:** A culture is a set of shared memes (replicating ideas), and cultural evolution, while governed by the same underlying logic as biological evolution (variation and selection of replicators), works through mechanisms utterly unlike genetics: every meme must be expressed as behavior to replicate, alternating between a brain-memory form and a behavior form. This produces two fundamentally different survival strategies. Rational memes rely on holders' critical faculties and evolve toward deep, widely useful truth; anti-rational memes survive by disabling those faculties. A static society is one built almost entirely from anti-rational memes, changing too slowly for its members to notice, and surviving by systematically extinguishing individual creativity; the modern West is history's only long-lived dynamic (rational-meme-dominated) society, and remains, even now, in an unstable transition where the two kinds of meme actively sabotage each other's replication.

*Synthesized from Chapter 15 of The Beginning of Infinity by David Deutsch.*

## Key lessons

- Deutsch defines culture as a set of shared ideas (memes, in Richard Dawkins's term, introduced in Chapter 4) that cause their holders to behave alike; much cultural content is inexplicit (like grammatical rules native speakers can enact perfectly without being able to state), and because ideas are never transmitted with perfect fidelity, no two people ever hold precisely the same version of a cultural meme, which is why movements schism after their founders die.
- Deutsch rejects the historical analogies between cultural and biological evolution used by Marx (who compared class struggle to inter-species competition, misreading Darwin, since real biological competition is between gene variants within a species, not between species) and by Nazism ("survival of the fittest" misapplied to justify violence): arguments by superficial analogy are fallacies, and treating culture as biology-like erases the high-level distinctions (creativity, choice, moral right and wrong) essential to understanding the human condition.
- The key structural difference between genes and memes: a gene is copied automatically and indiscriminately as a single physical strand, and can persist for generations without ever being "expressed." A meme, by contrast, must be expressed as behavior every single time it replicates (since a meme cannot be directly downloaded from one brain to another), so it exists alternately in two different physical forms, brain-memory and behavior, and must survive two independent selection events every generation: causing its current holder to enact it, and causing that behavior to be adopted by a new recipient. This is why most attempts to found a new religion, ideology, or habit of thought fail even when the founders try hard to propagate them.
- Static societies are defined as societies that change on a timescale longer than their members can notice; Deutsch argues the primary mechanism keeping a society static cannot be mere enforcement of tradition (since minor variations and drift are unavoidable), it must be disabling the source of new ideas itself, human creativity, through child-rearing that teaches people to value only conformity to the society's existing memes and to invest their sense of self-worth entirely in enacting them.
- Deutsch introduces the crucial distinction between rational memes (which rely on and require the holder's ongoing critical faculties to keep replicating, and therefore, under repeated criticism across changing circumstances, evolve toward genuinely deep, widely useful truths, his example is Newton's laws, useful for building cathedrals, bridges, and artillery alike) and anti-rational memes (which replicate precisely by disabling the holder's critical faculties, evolving instead toward exploiting genuine, deep vulnerabilities of the human mind while their overt content need not be true at all, and often works better if false, since a fictional threat like a hobgoblin is unconstrained by any real, finite, defeatable danger).
- Mixing the two strategies fails: a meme that is both true and helpful but also disables criticism will replicate less faithfully (errors in it can't be corrected), and a meme that requires belief in its own usefulness without being genuinely useful risks rejection. This is why rational memes thrive in dynamic societies (whose ongoing criticism weeds out even slightly-less-true variants) and anti-rational memes thrive in static societies (where their host society's suppression of criticism is exactly what they need).
- The West is described as the only known long-lived dynamic society in history, and its Enlightenment transition (which Deutsch traces to Galileo's philosophy, becoming irreversible with Newton's success) remains incomplete: many domains (gender-role expectations, clothing norms, much of formal education, which Deutsch argues still transmits knowledge "in an anaemic and instrumental way" via rote learning) still run on anti-rational memes, and because rational and anti-rational memes actively impede each other's replication (one needs critical thinking, the other needs its absence), the West's culture is in an inherently unstable transitional state, producing phenomena like anti-rational subcultures (bigotry, pseudoscience) persisting within an otherwise dynamic society.
- Deutsch criticizes Susan Blackmore's The Meme Machine for downplaying individual creativity in favor of pure meme-evolution explanations of progress (denying "the myth of the heroic inventor" and any objective sense of progress), arguing this description, while roughly true of static societies (where change happens blindly, the way Deutsch speculates jokes might evolve with no single author), mistakes the mechanism of a dynamic society, where innovations are typically the product of real individual creativity, evolution occurring within a single mind through conjecture and criticism, not mindless drift across a population. The chapter closes with practical advice for detecting anti-rational memes in oneself: suspicion is warranted whenever a behavior is narrowly defined, faithfully repeated, persists even after its stated justification disappears, thwarts one's own goals, or is defended with bad explanations like "because I say so" or "it never did me any harm."
