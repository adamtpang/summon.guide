# Unsustainable

> **Key principle:** Easter Island's collapsed civilization is popularly cited (notably by David Attenborough) as a miniature warning for Spaceship Earth, but Deutsch, siding with Jacob Bronowski's original reading of the same statues, argues the real lesson is the opposite: the islanders' static society failed not because it mismanaged its forest "resource" but because, like every static society, it was structurally incapable of solving whatever new problem eventually arose. "Sustainability" is an ambiguous, even dangerous, goal, since it can mean either providing what people need or preventing change, and only continuous, creative, inherently unsustainable progress, each solution creating the next problem, is actually sustainable in the long run.

*Synthesized from Chapter 17 of The Beginning of Infinity by David Deutsch.*

## Key lessons

- Deutsch contrasts two documentary treatments of the same Easter Island statues: Jacob Bronowski's The Ascent of Man (1973), which read them as evidence of failure ("watching the sun and the stars go overhead without ever trying to understand them"), a civilization that never took the first step toward rational knowledge, versus David Attenborough's The State of the Planet (2000), which used them as a direct warning that our "miniature world," like Easter Island's, could collapse from unsustainable resource use. Deutsch sides explicitly with Bronowski.
- The statues themselves are read as evidence for a static society, not just of one: hundreds were built, all alike, over centuries, and as disaster loomed the islanders diverted ever more effort into building bigger monuments to their ancestors rather than solving their actual problem, "the largest, are in various stages of completion" on roads made of the very trees they needed.
- Deutsch's key semantic move: "sustain" is dangerously ambiguous between "provide someone with what they need" and "prevent things from changing," and these are often nearly opposite. Curing a childhood disease or inventing more efficient farming is, by design, unsustainable, it changes population, labor needs, and the whole subsequent way of life, which is exactly why modern Britain, with a harsher climate than subtropical Easter Island, now supports several times the population density at a vastly higher standard of living, without the forests Easter Island lost.
- Deutsch rejects the broader Spaceship-Earth-style lesson people draw from Easter Island as nearly contentless: the specific claim that civilization depends on good forest management has almost no reach beyond forestry, and the vaguer claim that survival depends on good resource management can explain anything after the fact (his mocking example: attributing Julius Caesar's assassination to poor management of the iron in his body, i.e., the knife). The real explanatory question is never what resource was mismanaged but why the society failed to correct the resulting problem, since problems are inevitable, what matters is whether they're solved.
- The chapter directly attacks Jared Diamond's Guns, Germs and Steel (and, before Diamond, Friedrich Engels writing from Marx's notes) for proposing biogeography as the "ultimate explanation" of history, using llamas as the test case: Diamond attributes the absence of pre-Columbian American civilizations partly to llamas being confined to the Andes, but Deutsch points out this just relocates the puzzle (why didn't traders, who did travel between South and Central America, ever bring llamas north?) and argues the real answer must be about ideas and outlook, not geography, since Polynesian traders crossed far more formidable ocean barriers carrying livestock. Deutsch constructs several counterfactuals (Andean "war llamas," domesticated Ice Age megafauna) to show how easily the same events could have generated the opposite "ultimate explanation."
- Deutsch's broader claim: from the dawn of civilization onward, both the main opportunities for progress and the main obstacles to it have consisted of ideas, and ideas alone, so biogeographical factors can only ever affect the details of history, not its broad sweep, which is why, for instance, biogeography has nothing to say about why Western democracies rather than the Soviet system won the Cold War (the explanation, in Deutsch's view, is simply that Soviet ideology was false).
- The chapter revisits real failed pessimistic prophecies as case studies in the prophecy-versus-prediction fallacy (Chapter 9): population scientist Paul Ehrlich's 1971 lecture (attended by Deutsch as a student) forecasting mass starvation "in ten years, twenty at best" from overpopulation and resource depletion, and a graduate student's early-1970s argument that color television was doomed because of europium scarcity, both failed because they assumed only already-existing knowledge would ever be applied, missing that people are problem-solvers who create new, unforeseeable solutions (liquid-crystal displays needing no europium at all).
- Deutsch applies the same framework to climate change, treating it as a genuine, physics-based prediction (rising CO2 must eventually raise temperatures, that much is uncontroversial) entangled with pure prophecy (economic forecasts of how humanity will respond over a century, which cannot account for unforeseeable future knowledge, the same mistake Ehrlich made). His policy conclusion: since problems are inevitable and some will be unforeseeable, delay and prevention tactics (emissions reduction) are necessary but cannot be the primary strategy; the primary strategy must be building the wealth, science, and technological capacity to intervene and fix problems after they arise unforeseen, since "only progress is sustainable," and true sustainability is not a fixed lifestyle but an open-ended process where each solution is unsustainable until redeemed by the next.
