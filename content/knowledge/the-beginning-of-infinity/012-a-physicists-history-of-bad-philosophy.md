# A Physicist's History of Bad Philosophy

> **Key principle:** Deutsch defines bad philosophy as philosophy that is not merely false but actively prevents the growth of other knowledge by holding itself immune from criticism. He traces this through quantum theory's Copenhagen interpretation (which treated the theory as pure prediction with no reality behind it, using vague talk of "complementarity" to dodge the question of what is actually happening), through the broader arc of positivism, logical positivism, Wittgenstein, and postmodernism, and shows the same move, splitting a theory into an explanationless predictive core plus an arbitrary "interpretation," has produced bad, dehumanizing pseudo-science in fields from happiness research to animal-welfare studies.

*Synthesized from Chapter 12 of The Beginning of Infinity by David Deutsch.*

## Key lessons

- The chapter opens with a dialogue (between "Reader" and "Deutsch") reprising the interference-experiment argument from Chapter 11: the Mach-Zehnder interferometer alone is enough to establish, without appeal to the full theory, that unseen parallel histories genuinely exist and affect outcomes, since only the presence of both paths can explain the deterministic result.
- Deutsch narrates the historical divergence between Heisenberg's matrix-based and Schrodinger's wave-based formulations of quantum theory (1925-1927), both counter-intuitive, both later shown (with hindsight) to be describing multiple simultaneous histories, and both patched with the same ad hoc "rule of thumb": when a measurement is made, all histories but one cease to exist, chosen randomly with probability equal to their measure. Rather than pursuing why that rule worked, most of the physics community retreated into instrumentalism, the "shut-up-and-calculate" approach that treats quantum theory as pure prediction with no claim about reality.
- Niels Bohr's Copenhagen interpretation is presented as the paradigm case of bad philosophy in science: it combined instrumentalism with deliberate ambiguity (denying phenomena exist objectively while insisting observation reveals "relationships"), introduced the "principle of complementarity" (physical variables can only be discussed in "classical language," and only for what has just been measured), and used the equivocation "particle-wave duality" to dodge questions about the unobserved path in interference experiments. Deutsch notes the delicious irony that in one narrow multiversal sense the "wave and particle at once" description is literally true, but that is not what Bohr's followers meant by it, and later versions implied that human consciousness itself causes quantum "potentialities" to become "actual."
- Real historical dissenters are named: Einstein never accepted the Copenhagen interpretation; David Bohm built an alternative Deutsch calls "the multiverse theory in heavy disguise"; and Erwin Schrodinger himself, in a 1952 Dublin lecture, made what Deutsch identifies as the earliest known reference to the multiverse, joking his own idea (that his equation's different histories "all really happen simultaneously") might "seem lunatic." Hugh Everett published the full many-worlds theory five years later, in 1957, and it remains a minority view among physicists to this day.
- Deutsch's account of "because I say so" as a distilled model of bad philosophy: it explains anything (bad explanation), addresses only who said something rather than what was said, substitutes a demand for justification for a genuine explanation (the justified-true-belief chimera), conflates ideas with power, and claims immunity from criticism, all five hallmarks of bad philosophy in one child's phrase.
- Deutsch traces a genealogy of worsening bad philosophy after the Enlightenment (paradoxically, since the Enlightenment also produced far more good philosophy): empiricism (merely false, initially harmless since scientists ignored it in practice) degenerated into positivism (physicist Ernst Mach's denial that atoms or unobservable spacetime exist, which contributed to physicist Ludwig Boltzmann's despair and possible suicide), then logical positivism (statements not verifiable by observation are meaningless, a claim that is self-refuting since it cannot itself be verified), then Wittgenstein declaring all philosophy including his own meaningless, then postmodernism/deconstructionism/structuralism (all ideas are merely arbitrary "narratives" endorsed by a group, a claim Deutsch notes is self-undermining in exactly the same way as logical positivism).
- The chapter's central diagnostic tool: splitting a scientific theory into an "explanationless" predictive core plus a separate, supposedly optional "interpretation" is incoherent, because recognizing when a rule of thumb applies always requires explanation (as with conjuring tricks in Chapter 1). Deutsch illustrates the absurdity with a deliberately silly example: a museum visitor-count study that, if it refuses to build in explanatory assumptions about the building's doors, could "scientifically" report evidence of spontaneous human teleportation whenever counting errors produce a discrepancy between entry and exit counts.
- Deutsch applies the same critique to real behavioral science: "happiness heritability" studies cannot actually measure happiness (only a proxy, like a self-rated checkbox), so their finding that happiness is "50 percent genetically determined" smuggles in a bad-philosophy leap from statistical correlation to a pessimistic, dehumanizing claim that knowledge-creation (understanding and changing one's circumstances) cannot affect that "genetically fixed" portion, and animal-welfare "stress hormone" studies of stag hunting are shown to be equally unable to resolve the real underlying question (whether animals experience qualia) without a genuine explanatory theory of consciousness. The chapter's final claim is that bad philosophy, because it holds itself immune from argument, cannot be defeated by logic alone, only by continued progress making it harder to believe: Deutsch defines bad philosophy as philosophy that denies progress is possible or desirable, which is exactly why ongoing progress is the one effective way of opposing it.
