# The Spark

> **Key principle:** Deutsch dismantles two "truisms" of scientifically-minded culture. The Principle of Mediocrity (there is nothing significant about humans) and the Spaceship Earth metaphor (the biosphere is a fragile life-support system humans are looting) both turn out to be factually wrong and philosophically self-defeating. The real situation is that the biosphere is actually hostile to unprotected human life, that all our "life support" is knowledge we built ourselves, and that because people can create explanatory knowledge without limit, they are universal constructors whose reach is bounded only by the laws of physics, not by their evolutionary origin. Hence problems are inevitable, but no problem is insoluble in principle.

*Synthesized from Chapter 3 of The Beginning of Infinity by David Deutsch.*

## Key lessons

- Pre-Enlightenment thought was anthropocentric in two linked ways: explanations invoked human-like gods with intentions (winter caused by a god's sadness), and geocentrism put humans at the physical center of the universe. Modern physics and biology have eliminated both, replacing purposeful people with impersonal laws, particles, and evolution by natural selection.
- Deutsch defines parochialism as mistaking a quirk of one's own local environment or perspective for a universal, objective feature of reality (mistaking the rotation of the night sky for a property of the stars, or a rule of thumb like "the sun rises every 24 hours" for a law). Anthropocentrism is one species of parochialism, not the whole genus.
- The Principle of Mediocrity ("there is nothing significant about humans," in Stephen Hawking's phrase, humans as "chemical scum") is shown to be false even on narrow physical grounds: humans are not typical matter (85 percent of matter is dark matter we can't touch), not typical in density (concentrations like planets and stars are rare, the universe is mostly cold, dark, near-perfect vacuum), and the coldest, darkest known places in the universe are physics labs on Earth, not intergalactic space.
- The Spaceship Earth metaphor (the biosphere as a fragile life-support system whose passengers, humans, are recklessly overloading it) is shown to be false too: the primeval biosphere, including the Great Rift Valley where humans evolved, was a death trap, not a life-support system, killing most individuals of most species young through starvation, predation, and disease; genuine human life support (clothes, farms, medicine, sewage systems) was built by humans using knowledge, not provided by the biosphere.
- Deutsch inverts Richard Dawkins's "queerer than we can suppose" argument (that human brains, being evolved for a parochial ancestral environment, must have limited, parochial reach). His counter: explanatory knowledge, unlike genetically encoded rules of thumb, is not tied to the environment it was created in. Anything not forbidden by the laws of physics is achievable given the right knowledge, which is why a moon colony or a self-sufficient outpost drifting in the coldest, emptiest intergalactic space is, in principle, buildable using only local hydrogen, transmutation, and starlight for evidence.
- People are defined in this chapter as "entities that can create explanatory knowledge," and are described as universal constructors, in contrast to every other organism, which functions only as a highly specialized constructor limited to a narrow range of hospitable environments dictated by its genes (e.g., primates die without dietary vitamin C because synthesizing it isn't in their genetic knowledge, while humans can manufacture it anywhere).
- The chapter's thought experiment: a "typical" cubic region of intergalactic space, empty and dark by human standards, still contains over a billion tonnes of matter (mostly hydrogen), enough energy via nuclear fusion, and enough starlight and radiation as evidence to support a self-sufficient civilization doing real science, if it had the right knowledge. This shows knowledge-friendliness, not resource scarcity, is what actually separates a dead environment from a living one; "if we want to, we could be that spark."
- The chapter closes with two maxims Deutsch says are worth carving in stone: "problems are inevitable" and "problems are soluble." Fallibilism guarantees new problems will always arise from correcting old errors (there can be no ultimate, unimprovable explanation), but because human reach is bounded only by physical law, not biology, every problem is solvable in principle given the right knowledge, distinguishing his "British" Enlightenment (evolutionary, fallibilist, favoring institutions for correcting error) from the "Continental" Enlightenment's pursuit of a final, perfected utopian state.
