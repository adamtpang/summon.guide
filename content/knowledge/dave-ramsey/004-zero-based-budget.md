## Key lessons

- The article (by George Kamel) defines a zero-based budget as one where income minus planned expenses equals zero. It clarifies that this does not mean an empty bank account, and suggests keeping a buffer of roughly $100 to $300 in checking.
- It credits the idea to Peter Pyhrr, a Texas Instruments manager, in the early 1970s, and notes that President Jimmy Carter tried to bring it to the federal government.
- Five steps: list monthly income, list expenses, subtract until the result is zero, track spending all month, and make a new budget before each month begins.
- Expenses are listed in a set order: giving (10% of income is recommended), then the "Four Walls" of food, utilities, shelter, and transportation, then saving, then everything else. A person with debt and a $1,000 starter fund is told to send the savings line to the debt snowball instead.
- For irregular income, the article advises planning on the lowest-earning month from recent bank statements and adjusting upward if more comes in.
- If money is left over, it goes to the current Baby Step. If expenses exceed income, the article says to cut categories (restaurants first) and then raise income with side work or selling things.
- Tracking means logging every transaction to a category. Overspending in one category is fixed by moving money from another so the total still equals zero. A miscellaneous line is recommended for surprises.
- Its sample budget for a $5,000 monthly income includes $500 giving, $1,250 housing, $650 food, $850 insurance, and $1,110 toward debt, summing to exactly $5,000.
- The article lists its own drawbacks: the method takes more time than others, needs discipline in the first few months, can feel restrictive, and can cause friction between spouses over category amounts.
- It rejects the 50/30/20 rule, the 60% solution, and reverse budgeting, mainly because fixed percentages do not put debt payoff first. It treats cash envelopes as a tool that fits inside a zero-based budget.
- Limitation (not from the article, mainstream disagreement): mainstream sources do not agree that this is the single best method. NerdWallet, for example, recommends the 50/30/20 split as a guide for dividing a budget, the same rule this article dismisses, and simpler methods may suit people who will not track every transaction. The article also promotes Ramsey's own budgeting app; this note does not endorse any product.
