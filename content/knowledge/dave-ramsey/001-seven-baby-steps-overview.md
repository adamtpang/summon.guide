## Key lessons

- Ramsey's organization lists the steps in a set order: save $1,000, pay off all debt except the house with the debt snowball, save 3 to 6 months of expenses, invest 15% of household income for retirement, save for children's college, pay off the home early, then build wealth and give.
- The page frames the plan as getting rid of debt rather than managing it, and says the same seven steps have been taught for about 30 years.
- Step 1 exists because ordinary surprises (a broken window, a flat tire) turn into new debt when there is no cash in the bank.
- In Step 2 the page tells readers to get rid of credit cards and loans other than the mortgage, calls buy now, pay later a scam, and says to pause investing until the debt is gone.
- Step 3 is described as the guard against going back into debt: with 3 to 6 months of expenses saved, a temporary job loss or a failed air conditioner is covered.
- For Step 4 the page stresses patience, summed up in its phrase "Slow and steady wins the race."
- College savings comes after retirement because, the page argues, children may not attend college but the parents will certainly retire. It names Education Savings Accounts and 529 plans, plus grants and scholarships, as the way to avoid student borrowing.
- Step 6 says an intentional household can pay off a mortgage in 10 years or less, and Step 7 describes the end state as no debt, continued wealth building, generous giving, and an inheritance for the next generations.
- The page's questions section advises: lead a reluctant spouse by example instead of nagging, finish debt payoff and the emergency fund before buying a first home, buy life insurance as soon as someone depends on the income, and write a will at any step.
- Limitation (not from the page, mainstream disagreement): some reviewers argue that one fixed order does not fit every household. Physician and author Jim Dahle, for example, has published a list of points where he thinks Ramsey's plan is wrong, alongside the points he agrees with.
