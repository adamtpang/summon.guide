## Key lessons

- The page says Step 6 starts only after consumer debt is gone, the 3 to 6 month fund is in place, 15% is going to retirement, and college savings has begun (for those with children). Steps 4, 5, and 6 then run at the same time.
- Extra mortgage payments come from budget margin. The page says not to pause or reduce retirement or college contributions to speed up the payoff.
- It rejects keeping a mortgage for the interest tax deduction. Its example: paying $10,000 in interest to save $2,500 in tax (at a 25% bracket) is a bad trade, summed up in the phrase "trading a dollar for a quarter."
- Worked example: a $300,000 mortgage at 5% over 15 years has a payment of about $2,373. One extra full payment a year shortens the payoff to about 13 years and 4 months and saves roughly $16,300 in interest.
- Extra payments should be marked as principal only, so they reduce the balance and not future interest.
- On refinancing, the page says it is not needed to pay a loan off faster, is worthwhile only when the lower rate covers the refinance costs, and should never be a cash-out refinance. It also says to skip points and origination fees, since many people move or refinance before breaking even.
- Biweekly payment plans work only because they add up to 13 full payments a year. The page says never to pay a fee to enroll in one.
- It calls using a 401(k) to pay off the house a terrible idea, because income tax plus a 10% early withdrawal penalty can consume 30% or more of the money.
- Other points: keep homeowners insurance after the payoff even though no lender requires it, consider downsizing if the numbers and the family fit, keep budgeting, refill the emergency fund if it is used, and take on no new debt.
- Limitation (not from the page, mainstream disagreement): many economists treat early payoff as a question of opportunity cost, not a rule. Wharton finance professor Michael R. Roberts argues that when a mortgage rate is below what safe savings or Treasury bills pay (his 2023 example was a 3.0% mortgage against 4.5% yields), extra payments mean giving up money and liquidity, and that home equity is hard to reach after a job loss. The page does not discuss this trade-off.
