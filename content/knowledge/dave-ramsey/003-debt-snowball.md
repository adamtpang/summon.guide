## Key lessons

- The article (written by Ramsey personality George Kamel) places the snowball at Baby Step 2: it starts once a person is current on bills and has the $1,000 starter fund.
- The five steps: list non-mortgage debts from smallest to largest balance regardless of interest rate, pay minimums on all but the smallest, put all extra money on the smallest, roll that full payment into the next smallest when it is gone, and repeat until debt-free.
- The stated reason it works is behavioral. The article says personal finance is 80% behavior and 20% knowledge, and that an early payoff makes a person believe the rest is possible and act accordingly.
- It contrasts this with the debt avalanche (highest interest rate first), arguing that the highest-rate debt is often also the largest, so progress feels slow and people lose motivation and quit.
- Worked example: four debts totaling $20,000 (a $500 medical bill, $2,500 credit card, $7,000 car loan, $10,000 student loan). With $500 a month of extra income, the medical bill is gone in one month, the card in about four more, the car loan in under nine, and the student loan about nine months after that, under 24 months in total.
- In the example the monthly amount aimed at a single debt grows from $550 to $613 to $748 to $944 as each payment rolls forward (the last figure includes an extra $100 from further spending cuts).
- The article lists times to pause, not stop, the snowball: having a baby, an unexpected job loss, a health crisis, a major life change such as divorce, or an outstanding IRS bill. In those cases a person covers food, utilities, shelter, and transportation first.
- The article itself concedes that the snowball is not primarily designed to minimize interest and that the avalanche may save somewhat more interest on paper. Its claim is that a method a person sticks with beats an optimal one they abandon.
- Limitation (not from the article, mainstream disagreement): the article also calls the snowball the fastest way to pay off debt, which mainstream sources dispute. Fidelity's comparison states that the avalanche method saves more on interest and may clear debt sooner, especially when rates differ widely, while granting that the snowball can be more emotionally satisfying.
