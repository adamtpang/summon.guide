# Chapter Eight: Too Many Millionaires Next Door

> **Key principle:** Opening Part II, Taleb uses the parable of infinite monkeys on typewriters (one will eventually type the Iliad by chance, but that says nothing about whether it can type the Odyssey next) to introduce survivorship bias, then applies it to the bestseller The Millionaire Next Door and to a lawyer named Marc whose wife feels like a failure despite his being wealthier than 99.5% of Americans, simply because their sample of comparison (Park Avenue neighbors) excludes everyone who didn't succeed.

*Synthesized from Chapter 8 of Fooled by Randomness by Nassim Nicholas Taleb.*

## Key lessons

- Part II opens with the monkeys-on-typewriters thought experiment: with enough monkeys typing randomly, one will eventually reproduce the Iliad by chance, but betting on that same monkey to write the Odyssey next reveals the real question, which is how much a past success should update our belief in future success, a question that depends heavily on how many monkeys (competitors) were in the game and how visible the losers are.
- Taleb stresses that in real life the losing "monkeys" are not just uncounted, they are invisible: failed traders, failed funds, and failed businesses disappear from view entirely, so any sample built from currently visible winners is automatically and severely distorted, a distortion he calls the survivorship bias.
- Marc, a successful Park Avenue lawyer earning $500,000 a year, outperforms roughly 99.5% of the U.S. population, yet his wife Janet feels like a failure because their comparison group, their wealthy co-op neighbors, is itself a self-selected sample that excludes everyone who did not become that successful; Taleb's point is that living exclusively among winners systematically warps one's sense of normal outcomes.
- Taleb critiques the bestseller The Millionaire Next Door for containing a "double survivorship bias": first, its sample of "accumulators" excludes everyone who saved diligently but invested in losing assets and never became millionaires, and second, its conclusions implicitly assume the extraordinary 1982-2000 bull market's returns were normal and permanent rather than a specific, unusually favorable historical episode.
- He extends the critique to an investment "guru" who proposed a contrarian "Robin Hood" strategy of switching money from winning managers to losing ones, based on a backtest that only included managers still in business at the end of the period, silently excluding every manager who did poorly and was shut down, which invalidates the comparison.
- The chapter's broader lesson is methodological: to properly test whether "winners keep winning" or "losers deserve a second look," you must start from the full original population at a fixed past date and track everyone forward, including those who dropped out, rather than starting from today's survivors and reasoning backward.
