# Chapter Fourteen: Bacchus Abandons Antony

> **Key principle:** Taleb closes the book's substantive argument on stoicism, defined not as emotional suppression but as maintaining a fixed, dignified protocol of behavior no matter what randomness delivers, illustrated by the poet C.P. Cavafy's poem read at Jackie Kennedy Onassis's funeral, which counsels Mark Antony to face his ruin "while shaken by emotion" but without cowardly complaint.

*Synthesized from Chapter 14 of Fooled by Randomness by Nassim Nicholas Taleb.*

## Key lessons

- The chapter opens with French writer Henry de Montherlant, who chose to end his own life upon learning he would lose his eyesight to a degenerative disease, which Taleb presents as an extreme illustration of the Stoic and Epicurean principle that one should retain control over one's own response to an otherwise uncontrollable fate, though he is careful to note that suicide is only rarely the prescribed response and is otherwise considered cowardly.
- Taleb insists the ancient epic heroes, including those of the Iliad, were judged by their conduct, not by whether they won or lost their battles, since the outcome was understood to depend on the arbitrary favor of the gods; dignity, in this framework, is doing the honorable thing regardless of the result randomness ultimately delivers.
- He recounts that Maurice Tempelsman read C.P. Cavafy's poem "The God Abandons Antony" at Jackie Kennedy Onassis's funeral, a poem addressed to Mark Antony after his defeat, urging him to bid farewell to the city and life he is losing while fully feeling the loss, rather than deceiving himself with false hope or collapsing into complaint.
- Taleb clarifies that historical stoicism is not the "stiff upper lip" cliché of suppressing feeling; it permits and even expects real emotion, so long as the person's outward conduct, their courage, honesty, and composure, does not bend to circumstance, a nuance he says most people who invoke the term "stoic" today miss entirely.
- He notes the irony that Cavafy himself, dying later of throat cancer, reportedly failed to live up to his own poem's prescription, clinging to visitors in undignified distress, which Taleb uses to reinforce his book's recurring theme that intellectually understanding an ideal does not guarantee living up to it emotionally.
- The chapter ends with concrete, almost etiquette-like prescriptions for facing misfortune with "personal elegance": dress and comport yourself well on your worst day, stay courteous to subordinates when you lose money, avoid self-pity and blame, and treat both victory and defeat as equally an occasion for dignified conduct, since one's behavior is, in his words, "the only article Lady Fortuna has no control over."
