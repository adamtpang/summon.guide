# Chapter Eleven: Randomness and Our Brain: We Are Probability Blind

> **Key principle:** Taleb reveals Nero Tulip's hidden backstory, a cancer diagnosis with a 72% five-year survival rate, to show that even a trained probabilist cannot mentally hold "72% alive, 28% dead" at once; the brain snaps to one vivid image or the other, which Taleb generalizes into a catalogue of ways humans systematically misjudge probability, from medical diagnostics to the O.J. Simpson trial to option pricing.

*Synthesized from Chapter 11 of Fooled by Randomness by Nassim Nicholas Taleb.*

## Key lessons

- Taleb opens with the "Paris or the Bahamas" example: asked to imagine a 50/50 vacation, the mind cannot visualize a blended probabilistic state, only one full scenario or the other, and applies this directly to Nero Tulip's cancer diagnosis, where a 72% survival rate registered emotionally as pure hope, not as a real 28% chance of death.
- He recounts the O.J. Simpson trial as a public demonstration of probability illiteracy: a defense argument citing that several people in Los Angeles could match a DNA profile ignored the compounding effect of joint conditional probabilities, and a Harvard-trained lawyer conflated the unconditional probability that a wife-batterer becomes a murderer with the very different, and much higher, conditional probability that a murdered battered wife was killed by her batterer.
- A classic medical-diagnosis test is cited: given a disease with 1/1,000 prevalence and a test with a 5% false-positive rate, most doctors surveyed wrongly estimated a positive result meant a 95% chance of having the disease, when the correct conditional probability is closer to 2% (about 1 in 51).
- Taleb explains "option blindness": people, including some professionals, mentally default to the single most likely outcome (an out-of-the-money option expiring worthless) rather than correctly weighting it against the full probability-weighted expected value, which is why he says premium sellers "eat like chickens and go to the bathroom like elephants," collecting small steady gains until a rare large loss wipes them out.
- He credits Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky's research as the empirical foundation for "behavioral finance," showing systematically that trained people do not correctly understand contingencies and, even when they intellectually do, often fail to act according to that understanding; Taleb ties this to neurobiology, arguing our brains evolved to overweight survival-relevant fears rather than to compute accurate probabilities.
- The chapter also targets financial journalism: headlines that "explain" trivial, statistically meaningless market moves as if they were significant signals, confusing readers about the difference between noise and information (the topic first raised in Chapter 3), and closes with Taleb's own admission that despite his expertise, he remains personally vulnerable to exactly these biases.
