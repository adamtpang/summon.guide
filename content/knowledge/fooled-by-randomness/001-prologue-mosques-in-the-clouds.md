# Prologue: Mosques in the Clouds

> **Key principle:** The book's whole subject is the "lucky fool," a person who benefited from a disproportionate share of luck but credits some precise personal skill instead, and Taleb argues this confusion is everywhere but nowhere as costly as in trading and business, where the confusion is greatest and the money at stake makes the self-deception most dangerous.

*Synthesized from the Prologue of Fooled by Randomness by Nassim Nicholas Taleb.*

## Key lessons

- Taleb opens with the poet Arthur Rimbaud, who announced "I saw mosques in the clouds," took the vision seriously, and chased it into a ruinous life in Abyssinia; Taleb uses him as the emblem of our will to find meaning (symbolism) in what is really just random shape, whether in clouds, inkblots, or market data.
- He presents a "Table of Confusion" that structures the whole book: on one side sit luck, randomness, belief, theory, anecdote, and forecast; on the other sit skill, determinism, knowledge, reality, causality, and prophecy. The book's central claim is that people habitually mistake the left column for the right one.
- The confusion is not limited to the unsophisticated. Literary critics find deep meaning in accidental word patterns, and financial statisticians proudly detect "regularities" in data that is plain noise; the trained specialist can be the biggest fool of randomness precisely because he trusts his own expertise.
- Taleb explicitly narrows the book's scope to business and trading, "the world in which I operate," because it is the arena where mistaking luck for skill is most prevalent, most visible, and has the most entertaining (and instructive) laboratory of consequences: strategies, entrepreneurs, and traders get credited with "vision" when 99.9% of their track record may be pure chance.
- He distinguishes his project from the opposite question (non-randomness mistaken for randomness, i.e. missed patterns): he considers that risk to be smaller and less interesting than the risk of over-crediting order where there is none, partly because humans are already extremely good at finding patterns everywhere, wanted or not.
- The book is structured in three parts: Part I meditates on Solon's warning about visible and invisible histories (skewness, asymmetry, induction); Part II catalogs the specific probability biases, chiefly survivorship bias, that fool even trained people; Part III concludes that we cannot simply reason our way out of these biases and need practical tricks instead of moralizing.
- Taleb frames his own posture as double-edged: a "practitioner of uncertainty" who resists being fooled by randomness in his profession, but who also loves being fooled by the harmless, beautiful randomness of literature and poetry, refusing to apply the same skepticism to art that he applies to markets.
