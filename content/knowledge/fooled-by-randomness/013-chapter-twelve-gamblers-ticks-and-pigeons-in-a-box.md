# Chapter Twelve: Gamblers' Ticks and Pigeons in a Box

> **Key principle:** Opening Part III with Odysseus stopping his sailors' ears with wax so they could row him safely past the sirens' song, Taleb argues that he, like Odysseus's crew rather than Odysseus himself, cannot defeat his own irrational impulses through willpower or reasoning, and must instead build small behavioral tricks to route around them; he illustrates the underlying weakness with his own gambler's-tick habit of repeating a "lucky" tie, entrance, or pair of glasses after a good trading day.

*Synthesized from Chapter 12 of Fooled by Randomness by Nassim Nicholas Taleb.*

## Key lessons

- Part III opens with the Odyssey's sirens episode: Odysseus has himself tied to the mast so he can hear the sirens' song without steering the ship to ruin, while his sailors plug their ears with wax; Taleb identifies with the sailors, not the hero, concluding his own strategy must be avoidance ("wax in my ears") rather than a doomed attempt at superhuman self-control.
- Taleb catches himself, early in his New York trading career, unconsciously repeating the exact route, entrance, and even the same coffee-stained tie from a day he made unusually large profits, and recognizes this as a "gambler's tick," a spurious statistical association his own mind manufactured between an irrelevant detail and a trading outcome.
- He connects this to B.F. Skinner's famous 1948 pigeon experiment, in which birds fed on a random schedule (unconnected to any lever-pressing) developed elaborate, individualized ritual behaviors, such as head-swinging or spinning, that they had wrongly associated with triggering food delivery, showing that superstition is not a uniquely human or cultural failure but a basic feature of any brain trying to find causal patterns in randomness.
- Taleb argues our minds are built to assume causal connection between any two events observed together (A causes B, B causes A, or a shared cause links them), which makes withholding judgment about causality emotionally much harder than jumping to a conclusion, even for scientists trained to know better.
- He describes his practical countermeasure of "denying himself access to performance reports" unless results cross a predetermined statistical threshold, comparing it to keeping no chocolate under his desk to manage a craving he cannot simply reason away; the point is that willpower fails where structural avoidance succeeds.
- The chapter closes by generalizing this insight past trading: cigarette smokers who fully know lung cancer statistics still smoke, because knowing the correct probability and acting on it emotionally are two separate systems, and moralizing lectures about "the right thing to do" therefore accomplish very little compared to concrete behavioral tricks.
