# Chapter Six: Skewness and Asymmetry

> **Key principle:** Stephen Jay Gould's discovery that "median" and "expected" survival time are not the same thing for a skewed cancer prognosis becomes Taleb's entry point into skewness: a bet with a 999-in-1,000 chance of a small gain and a 1-in-1,000 chance of a catastrophic loss has a negative expected value despite being "right" almost all the time, and Taleb builds his whole trading philosophy around favoring rare, asymmetric payoffs over frequent, small, capped ones.

*Synthesized from Chapter 6 of Fooled by Randomness by Nassim Nicholas Taleb.*

## Key lessons

- Taleb opens with Gould's own cancer diagnosis: told the median survival was eight months, Gould researched further and found the distribution was heavily skewed, with most patients who survived past the median living far longer, which taught him that "median" does not describe the shape of a distribution and can badly mislead someone about their real odds.
- He formalizes this with a simple gambling table: a strategy with a 999/1000 chance of winning $1 and a 1/1000 chance of losing $10,000 has a negative expected value near $9, even though it "wins" almost every time, illustrating that probability of an outcome and its expected value are entirely different things that people routinely conflate.
- Recounting his own experience in Wall Street "discussion meetings," Taleb explains why he rejects the words "bullish" and "bearish" outside of zoology: he could believe the market was more likely to rise (making him "bullish" in probability) while still being positioned short (economically "bearish"), because the potential downside was far larger than the potential upside.
- He defines his own trading style as chasing "rare events": deliberately making money infrequently, because rare events tend to be systematically underpriced by markets, a bias he attributes to people's fixation on frequent, everyday information over less visible tail risk.
- Statisticians and forecasters often discard extreme values as "outliers," a habit borrowed reasonably from fields like education where extremes don't matter much, but Taleb argues this becomes dangerous in finance and in phenomena like ice-cap melting, where the rare extreme event is precisely what does the damage and should not be averaged away.
- He introduces the "peso problem" (named for erratic 1980s Mexican economic data): periods of apparent calm and stability in a security or fund can be followed by sudden, severe breaks, and because rare events are by definition unexpected when they occur, statistical confidence about their absence grows much more slowly than confidence about their presence, an asymmetry central to Hume's and Popper's philosophy of knowledge.
- The chapter ends by connecting the "mischievous child" thought experiment, where an unseen hand keeps changing the composition of balls in an urn you are sampling from, to the deeper problem of non-stationarity in financial time series: if the underlying structure of markets keeps changing, then most of what econometrics infers from historical data may be worthless.
