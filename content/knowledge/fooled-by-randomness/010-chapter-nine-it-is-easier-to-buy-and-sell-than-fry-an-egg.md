# Chapter Nine: It Is Easier to Buy and Sell Than Fry an Egg

> **Key principle:** Taleb runs a Monte Carlo simulation of 10,000 fictional fund managers with a coin-flip-fair game and shows that pure luck alone will still leave 313 managers with five straight winning years; running the same simulation with managers who are provably bad on average (a rigged urn stacked against them) still produces a small number of glittering five-year track records, proving that an impressive record's meaning depends entirely on how large the original competing population was.

*Synthesized from Chapter 9 of Fooled by Randomness by Nassim Nicholas Taleb.*

## Key lessons

- Taleb opens by contrasting professions where skill is nearly impossible to fake, such as dentistry, piano performance, or sculpture, with fund management, where "it is easier to buy and sell than fry an egg": a plausible-looking track record can be produced with no underlying skill at all, because financial markets provide abundant historical data but no ability to run controlled experiments.
- Simulating 10,000 managers in a perfectly fair coin-flip game where anyone with a losing year is cut from the pool, he shows roughly 313 "surviving" managers with five consecutive winning years purely by chance; running the same process with managers who are mathematically expected to lose money on average (a 45%/55% weighted coin) still yields around 184 apparent five-year winners out of the original 10,000.
- He names the key variable as the size of the initial population, not the individual manager's skill: with only ten managers competing, a five-year winning streak is genuinely impressive, but with 10,000 competing, that same streak is statistically expected from luck alone and carries almost no information.
- The chapter connects this to ergodicity: because time eventually washes out lucky streaks, the "surviving" lucky managers are expected to revert to breaking even going forward, no better than the initial cohort that failed earlier, which is why past five-year outperformance predicts almost nothing about future five-year performance in a large, competitive field.
- Taleb extends the same logic to everyday "coincidences": chain-letter investment scams that mail contradictory predictions to thousands of people and only recontact the ones whose prediction happened to be right; the birthday paradox, where any 23 people in a room have roughly a 50% chance some pair shares a birthday; and data-mined backtesting software that will always eventually find some historical trading rule that "worked" purely by chance.
- He extends the bias beyond finance into medicine (the danger of overselling drugs or alternative treatments based on cases of "spontaneous remission" that would have happened anyway) and into science publishing itself, where studies with null results are far less likely to get published than ones with a "significant" finding, meaning the scientific literature as a whole is subject to the same survivorship distortion as fund-manager track records.
