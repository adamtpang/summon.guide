# Chapter Seven: The Problem of Induction

> **Key principle:** Taleb takes David Hume's and John Stuart Mill's black-swan formulation, that no amount of confirming white-swan sightings proves the claim "all swans are white" while a single black swan disproves it, into finance, and pairs it with Karl Popper's falsificationism (a theory is never "true," only "not yet falsified") to justify his rule of using past data to make bets but never to bound his own risk.

*Synthesized from Chapter 7 of Fooled by Randomness by Nassim Nicholas Taleb.*

## Key lessons

- Taleb frames the chapter around Hume's problem of induction as later sharpened into the black-swan image: seeing any number of white swans never logically proves all swans are white, while spotting one black swan (a real species discovered in Australia) instantly disproves it, an asymmetry he treats as central to how markets punish overconfident inference from historical data.
- He credits trader Victor Niederhoffer with teaching him rigorous empiricism, specifically the discipline that any "testable" claim should actually be tested statistically rather than accepted on intuition, while noting his own key refinement: he uses data only to disprove a proposition, never to prove one, since confirming instances cannot establish certainty.
- Taleb describes discovering Karl Popper's philosophy of science through trader-philosopher George Soros, and states Popper's core distinction as he uses it: legitimate theories are either already falsified through testing, or not-yet-falsified but structured so that some observation could falsify them; a claim that cannot in principle be disproven (his examples include unfalsifiable astrology) is not science at all.
- Popper's related concept of the "open society," where no belief is held as permanently true so that it can always be challenged and revised, becomes Taleb's model for intellectual humility in trading: an open mind that keeps revising positions in light of new evidence versus a closed mind that defends a fixed thesis regardless of contrary data.
- Taleb draws a biographical contrast between Popper's ideas and Popper the man, described by acquaintances as insecure, self-righteous, and poor at listening, to make the point that holding correct abstract beliefs about rationality does not guarantee behaving rationally, since our genetic wiring rewards transmitting confident-sounding ideas more than practicing them consistently.
- He closes with his personal application of Pascal's wager: since the asymmetry of knowledge means statistics can sometimes help and sometimes seriously mislead, he resolves to use inductive, historical methods to look for aggressive opportunities, but never to rely on them to manage his risk exposure or set a stop-loss, unlike Carlos and John in Chapter 5, who used the same historical data both to make their bets and measure their safety.
