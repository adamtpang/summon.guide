# Chapter Thirteen: Carneades Comes to Rome: On Probability and Skepticism

> **Key principle:** Taleb retells how the Greek skeptic philosopher Carneades argued brilliantly for justice before the Roman Senate in 155 B.C., then returned the next day and argued the opposite case with equal power, outraging Cato the Elder, and uses the story to argue that genuine intellectual honesty means holding beliefs as provisional probabilities you can reverse without shame, the trait he most admires in trader George Soros and least forgives in the Nobel laureates behind Long-Term Capital Management.

*Synthesized from Chapter 13 of Fooled by Randomness by Nassim Nicholas Taleb.*

## Key lessons

- Taleb tells the story of Carneades of Cyrene, sent to Rome as an ambassador in 155 B.C., who argued eloquently for justice one day and then, to demonstrate that certainty is unattainable, argued just as eloquently against it the next day, so outraging the elder statesman Cato that he had the ambassadors expelled from Rome to protect Roman youth from their skepticism.
- He traces probability's origins to ancient legal rhetoric in Sicily, where early advocates like Korax and Gorgias argued cases from "the most probable" explanation rather than certainty, tying the discipline's roots explicitly to skepticism rather than to mathematics, which only formalized it much later.
- Using Proust's fictional diplomat Monsieur de Norpois, who is mocked in the novel for forgetting and contradicting a past opinion, Taleb argues that our culture wrongly treats changing one's mind as shameful; he cites 1968 Paris student graffiti demanding "the right to contradict ourselves" as an unusually honest counter-statement.
- He praises George Soros for holding beliefs with no "path dependence," recounting an anecdote in which Soros reversed a heavily bearish market position within days without any embarrassment once new evidence emerged, and defines "path dependence of belief" through a simple test: if you would not buy an asset today at its current price, but keep holding it anyway, you are emotionally married to a position rather than rationally assessing it.
- Taleb criticizes Harry Markowitz's Nobel-winning portfolio theory (and by extension Robert Merton and Myron Scholes, principals of Long-Term Capital Management) for assuming markets have clearly computable risk the way a game with known rules does, and blames this false certainty for LTCM's near collapse of the financial system in 1998.
- After LTCM's failure, Taleb notes its principals mostly blamed the "unprecedented" market conditions rather than revising their underlying framework, in contrast to Soros's habitual admission of fallibility, and closes with his own bleak observation that in the soft sciences, flawed paradigms tend to be replaced only when their defenders die off, so that "science evolves from funeral to funeral."
