# Chapter 8: Secrets

> **Key principle:** A conventional truth (like the Pythagorean theorem, once Pythagoras's own secret) won't give you an edge because it's already agreed upon; only an unconventional, still-undiscovered truth can. Thiel argues that if the contrarian question from Chapter 1 has good answers, secrets must still exist, and that the search for them, in nature and in what people won't say about themselves, is the real work of building something new.

*Synthesized from Chapter 8 of Zero to One: Notes on Startups, or How to Build the Future by Peter Thiel with Blake Masters.*

## Key lessons

- Thiel distinguishes secrets (hard but discoverable truths, like Andrew Wiles's 1995 proof of Fermat's Last Theorem after 358 years of failed attempts) from mysteries (possibly unknowable, like whether string theory is even testable); contrarian thinking only makes sense if the world still has real secrets left to find.
- He uses Ted Kaczynski, the Unabomber, as an extreme case study: a Harvard-at-16 prodigy whose 35,000-word manifesto argued modern people are depressed because society has sorted problems into the trivially easy and the flatly impossible, leaving nothing satisfyingly hard in between; Thiel treats Kaczynski's diagnosis as revealing even while rejecting his violent, technology-destroying prescription, and links the same "nothing hard is left" attitude to hipster nostalgia culture.
- Four social trends erode belief in secrets: incrementalism (schools reward small, credentialed steps, not overreaching curiosity), risk aversion (a secret is unvetted by definition, so pursuing one risks being "lonely and wrong"), complacency among elites (why search for secrets if you can comfortably collect rents on what's already known, illustrated by the implicit "you're set for life" message top law and business schools send incoming classes), and "flatness" (globalization makes the world feel too fully mapped and too competitive for any individual to find something new).
- A world with no secrets left would have to mean society already has a perfect understanding of justice, which is false, Thiel notes, since early abolitionists held a "secret" moral truth about slavery that only later became conventional; belief in efficient markets is a parallel case, disproven by real bubbles (the 1999 dot-com mania, Greenspan's dismissal of 2005 housing "froth" ahead of the 2008 crash).
- Hewlett-Packard's arc is his cautionary business case: from $9 billion in 1990 to $135 billion by mid-2000 on a run of real inventions (the first affordable color inkjet printer, an early "superportable" laptop, the first all-in-one printer/fax/copier), then a shift to a 1999 "invent" ad campaign that coincided with the company no longer actually inventing, a services pivot, the Compaq merger, and boardroom dysfunction (chairwoman Patricia Dunn's illegal wiretaps to catch a leaker) that left HP worth roughly $23 billion by 2012.
- There are two kinds of secrets: secrets of nature (found by studying the undiscovered physical world) and secrets about people (things people don't know about themselves, or hide because they don't want others to know); Thiel is skeptical that technical expertise alone transfers, recalling a physics PhD candidate at PayPal who confidently claimed to already know the interview question and was wrong.
- The best place to look for secrets is a field that matters but hasn't been standardized or institutionalized, his example is nutrition, which affects everyone yet isn't a real university major, has few recent rigorous studies, and produced a food pyramid he says was shaped more by lobbying than science.
- On what to do with a secret once found: the right amount of disclosure is neither telling nobody nor telling everybody, but telling exactly who you need to, which in practice usually means forming a company, "the golden mean between telling nobody and telling everybody," a conspiracy of the right size to change the world.
