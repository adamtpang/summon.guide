# Preface: Zero to One

> **Key principle:** Every valuable company does something singular that has never been done before; copying an existing model, however skillfully, only ever produces incremental (horizontal) progress, while true (vertical) progress requires building the one new thing nobody else has built. The book offers no formula for this because a genuine formula for originality is a contradiction in terms.

*Synthesized from the Preface of Zero to One: Notes on Startups, or How to Build the Future by Peter Thiel with Blake Masters.*

## Key lessons

- Thiel opens with the claim that "every moment in business happens only once": the next great founder will not repeat what Bill Gates, Larry Page, or Mark Zuckerberg did, because copying a solved problem isn't learning from it.
- He distinguishes two kinds of progress: horizontal/extensive progress (going from 1 to n, copying things that already work, exemplified by globalization) and vertical/intensive progress (going from 0 to 1, doing something new, exemplified by technology in the broad sense, not just computers).
- Both kinds of progress can occur together, separately, or not at all; he cites 1815-1914 as a period with both, and 1971-present as a period of rapid globalization but technological progress mostly confined to IT.
- His contrarian answer to "what do most people believe that is wrong" is that most people think globalization will define the future, but technology matters more: spreading old, resource-intensive habits (like current American consumption patterns) to the whole world without new technology would be environmentally catastrophic.
- New technology has never been automatic. After roughly 10,000 years of slow advance, the world had a burst of relentless technological progress from the steam engine (1760s) to about 1970, after which progress narrowed almost entirely to computers and communications, leaving physical surroundings "strangely old."
- The book's origin is Thiel's 2012 Stanford course on startups, whose class notes, taken by student Blake Masters, circulated widely online and were revised together into this book; the stated aim is to help readers see past narrow specialties toward a future they can help create themselves, not necessarily at Stanford or in Silicon Valley.
- Thiel explicitly says the book is "an exercise in thinking," not a manual of best practices: because every innovation is new, no authority can prescribe concrete steps to be innovative, and the single most powerful pattern he has noticed is that successful people find value in unexpected places by reasoning from first principles rather than formulas.
