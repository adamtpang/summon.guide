# Chapter 13, Part 2: Tesla, Seven for Seven, and Energy 2.0

> **Key principle:** Tesla rode the same "social buzz" as failed cleantech peers but, unlike them, actually answered all seven questions well: real 10x technology, precisely timed government support, a deliberately small starting monopoly (luxury electric sports cars), a founder-led team, owned distribution, a widening durability lead, and a secret (that buyers wanted to look cool, not just be green) that competitors missed entirely.

*Synthesized from Chapter 13 of Zero to One: Notes on Startups, or How to Build the Future by Peter Thiel with Blake Masters.*

## Key lessons

- Technology: Tesla's engineering is good enough that rival automakers license it directly, Daimler uses Tesla battery packs, Mercedes-Benz a Tesla powertrain, Toyota a Tesla motor, and its real achievement is systems integration, not any single part; the Model S was rated by Consumer Reports higher than any car it had ever reviewed and won 2013 Car of the Year from both Motor Trend and Automobile.
- Timing: in January 2010, roughly a year and a half before Solyndra's collapse politicized clean-energy subsidies, Tesla secured a $465 million Department of Energy loan, a scale of government support Thiel calls "unthinkable" both before and after that narrow window; Musk read the one-time nature of the opportunity correctly while cleantech peers assumed subsidies would simply continue indefinitely.
- Monopoly: Tesla deliberately started in the tiny, high-end electric sports car niche (roughly 3,000 Roadsters sold at about $109,000 each) rather than chasing the mass market immediately, using that beachhead to fund R&D toward the Model S, which then went on to sell more than 20,000 units in 2013 and take over the luxury electric sedan segment.
- People: Musk built (in his own words) an engineer-and-salesman "Special Forces" team, explicitly contrasting the intensity expected at Tesla with a more comfortable "regular army" elsewhere in the industry.
- Distribution: unlike Ford or Hyundai, which depend on independent dealership networks, Tesla chose to own its entire sales and service chain through company-run stores, a higher upfront cost that bought Tesla direct control over the customer experience, stronger branding, and long-run savings.
- Durability: Tesla's early lead compounds because trust in a category as consequential as a car purchase is hard-won and because, unlike most car companies, its founder remained in charge, avoiding the kind of leadership churn that can slow long-term execution.
- Secret: while other cleantech and hybrid brands (the boxy Prius, the Honda Insight) let buyers signal environmental virtue at the cost of looking uncool, Tesla's insight was that "cleantech was even more of a social phenomenon than an environmental imperative," so it built a car that made owners look good first, illustrated by Leonardo DiCaprio trading in his Prius for a Tesla Roadster.
- Closing the chapter, Thiel argues cleantech's core premise, that the world genuinely needs new energy sources, remains true, and draws a direct parallel to the dot-com bubble: skeptics who doubted the internet would reshape retail and media looked right in 2001 and foolish a decade later, so a real "Energy 2.0" wave could plausibly follow the cleantech crash the way Web 2.0 followed the dot-com crash.
- His prescription for that next wave mirrors the whole book's central strategic move: no sector is so important that merely participating guarantees success, only a company solving a specific problem in a specific, dominable niche can, just as Facebook started at one university before expanding; his suggested cleantech-adjacent niches (replacing diesel power for remote islands, modular reactors for isolated military installations) illustrate that Energy 2.0's founders will need to, paradoxically, "think small."
