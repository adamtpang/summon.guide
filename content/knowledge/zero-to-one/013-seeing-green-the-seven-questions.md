# Chapter 13, Part 1: Seeing Green and the Seven Questions

> **Key principle:** Every business, in any industry, must answer seven questions to succeed: can you make a real technological leap, is now the right time, are you starting with real monopoly potential in a small market, do you have the right team, can you actually distribute the product, will your position hold for a decade or more, and have you found a secret opportunity others have missed. The cleantech bubble collapsed because most companies in it answered none of them.

*Synthesized from Chapter 13 of Zero to One: Notes on Startups, or How to Build the Future by Peter Thiel with Blake Masters.*

## Key lessons

- Thiel opens with the early-2000s consensus that clean technology was inevitable, driven by visible crises (Beijing smog, arsenic-laden water in Bangladesh, Hurricanes Ivan and Katrina, Al Gore's call to mobilize "with the urgency... previously seen only when nations mobilized for war"); thousands of cleantech startups launched and investors poured more than $50 billion in, but the result was a bubble, not a cleaner world, with over 40 solar manufacturers going bankrupt or closing in 2012 alone (Solyndra being the most famous casualty).
- He rejects the purely political explanation (that government involvement "poisoned" cleantech) and instead argues most cleantech companies failed by neglecting one or more of seven questions every business plan must answer, and that getting even five or six of the seven right can work, while getting zero, common across cleantech, amounts to betting on a miracle.
- The Engineering Question (can you create a 10x, not incremental, improvement?): Solyndra's cylindrical solar cells were actually only about 1/π as efficient per unit area as flat cells, a physically worse starting point no amount of added mirrors could fully offset; Thiel's broader rule is that anything short of a 10x improvement reads to customers as marginal and is hard to sell against skepticism built up from years of exaggerated product claims.
- The Timing Question (is now the right moment?): SpectraWatt's 2008 CEO compared the solar industry to microprocessors in the late 1970s, but the analogy was false, microprocessors improved exponentially from 1970 onward, while silicon solar cell efficiency crept up only slowly and linearly from Bell Labs' first 6%-efficient cell in 1954 to a ceiling around 25% decades later; there was no equivalent inflection point to exploit.
- The Monopoly Question (are you starting with real dominance of a small market?): MiaSolé's CEO told Congress in 2006 his firm was one of several "very strong" thin-film solar startups, then predicted it would become the world's largest thin-film producer within a year, it didn't, and MiaSolé was later acquired for far less than investors had put in; Thiel also shows how the "monopoly question" can be gamed by shrinking your claimed market until you appear dominant (10.53% of US solar becomes under 1% of global solar becomes 0.02% of global renewables), which is really just admitting there's no real monopoly at all.
- The People Question (do you have the right team?): cleantech's failed companies were disproportionately run by "shockingly nontechnical," suit-and-tie executives skilled at raising capital and securing subsidies but not at building what customers wanted; Founders Fund reportedly used a literal dress-code heuristic, pass on any cleantech company whose founders wore suits to pitch meetings, as a fast proxy for the deeper truth that "the best sales is hidden."
- The Distribution Question (can you actually deliver the product to customers?): Israeli EV startup Better Place raised and spent over $800 million on swappable battery infrastructure but sold only about 1,000 cars before its 2012-13 collapse, hampered by a genuinely confusing purchase process (approval requirements, route promises, fueling subscriptions) and a near-total failure to market clearly what it was actually selling or why.
- The Durability Question (will your position hold 10-20 years out?): Evergreen Solar, Abound Solar, and Energy Conversion Devices all collapsed blaming low-cost Chinese manufacturing competition they should have anticipated; the industry as a whole was also blindsided by the fracking boom, US shale gas share rising from 1.7% (2000) to 34% (2013) while gas prices fell over 70% since 2008, undercutting renewables' economics from an entirely different direction than expected.
- The Secret Question (have you found something others have missed?): cleantech pitches almost universally cited the same conventional, widely agreed-upon justification (the world needs cleaner energy), which Thiel argues is precisely the problem, real businesses need a specific, non-obvious reason for success, not a shared, socially-approved motive everyone already accepts.
