# Chapter 4: The Ideology of Competition

> **Key principle:** Competition, unlike monopoly, benefits nobody: it produces no profit, no real differentiation, and a Darwinian struggle for survival, yet it is preached as a virtue from grade school onward. Thiel uses his own path through Stanford, Stanford Law, and a near-miss Supreme Court clerkship, and a set of corporate rivalries (Microsoft vs. Google, Oracle vs. Siebel, PayPal vs. X.com), to argue conflict is usually driven by sameness (Shakespeare's model) rather than genuine difference (Marx's model), and is best avoided rather than won.

*Synthesized from Chapter 4 of Zero to One: Notes on Startups, or How to Build the Future by Peter Thiel with Blake Masters.*

## Key lessons

- Thiel argues competition functions as "the ideology," internalized through schooling: grades measure competitiveness precisely, students are pushed to conform to the same curriculum regardless of talent, and the tournament intensifies at each level until, at elite universities, students end up competing fiercely for conventional prizes like consulting and banking jobs.
- He illustrates this with his own biography: a tracked path from Stanford to Stanford Law School to a near-miss on Supreme Court clerkships with Justices Kennedy and Scalia; years later, after selling PayPal, an old friend asked him, "aren't you glad you didn't get that clerkship?", since winning it would likely have locked him into drafting other people's deals rather than creating anything new.
- Thiel offers two models for why people fight: Marx's, where conflict comes from genuine difference, and Shakespeare's, where combatants (like the Montagues and Capulets) grow more alike as their feud escalates until they lose sight of why they started; he argues Shakespeare is the better guide to business rivalry.
- His central case is a fictionalized "Gates and Schmidt" retelling of Romeo and Juliet: Microsoft and Google started in genuinely different businesses (operating systems vs. search) but became obsessed with mirroring each other (Windows vs. Chrome OS, Bing vs. Search, Office vs. Docs), and while they fought, Apple overtook both, its market cap exceeding their combined value by January 2013.
- Imitative rivalry can become absurd: he cites the wave of copycat mobile card readers that followed Square (NetSecure's half-moon reader, Intuit's cylinder, PayPal's triangle) and the late-'90s scramble among Pets.com, PetStore.com, and Petopia.com to out-compete each other in a market none of them had validated was worth being in; $300 million evaporated when Pets.com folded.
- Rivalry can also turn personal and self-destructive, illustrated by Larry Ellison's feud with his former protege Tom Siebel (dueling ice-cream-sandwich and billboard campaigns) and by rival Informix CEO Phil White's obsession with humiliating Ellison via billboards, shortly before Informix collapsed in an accounting scandal that sent White to prison.
- Thiel's own formative rivalry was PayPal (his Confinity) versus Elon Musk's X.com, offices four blocks apart, with 100-hour weeks spent fixated on "defeating X.com" (at one point an engineer literally sketched a bomb before being talked down); in early 2000, fear of the tech bubble popping first, not fear of each other, drove the two founders to negotiate a 50-50 merger at a neutral cafe, letting the combined company survive the crash.
- The chapter closes on when fighting is unavoidable: "either don't throw any punches, or strike hard and end it quickly," with Hamlet's speech on fighting "even for an eggshell" used to show how pride and honor can trap people in destructive conflict; recognizing competition as a destructive force, not a badge of value, is framed as the sane starting point for building a monopoly.
