# Chapter 5: Last Mover Advantage

> **Key principle:** A company's value is the sum of its discounted future cash flows, which is why Twitter, unprofitable at its 2013 IPO, was valued at 12 times the profitable New York Times: investors were pricing in Twitter's future monopoly-style cash flows, not its current earnings. Durable monopolies share some combination of proprietary technology, network effects, economies of scale, and branding, and the surest way to build one is to start in a market small enough to dominate completely, then expand into adjacent markets.

*Synthesized from Chapter 5 of Zero to One: Notes on Startups, or How to Build the Future by Peter Thiel with Blake Masters.*

## Key lessons

- Company value equals the present-day sum of all future cash flows, discounted back to today; this is why tech companies that lose money for years (Thiel cites PayPal's own March 2001 projection that 75% of its value would come from 2011 and beyond) can be worth more than profitable "Old Economy" businesses with shrinking multi-year horizons, like newspapers or restaurants facing fickle customers.
- Chasing short-term growth metrics (weekly active users, quarterly revenue) can mask a lack of durability; Thiel cites Zynga's inability to reliably produce hit games after Farmville and Groupon's difficulty converting one-time merchant trials into repeat business as cases where fast growth concealed a durability problem.
- Four characteristics build a durable monopoly: (1) Proprietary technology, ideally 10x better than the nearest substitute (PayPal's instant payment vs. a 7-10 day mailed check, Amazon's "Earth's largest bookstore" via zero physical inventory, the iPad's leap over unusable prior tablet designs); (2) Network effects, which require the product to be valuable even to its first small users, illustrated by Facebook starting at Harvard rather than trying to reach everyone at once, versus Xanadu's failed attempt to launch a computer network that needed universal adoption from day one.
- (3) Economies of scale, where software's near-zero marginal cost of reproduction lets a firm like Twitter (250+ million users) scale almost without limit, unlike a yoga studio capped by physical class size; (4) Branding, exemplified by Apple, whose brand only works because it rests on real substance (proprietary hardware/software, manufacturing scale, a developer-network effect), a warning against Yahoo's attempt under Marissa Mayer to buy "coolness" (a Tumblr acquisition, a new logo) without a clear underlying product plan.
- The core scaling strategy is "start small and monopolize": every startup begins small, every monopoly dominates a large market share, so every startup should target a market small enough to actually dominate; PayPal's early failure targeting millions of scattered PalmPilot users, versus its quick win capturing 25% of eBay's few thousand "PowerSellers" in three months, illustrates the point directly.
- Scaling up means expanding deliberately into adjacent markets once a niche is dominated: Amazon started with books (uniform shape, easy shipping, a long tail no physical store could stock) before expanding to CDs, videos, software, and eventually "the world's general store"; eBay dominated niche collector communities (Beanie Babies) before broadening, though it later found the auction model doesn't fit commodity goods as well as Amazon's fixed-price model.
- Thiel warns against chasing "disruption" as a self-understanding: the term describes threats to incumbents from the incumbent's point of view, and startups that define themselves by opposition to existing firms (like Napster's short, explosive, ultimately bankrupt collision with the recording industry) invite a fight they may not win; PayPal's growth, by contrast, expanded the overall payments market and gave Visa more business than it took.
- "First mover advantage" is a tactic, not a goal; what matters is being the LAST mover, the company that makes the final major advance in a market and then holds years or decades of monopoly cash flow, which Thiel likens to chess grandmaster Jose Raul Capablanca's advice to "study the endgame before everything else."
