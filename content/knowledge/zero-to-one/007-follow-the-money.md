# Chapter 7: Follow the Money

> **Key principle:** Thiel opens with the (likely apocryphal) Einstein quote about compound interest to set up his real subject: the power law. A small number of outliers vastly outperform everything else, in earthquakes, cities, land ownership (Pareto's 80-20 rule), and above all in venture capital, where the top investment in a good fund typically equals or beats the combined return of every other investment in that fund.

*Synthesized from Chapter 7 of Zero to One: Notes on Startups, or How to Build the Future by Peter Thiel with Blake Masters.*

## Key lessons

- Thiel opens with the widely misattributed Einstein quote calling compound interest the "eighth wonder of the world," noting the misattribution itself illustrates the power law: a small number of names (Einstein, Shakespeare) get credited disproportionately, just as Vilfredo Pareto found 20% of Italians owned 80% of the land, a pattern he also saw in his own garden's peapods.
- Venture funds typically lose money early (most startups fail fast) and hope for a late "exponential growth spurt" from the few winners; the mistake most VCs make is expecting a bell curve of outcomes (bad companies fail, good ones return 2-4x) rather than a power law where a tiny minority radically outperforms everyone else, a "spray and pray" diversified portfolio built on the bell-curve assumption usually produces a fund full of flops.
- Founders Fund's own results illustrate this starkly: Facebook, the best investment in its 2005 fund, returned more than all the other investments in that fund combined; Palantir, the second-best, was on track to return more than everything else besides Facebook.
- The power law implies two rules for VCs: only invest in companies that could return the value of the entire fund (which eliminates most possible investments), and because that rule is so restrictive, there can be no other rules, since chasing a merely-good outcome (like Andreessen Horowitz's real but comparatively small 312x return on its $250,000 Instagram bet) isn't enough at fund scale.
- The power law is hard to see day to day because most of an investor's time is spent on early-stage companies that all look roughly similar before diverging, and because problem companies (not star companies) absorb the most attention; only after roughly a decade does the split into "one dominant investment and everything else" become visible.
- Venture-backed companies are a tiny slice of the economy (less than 1% of new US businesses funded each year, under 0.2% of GDP in VC investment) yet create about 11% of private-sector jobs and roughly 21% of GDP in revenue; the 12 largest venture-backed tech companies alone were worth more than all other tech companies combined.
- Thiel extends the power law beyond investing: every entrepreneur is an investor of her own time, and every individual is an investor in a career; the standard advice to diversify ("don't put all your eggs in one basket") is folk wisdom that schools reinforce (uniform 45-minute periods, broad course catalogs implying "it doesn't matter what you do, as long as you do it well"), but a startup founder cannot literally diversify by running several companies at once, and neither can an individual meaningfully diversify a single life or career.
- His conclusion is that the most important things in business are singular, not diversified: one market usually beats all others (Chapter 5), one distribution channel usually dominates (Chapter 11), and some moments and decisions matter far more than others (Chapter 9); since the world denies the power law by default, its biggest implications are rarely obvious and may even be secret.
