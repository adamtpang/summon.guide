# Chapter 9: Foundations

> **Key principle:** Beginnings are qualitatively different from what follows, true of the universe's first microseconds and of the US Constitutional Convention alike, and company founding is no exception. Bad early decisions, the wrong co-founder, a misaligned cap table, a bloated board, are extraordinarily hard to correct later, often requiring something as drastic as a bankruptcy-level crisis before anyone even tries.

*Synthesized from Chapter 9 of Zero to One: Notes on Startups, or How to Build the Future by Peter Thiel with Blake Masters.*

## Key lessons

- Thiel frames "Thiel's law" (a startup messed up at its foundation cannot be fixed) with two analogies: the universe's first microseconds, when the laws of physics themselves were different, and the US Constitutional Convention, whose compromises (like California and Alaska having equal Senate representation despite a 50x population gap) have proven durable precisely because they were set at the founding and rarely revisited since.
- Choosing a co-founder is "like getting married," and Thiel illustrates the risk with his own early, costly mistake: investing in a company where his friend Luke Nosek partnered with a virtual stranger met at a networking event, a mismatch (eccentric technologist vs. gold-rush-chasing MBA type) that doomed the company; he now studies founding teams and prehistory, not just individual talent, before investing.
- He rejects two extremes for internal alignment: the "libertarian" solution of pure sole proprietorship (limits what you can build) and the "anarchist" fantasy of a structureless team that self-organizes perfectly (ignores that "men aren't angels," per Madison); the fix is distinguishing three roles, ownership (who holds equity), possession (who runs day-to-day operations), and control (who governs, i.e., the board), each of which can misalign with the others.
- The DMV is his worst-case illustration of misalignment: citizens nominally "own" it, elected officials nominally "control" it, but clerks in actual possession of daily operations answer to neither; large corporations show a milder version, where a CEO who owns only a trivial equity stake is incentivized to protect short-term possession-based rewards (salary, perks) over long-term shareholder value.
- Board size matters enormously: three is ideal, five should be the outer limit unless a company is public (where regulation pushes boards toward an average of nine); a small board can communicate and reach consensus effectively, but a giant board (common in poorly run nonprofits) provides only the appearance of oversight while giving free rein to whoever actually runs the organization.
- Nearly everyone involved with the company should be full-time; Thiel treats the on-the-bus/off-the-bus decision as binary (a nod to Ken Kesey), arguing part-time employees, outside consultants (with narrow exceptions like lawyers and accountants), and even full remote work create structural misalignment, since anyone not drawing salary or holding equity is biased toward near-term value extraction.
- On compensation, Thiel's rule is that CEO salary should never exceed $150,000 at an early-stage venture-backed startup, since high cash pay turns a founder into "more like a politician than a founder," defending the status quo rather than building; he cites Box CEO Aaron Levie living in a bare one-bedroom apartment years after founding as the kind of visible restraint that sets the tone for the whole company.
- Equity, not cash, is the tool that actually aligns people with a startup's long-term future, but allocating it fairly is inherently impossible (equal shares feel arbitrary given unequal risk and contribution, unequal shares feel unfair too); Thiel notes wild historical disparities (an early eBay secretary out-earning her own boss who joined a few years later) and advises founders to keep equity details close rather than triggering resentment by publishing them.
- The founding moment isn't strictly a single instant: it lasts as long as a company keeps creating new things and ends when creation stops, meaning a founder who keeps the original openness to invention alive can, in Thiel's phrase, "extend its founding indefinitely."
