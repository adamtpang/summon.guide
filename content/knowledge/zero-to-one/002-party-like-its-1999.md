# Chapter 2: Party Like It's 1999

> **Key principle:** The internet bubble's collapse got reinterpreted as proof that big, definite plans are dangerous, but Thiel argues the opposite lessons are closer to correct: boldness beats triviality, a bad plan beats no plan, competitive markets destroy profits, and sales matters as much as product. The crash was real, but the dogma that followed it overcorrected.

*Synthesized from Chapter 2 of Zero to One: Notes on Startups, or How to Build the Future by Peter Thiel with Blake Masters.*

## Key lessons

- Thiel starts from Nietzsche's line that madness is rare in individuals but the rule in groups, and treats "companies exist to make money, not lose it" as the kind of obvious truth the late-'90s New Economy conveniently forgot, prizing page views over profit.
- He recaps the actual 1990s: a recession from 1990-92, slow recovery, Somalia and grunge-era pessimism, then Mosaic (1993) and Netscape's 1995 IPO igniting the boom; by the late '90s the dot-com mania (September 1998-March 2000) was just 18 months of "insanity" set against a backdrop of real global instability (the 1997 Asian financial crisis, the 1998 Russian ruble default, LTCM's collapse, a shaky new euro).
- At PayPal in late 1999, Thiel was "scared out of my wits" watching peers plan IPOs from their living rooms; PayPal's own first product (beaming money between PalmPilots) flopped, so the team pivoted to email payments and paid users $10 to sign up plus $10 per referral, buying exponential but expensive growth while racing to raise capital before the bubble burst.
- PayPal closed a $100 million round in March 2000, literally days before the NASDAQ peaked at 5,048 and then crashed to 1,114 by October 2002; the funding round's timing, not luck alone, is what let the company survive the crash it had bet would come.
- Out of the crash came four dogmas that became Silicon Valley orthodoxy: make incremental advances, stay lean and flexible ("unplanned"), improve on the competition rather than create a new market, and focus on product, not sales or advertising.
- Thiel argues the opposite of each dogma is probably closer to true: it's better to risk boldness than triviality, a bad plan is better than no plan, competitive markets destroy profits, and sales matters just as much as product, because these dogmas mistake reactions to a specific historical mistake for timeless wisdom.
- The chapter's closing move is not to simply invert the crowd's post-crash consensus either; the real discipline is asking how much of what you believe about business is just a mistaken reaction to past mistakes, since "the most contrarian thing of all is not to oppose the crowd but to think for yourself."
