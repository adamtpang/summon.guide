# The Merchandising of Virtue

> **Key principle:** Taleb argues that genuine virtue must be private, costly, and risk-bearing (something you do when nobody is watching and that could cost you your reputation), whereas most modern displays of virtue, from corporate environmentalism to celebrity activism, are low-cost, low-risk performances that function more like a purchased indulgence than an ethical act.

*Synthesized from Chapter 13 of Skin in the Game by Nassim Nicholas Taleb.*

## Key lessons

- Taleb opens with a personal anecdote about writer Susan Sontag, whom he met in 2001: on learning he was a trader, she declared herself "against the market system" and turned her back on him mid-sentence, yet he later learned she had squeezed her publisher for millions and shared a $28 million Manhattan mansion, an example of claiming anti-market virtue while living entirely inside its comforts. He contrasts this with people he says actually lived their stated ideals, naming activist Ralph Nader's monk-like personal austerity and philosopher Simone Weil's decision to work a year in a car factory to understand the working class firsthand.
- "The Public and the Private" argues that people who feel virtuous in the abstract, through conferences, PowerPoint presentations, and meetings about helping "the poor," often feel licensed to treat real individuals badly; Taleb's rule is that if a person's private conduct contradicts their public intellectual position, the private conduct discredits the stated position, not the other way around, and cites students at elite colleges lecturing on "privilege" without being willing to give up their own admitted spot.
- "The Virtue Merchants" uses hotel signs urging guests to reuse towels "to protect the environment" (really to cut laundry costs) as a template for how big causes like poverty and the environment become marketing tools; Taleb cites Matthew 6:1-4's instruction to give to the needy secretly, without letting even your own left hand know what your right hand is doing, as evidence that this critique of showy virtue is ancient, not new.
- "To Be or to Seem?" invokes Charlie Munger's question about whether you'd rather actually be the world's greatest lover or merely be believed to be one, and traces the underlying Latin phrase esse quam videri, which Taleb translates as "to be or to be seen as such," through Cicero, Sallust, and Machiavelli, who inverted it into videri quam esse, "show rather than be."
- "Simony" compares modern public charity giving to the medieval Catholic practice of selling church offices and indulgences: both let the wealthy purchase a reputation for goodness cheaply, with a share of donated money often spent advertising the donation itself (galas, naming rights, marathons framed as altruism) rather than the underlying cause.
- "Virtue Is About Others and the Collective" and "Unpopular Virtue" argue that true virtue means helping people the larger charity apparatus overlooks, and that its highest form is inherently unpopular because it risks something real, your reputation; Taleb illustrates this with journalists during the Syrian war who were smeared as "Assadist" for questioning support for jihadist factions, arguing that popular, safe moral posturing (mob shaming when it's socially costless) is closer to cowardice than to virtue.
- "Take Risk" closes the chapter with Taleb's advice to young people who want to "help mankind": avoid virtue signaling, avoid rent-seeking, and start a business instead, since courage and real risk-taking (which may or may not lead to riches you can then give away) is, in his view, the highest and only unfakeable virtue.
