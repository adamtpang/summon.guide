# An Expert Called Lindy

> **Key principle:** Taleb names and formalizes the Lindy effect, the idea that for non-perishable things, every extra period of survival increases expected future survival, and argues this makes time itself the only judge whose verdict cannot be gamed, which is why old, tested heuristics often beat new academic findings.

*Synthesized from Chapter 8 of Skin in the Game by Nassim Nicholas Taleb.*

## Key lessons

- The chapter takes its name from Lindy's, a New York deli where actors and, later, mathematicians noticed that a Broadway show's future run length correlated with how long it had already run: a show running one hundred days could be expected to run about a hundred more, and one running two hundred days about two hundred more. Taleb credits himself and mathematician Iddo Eliazar with giving this "Lindy effect" a rigorous grounding in the theory of fragility.
- Taleb defines fragility as sensitivity to disorder and shows that fragile things (his example is a porcelain owl on his desk) have an asymmetric, nonlinear response to shocks: they suffer disproportionately more harm from larger stressors. Since time and disorder are functionally equivalent in probability, survival over time is really a demonstration of resistance to disorder, which is what makes something Lindy.
- The key rule: something is "Lindy" if its remaining life expectancy grows the longer it has already survived (the opposite of biological aging, where remaining life expectancy shrinks with age). Taleb illustrates with turtles and crocodiles, whose remaining life expectancy stays roughly constant regardless of current age, unlike humans. Only non-perishable things (ideas, books, technologies, institutions, political systems) can be Lindy; a physical copy of a book can degrade, but the book as an idea does not.
- Lindy solves the "who guards the guards" problem: instead of needing meta-experts to judge experts (turtles all the way down), survival itself, filtered through real exposure to failure, becomes the judge. Things that persist despite being exposed to real risk of failure carry genuine evidence of robustness; things that were never tested by real stakes can look fine for a long time and then collapse.
- "The Lindy of Lindy" argues the Lindy effect passes its own test: Taleb cites the ancient Greek statesman Periander of Corinth and the medieval king Alfonso X of Spain both giving versions of "use old things, trust old wisdom," showing the idea itself has already survived roughly 2,500 years.
- "Do We Need a Judge?" and "Tea with the Queen" argue that peer approval (academic prizes, prestigious invitations, elite social recognition) is a weak, gameable substitute for the real judge, which is the verdict of future readers or future outcomes, not present-day peers; Taleb describes deliberately avoiding full-time academic employment to keep from being captured by peer judgment rather than judged by time.
- The "Institutions" section extends this to argue that academic fields disconnected from skin in the game (Taleb names macroeconomics, some psychology, and gender studies as examples) tend to degenerate into closed, self-referential citation rings that reward looking sophisticated over being right, because their internal "peer review" judges are not Lindy-compatible, they only reflect what currently powerful insiders like.
- "The Grandmother vs. the Researchers" is the chapter's practical payoff: because science in fast-moving, low-replication fields (psychology, nutrition, behavioral economics) fails to replicate at high rates, while inherited folk wisdom and classical moral literature (Cicero, Seneca, Montaigne) have already survived a vastly longer real-world test, old heuristics ("grandmother's wisdom") often deserve more trust than a recent academic paper making the same or a contradicting claim.
