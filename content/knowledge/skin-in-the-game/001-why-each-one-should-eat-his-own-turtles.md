# Why Each One Should Eat His Own Turtles: Equality in Uncertainty

> **Key principle:** Taleb opens Book 2 with an old fable about fishermen forced by the god Mercury to eat the turtles they caught but didn't want, establishing his rule that whoever benefits from an action should also bear its consequences; he then works through a series of real-world transactions — a lecture agent, Wall Street "stuffing," a Talmudic story about Rav Safra, and the boundary between insiders and outsiders — to show how asymmetric information and asymmetric exposure to risk corrupt supposedly ethical relationships.

*Synthesized from Chapter 1 of Skin in the Game by Nassim Nicholas Taleb.*

## Key lessons

- The chapter's title comes from an ancient fable: fishermen who catch turtles they don't want to eat try to unload them on the god Mercury, who instead forces the whole group to eat their own catch, establishing the principle that whoever feeds something to others should be willing to eat it themselves — the model for all fair transactions.
- Taleb defines the core agency problem through personal example: a lecture agent's pitch was framed as beneficial to Taleb, when it was really structured to serve the agent's own interest; when a tax problem later arose from the arrangement, the agent flatly disclaimed any responsibility for it. The lesson: be suspicious of anyone who tells you an action is good for you while it is also, unmistakably, good for them.
- From his own time at a "white shoe" investment bank, Taleb describes how salesmen were paid to "unload" or "stuff" unwanted inventory onto clients under the guise of friendly advice, summed up by one top salesman's cynical creed: "Rip them off, don't tick them off."
- He distinguishes ethical positions on disclosure via the ancient Stoic debate (relayed by Cicero) between Diogenes of Babylon, who held sellers need only disclose what civil law requires, and his student Antipater of Tarsus, who argued for full disclosure of everything the seller knows that the buyer doesn't — Taleb sides with Antipater's more robust standard, and connects it to Sharia's concept of gharar (banning transactions with lopsided uncertainty between buyer and seller).
- The Talmudic anecdote of Rav Safra, a scholar-trader who refused to accept a buyer's raised price even though he had said nothing to invite it (because his initial silent intention had been to sell at the lower price), is used as the chapter's model of total transparency — even transparency of unspoken intention — as the most robust and shame-free way to transact.
- Taleb introduces the idea of "Members and Non-Members" (also framed as "are you on the diagonal?"): ethical rules of reciprocity apply fully within a bounded group (family, tribe, club, city) but weaken or vanish toward outsiders — historically dubbed "the Swiss," meaning anonymous, faraway counterparties to whom looser rules apply; he draws on Elinor Ostrom's research showing that communities below a certain scale self-regulate shared resources ("commons") without needing either pure private property or centralized control.
- "All (Literally) in the Same Boat" describes ancient maritime risk-sharing law (the Lex Rhodia): if cargo had to be jettisoned to save a ship, the loss was split proportionally among all merchants aboard, not borne only by the owner of the discarded goods — a literal, contractual example of shared skin in the game, which Taleb offers as the model of true risk-sharing (as opposed to risk transfer).
- "Talking One's Book" distinguishes disclosed self-interest (buying a stock you believe in, then openly discussing it) from market manipulation (buying a stock in order to hype it, then selling into the hype); Taleb argues that a person's undisclosed but risk-free opinion (like a pundit or journalist with no stake in the outcome) is more dangerous than an openly conflicted one, because skin in the game at least aligns incentives with real outcomes.
- The chapter closes with a doctor's-office example of the agency problem in medicine: doctors are structurally pushed by liability and five-year survival metrics to choose treatments (like radiation over more precise laser surgery) that look safer on paper but shift risk into a future window outside the horizon they are judged on, illustrating how metrics can be gamed even by well-intentioned professionals.
