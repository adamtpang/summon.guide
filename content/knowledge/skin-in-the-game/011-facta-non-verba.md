# Facta non Verba (Deeds Before Words)

> **Key principle:** Taleb argues, using the medieval order of the Assassins as his central case, that real threats are proven through costly, irreversible action rather than words, and that owning a living enemy who knows he could be killed at any moment is more powerful than actually killing him.

*Synthesized from Chapter 11 of Skin in the Game by Nassim Nicholas Taleb.*

## Key lessons

- The chapter's Latin title, "facta non verba" (deeds, not words), states its thesis directly: verbal threats reveal only weakness and unreliability, while a credible threat has to be backed by demonstrated willingness to act.
- In "An Offer Very Hard to Refuse," Taleb uses the horse's-head scene from The Godfather to distinguish two categories often confused as "terrorism": indiscriminate civilian-killing groups aiming purely to make a statement (he names Al Qaeda and ISIS), versus groups pursuing targeted political assassination in a strategic cause (he lists the IRA, Algerian independence fighters, and French Resistance fighters), the latter tradition tracing back through the Assassins to the Judean Sicarii, dagger-wielding fighters against Roman occupiers.
- In "The Assassins," Taleb describes the Nizari Ismaili sect (also called Hashishins) who preferred to own rather than kill their enemies: in 1118 they left a dagger planted in the ground beside the sleeping Seljuk sultan Ahmad Sanjar, communicating that the same blade could have been in his chest, without ever issuing a spoken threat; Sanjar got the message and left them alone for the rest of his reign. The Assassins reportedly sent a similar wordless warning to Saladin about a cake they claimed to have poisoned.
- "Assassination as Marketing" makes the point that political assassinations, unlike ordinary contract killings, are historically more valuable when they visibly look intentional rather than like an accident, because the point is deterrence through reputation; Taleb also argues (citing his own research with Mark Weisenborn and Pasquale Cirillo) that historical war-casualty figures, including Mongol conquests and the 1982 Hama events in Syria, are routinely inflated by both sides for strategic effect.
- "Assassination as Democracy" argues that political turnover achieved through the threat or reality of assassination functioned as a check on bad rulers when democratic accountability was absent, citing Count Ernst zu Münster's line about the Russian constitution ("Absolutism tempered by assassination") and noting that Rome's worst emperors (Caligula, Caracalla, Elagabalus, Nero) were all removed by murder or forced suicide.
- In "The Camera for Skin in the Game," Taleb describes discovering that simply photographing someone behaving abusively (a subway harasher, a man who stole his parking spot, cyclists trespassing on a nature preserve) works as a modern, nonverbal equivalent of the Assassins' method: the mere threat of unknown consequences, without any spoken words, quickly changes behavior, echoing the Ring of Gyges question from Plato's Republic about whether people are ethical only because they're watched.
- Taleb closes by siding with Socrates that true virtue goes beyond merely satisfying watchers and can even irritate them, foreshadowing the book's later chapters on virtue.
