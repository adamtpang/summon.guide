# Is the Pope Atheist?

> **Key principle:** Taleb uses the 1981 assassination attempt on Pope John Paul II, who was rushed straight to a top modern hospital rather than to prayer or ritual intercession, to argue that the real test of belief is action: since the pope's emergency-room behavior would be indistinguishable from an equivalent-ranking atheist's, religious commitment has to be measured by deeds and ritual practice, not by professed inner conviction, which is largely unknowable and beside the point.

*Synthesized from Chapter 17 of Skin in the Game by Nassim Nicholas Taleb.*

## Key lessons

- After John Paul II was shot in 1981, he was taken immediately to the Gemelli Polyclinic, a top-tier modern hospital, rather than to a chapel for prayer or ritual intercession first; Taleb stresses that no one in the Vatican has ever seriously proposed reversing that order (prayer first, doctor second), and doing so would in fact be seen as an act of madness, effectively voluntary death, which Catholic doctrine bans.
- Taleb constructs a thought experiment: an equivalent-ranking leader of an "atheist" movement suffering the same emergency would end up at the same hospital, surrounded by well-wishers offering "hope" in secular language, and their actions would be nearly identical to the pope's entourage's, just with less ornate vocabulary and dress.
- He notes the difference between the pope and an atheist counterpart shows up mainly in matters that are not life-threatening: sacrifices like priestly celibacy (which he undercuts by noting several historical popes, notably Alexander VI, fathered children, one in his sixties) or the sheer amount of time spent on prayer and religious ritual.
- Taleb points out that avowed atheists commonly engage in comparable ritualistic behavior of their own (yoga, sitting in reverent silence at concert halls) even while insisting they have no "religion," which supports his claim that ritual behavior, not stated belief, is the real observable data.
- He references the thirteenth-century Albigensian Crusade, where crusaders killed heretics and non-heretics indiscriminately on the reasoning that "the Lord would be able to tell them apart," as an example of belief taken destructively literally, in contrast to how most modern Christians, including himself as an Orthodox Christian, actually behave on medical, ethical, and political decisions, indistinguishably from atheists, having largely accepted secular institutions like democracy over theocracy.
- In "Religious in Words," Taleb proposes defining atheism or religiosity by the gap between a person's actions and those of the opposite type in an equivalent situation, not by stated belief; he sorts people into "atheist in action, religious in words" (most Orthodox and Catholic Christians) and "religious in action, religious in words" (Salafi Islamists, suicide bombers), and says he knows of no one who is atheist in both words and actions, entirely free of ritual, reverence for the dead, or superstition (including secular superstitions like faith in economics or state institutions).
- The chapter closes by setting up the book's next move: rationality lives in what people do, not in what they think or claim to believe, and rationality itself is fundamentally about survival, the themes the following chapters develop.
