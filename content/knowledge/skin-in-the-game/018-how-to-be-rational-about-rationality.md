# How to Be Rational About Rationality

> **Key principle:** Taleb argues that both perception and belief are built for survival and useful action, not for literal accuracy, so judging a belief "irrational" by whether it is scientifically literal (rather than by whether the actions it produces are harmful) is a category error; real rationality is definable only as whatever allows a person, tribe, or species to survive over time.

*Synthesized from Chapter 18 of Skin in the Game by Nassim Nicholas Taleb.*

## Key lessons

- Taleb opens by analogizing religion (and inherited superstition) to his friend Rory Sutherland's observation that swimming pools and fancy restaurants aren't really "about" what they claim to be about; similarly, belief should be judged by the function it serves, not treated as a literal scientific claim. He compares this to human eyes, which aren't built to accurately capture the electromagnetic spectrum but to keep us out of trouble and help us survive.
- In "Ocular Deception," he cites the intentional visual distortions built into Greek and Roman temple architecture, columns tilted inward and floors curved so they appear straight, as Vitruvius describes, to argue that a "distortion" from literal reality can still be functionally correct, exactly as inherited superstitions can be functionally correct even when literally false.
- In "Ergodicity First," Taleb states his organizing claim for the rest of the book: survival comes before truth, understanding, or science, because you need to survive in order to do science at all, not the reverse; he cites Warren Buffett's "to make money you must first survive" and gives an early, informal definition of "ergodic" as a system with no absorbing barrier, no irreversible exit (a fuller technical treatment follows in the next chapter). He also introduces the bias-variance tradeoff (illustrated with two marksmen: one biased but consistently closer to target, one unbiased but high-variance) to argue that accepting a systematic "error" that keeps you away from ruin beats an unbiased method with occasional catastrophic misses.
- In "From Simon to Gigerenzer," Taleb credits Herbert Simon's concept of bounded rationality (we cannot compute and weigh everything like a machine, so evolution gives us shortcuts) and Gerd Gigerenzer's research program on "ecological rationality," which shows that many seemingly illogical heuristics have real underlying justifications once you account for real-world uncertainty and limited information.
- In "Revelation of Preferences," Taleb leans on economist Ken Binmore's argument that the very concept of "rational belief" is close to meaningless; what can be judged rationally is only action, evaluated by evolutionary survival, not stated belief. He restates the economic concept of revealed preferences (tracing to Paul Samuelson): you learn what people actually value from what they spend money and effort on, not from what they say they think, and notes that even psychology experiments require real monetary stakes to be considered scientifically valid.
- In "What Is Religion About?," Taleb proposes that religion's core function is enforcing tail-risk management across generations through simple, binary, easily-taught-and-enforced rules; he cites anthropologist Jared Diamond's account of "constructive paranoia" among Papua New Guinea residents who refuse to sleep under dead trees, arguing it doesn't matter whether the underlying belief is superstition or science, since surviving populations are evidence the rule works.
- In "'Tawk' and Cheap 'Tawk'," Taleb (using Fat Tony's phrase) draws the line that matters: the only real difference between a merely decorative belief and one that functions is revealed by risk taken, not by words, since talk is cheap; how much you truly believe something is shown only by what you're willing to risk for it.
- In "What Does Lindy Say?" and "The Nondecorative in the Decorative," Taleb contrasts the modern, post-Enlightenment idea of "rationality" (assuming a simplified, mostly non-random world) with the older Greek notion of sophrosyne (precaution, self-control, temperance), and proposes his own working definition: rational is whatever aids survival. He then illustrates with the roughly five hundred dietary laws of Jewish kashrut, seemingly arbitrary or "irrational" rules (four sets of dishes, never mixing meat and dairy, avoiding pork and shellfish) that nonetheless correlate with millennia of group survival and cohesion, regardless of whether their original rationale can be reconstructed; his conclusion is that not everything that happens happens for a reason, but everything that survives, survives for a reason, and rationality is, in the end, risk management.
