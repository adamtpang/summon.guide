# How to Legally Own Another Person

> **Key principle:** Taleb argues that firms don't hire employees primarily because it's economically efficient in Coase's transaction-cost sense; they hire them because dependence on a regular paycheck makes a person dependable and controllable, the same function slavery and hostage-taking served in earlier eras, and this control comes at the cost of the employee's real freedom.

*Synthesized from Chapter 3 of Skin in the Game by Nassim Nicholas Taleb.*

## Key lessons

- Taleb opens with the medieval gyrovagues, wandering monks with no institutional affiliation who lived on begging and were eventually banned by church councils (Chalcedon, then Nicaea) precisely because their total freedom from material want made them impossible to control; Saint Benedict's rule-bound, hierarchical monasticism won out instead, because organizations need most of their members to have something to lose.
- The "Bob the pilot" thought experiment: a contractor pilot backs out of a fully booked charter flight at the last minute for a more lucrative offer, nearly bankrupting the airline owner, illustrating that contractors are free precisely because their only real risk is legal penalty, while employees are dependable because their livelihood, routine, and reputation are all on the line every day — employees "sell" their dependability, not just their labor.
- Taleb reframes Ronald Coase's theory of the firm (that firms exist to avoid the transaction costs of constantly renegotiating contracts) as incomplete: Coase missed that an employee is fundamentally a risk-management strategy, functionally similar to how Roman households used slaves rather than free men as household treasurers, because you can threaten a slave (or an employee) with a much larger penalty than the law alone would allow.
- The shift from "company man" (someone like the old IBM employee, whose identity, dress code, and social life were fully absorbed by one loyal-for-life employer) to what Taleb calls the "companies person" — someone no longer owned by one firm but by the more diffuse anxiety of needing to stay "employable" across an entire industry, afraid of alienating not just their current employer but every future one.
- The dog-and-wolf fable (from Ahiqar, later Aesop and La Fontaine): a well-fed dog boasts of its comfortable life to a wolf, until the wolf notices the mark left by the dog's collar and runs away in horror rather than trade its freedom for security — Taleb's image for employees who enjoy security but wear an invisible collar, versus contractors and risk-takers who are hungrier but genuinely free.
- "Loss aversion" as the real mechanism of control: Taleb argues what compels good behavior isn't what a person owns but what they're afraid of losing, illustrating this with Nobel-prize-winning economists who grew visibly anxious about losing a public argument with him, and with the observation that senior officials (he cites David Petraeus) are often more constrained and exposed than an ordinary truck driver because they have so much status left to lose.
- "Wolves among the dogs" describes a small class of employees, mainly traders and top salespeople whose value is judged purely by profit and loss, who behave with the freedom of contractors even while nominally employed, including using deliberately unpolished manners (cursing, refusing ties) as a costly signal of not being dependent on approval — but this freedom lasts only as long as their performance does.
- The chapter closes on "Do Not Rock Bureaucristan": employees and bureaucrats, whose survival depends on satisfying a superior's job assessment rather than reality itself, cannot be trusted with hard decisions or emergencies, illustrated by Taleb's argument that U.S. policy toward Saudi Arabia and the decision to invade Iraq after 9/11 reflected bureaucratic risk-aversion rather than a decision-maker with real skin in the game.
