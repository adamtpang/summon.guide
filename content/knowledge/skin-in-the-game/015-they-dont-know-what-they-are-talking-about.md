# They Don't Know What They Are Talking About When They Talk About Religion

> **Key principle:** Taleb argues that "religion" has meant radically different things across history and cultures, so treating it as one coherent, testable metaphysical claim (as New Atheist-style critics do) is a verbalist mistake; for most practicing people it functions instead as law, tribal identity, aesthetics, ritual, or a code of ethics, and the specific label matters enormously for what kind of thing you're actually criticizing or defending.
>
> He opens the section by warning against "verbalism": people using the same word for different underlying constructs and never noticing, which he contrasts with mathematicians, jurists, and logicians who are forced to define their terms precisely.

*Synthesized from Chapter 15 of Skin in the Game by Nassim Nicholas Taleb.*

## Key lessons

- Taleb's opening warning is against "verbalism," the fool's habit of arguing in loose words rather than precisely defined terms; he credits Socrates with pioneering the method of tripping up sloppy talkers just by asking them to define what they mean, which he says gave birth to philosophy as rigor against the sophists' rhetoric.
- He shows how "religion" itself shifts meaning by tradition: for early Jews and Muslims religion effectively meant law (din means both "law" and "religion" in Hebrew and Arabic); for Romans it meant social ritual and festival (religio versus superstitio); for Orthodox and Catholic Christians it is largely aesthetics, pomp, and ritual; for Protestants it is belief stripped of aesthetics or law; for Buddhists, Shintoists, and Hindus it is a practical, ethical philosophy rather than a creed to be affirmed.
- He traces how early Christianity, via Saint Augustine and Jesus's "give to Caesar what belongs to Caesar," separated the holy from the profane in a way Judaism and Islam never fully did, and notes the Roman legal code under Theodosius stayed substantively pagan Roman law with only a Christian preface grafted on.
- In "Belief vs. Belief," Taleb's central claim of this chapter: belief can be literal/epistemic, or it can be decorative, metaphorical, or purely functional (aiding survival), and confusing these categories is the source of most religion debates. He argues Christianity, Judaism, and Shiite Islam matured partly by moving away from taking their own doctrines literally, quoting Gibbon's line that pagan Roman worship was seen as equally true by the people, equally false by philosophers, and equally useful by the magistrate.
- He gives a concrete failure case: a Salafi fundamentalist assumes an ordinary Christian holds beliefs as literally as he does, while the Christian assumes the Salafi's beliefs are just as metaphorical as his own; the mismatch matters because Taleb classifies Salafism not as an ordinary "religion" the EU can tolerate like a church, but as an intolerant political system that will exploit Western tolerance to spread, paralleling how a minority of committed intolerants can dominate a majority of tolerant people (his "minority rule" concept from earlier in the book).
- In "Libertarianism and Church-Free Religions," Taleb uses the emperor Julian the Apostate's failed attempt to revive paganism (he mistakenly tried to organize it with Christian-style bishops and synods, not realizing paganism was inherently decentralized with no shared category for itself) as a parallel to libertarianism: it cannot function as a hierarchical political party because its members share only a minimal creed (substituting rule of law for rule of authority) and resist any unified party line, yet it still counts as a real belief system in the "religion" sense Taleb is using, without gods or churches.
- The chapter closes by previewing the book's next argument: that despite all this definitional chaos, there is one thing genuine religions share, which is that they demand real commitment rather than costless lip service, i.e., skin in the game.
