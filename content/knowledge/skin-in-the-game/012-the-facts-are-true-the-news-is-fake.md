# The Facts Are True, the News Is Fake

> **Key principle:** Taleb argues that the danger of modern media is not outright lying but selective emphasis: technically true statements assembled into a misleading narrative, a distortion made possible because journalists, unlike traders or restaurant owners, bear none of the cost when they get it wrong.

*Synthesized from Chapter 12 of Skin in the Game by Nassim Nicholas Taleb.*

## Key lessons

- In "How to Disagree with Yourself," Taleb recounts a 2009 public discussion with future U.K. prime minister David Cameron on societal robustness and his precautionary principle, after which London papers seized on a single twenty-second, out-of-context comment and branded him a "climate denier," even though the newspapers had previously praised the same argument in The Black Swan; he forced a correction only through legal threats and his own public platform, noting most people misrepresented this way have no such recourse.
- He generalizes this into an "agency problem": journalists, like a Milan restaurant owner who calls a cousin's inflated taxi meter, or a doctor who misdiagnoses to sell a drug he profits from, have interests that diverge from their readers' interests.
- "Information Doesn't Like to Be Owned" argues that pre-mass-media information transmission (Roman marketplaces, barbers who doubled as news reporters, condolence-call gatherings in the Eastern Mediterranean, coffee houses) was naturally two-way and self-correcting because unreliable sources lost credibility ("you can't fool people more than twice"), whereas centralized twentieth-century media (television, newspapers) could distort information one-way until social media partly restored a two-way flow around the 2016 U.S. election.
- Taleb attributes journalism's persistent agency problem to economic insecurity: because most journalists live paycheck to paycheck and fear ostracism by peers more than they value reader trust, they become vulnerable to manipulation by lobbyists on issues like GMOs or the Syrian war, unlike restaurant owners (per Chapter 8) who answer to customers rather than to each other.
- "The Ethics of Disagreement" distinguishes attacking what someone literally said from attacking what they meant; Taleb calls focusing on an isolated statement, out of its full context, the mark of a charlatan (he names Sam Harris as an example) and traces the discipline of engaging an opponent's real position to Karl Popper (who restated opponents' views fully before dismantling them), Hayek's critiques of Keynes, and the Scholastic method of Aquinas's Summa Theologica.
- He frames the "straw man" as a form of theft, cites the biblical warning in Isaiah 29:21 against ensnaring someone over a single word, and notes that calumny was a capital-equivalent crime in ancient Babylon, contrasting this with the "principle of charity" (interpreting an argument as its author would) which he says only became formalized in philosophy in roughly the last sixty years, a sign, per Lindy reasoning, that older informal norms against misrepresentation had eroded.
- The chapter's title line, "The facts are true, the news is fake," is attributed to Donald Trump, and Taleb notes the irony that Trump made the remark at a press conference that then suffered the same selective, out-of-context reporting Taleb himself experienced with Cameron.
