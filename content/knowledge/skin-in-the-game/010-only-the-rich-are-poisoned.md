# Only the Rich Are Poisoned: The Preferences of Others

> **Key principle:** Taleb argues that wealth strips away the direct, experiential feedback loop that normally disciplines taste, leaving rich people dependent on "experts" and salespeople who profit from selling them complicated, unnatural preferences that make them miserable rather than happy.

*Synthesized from Chapter 10 of Skin in the Game by Nassim Nicholas Taleb.*

## Key lessons

- Taleb opens with an anecdote about a Michelin-starred dinner he was dragged to by a wealthy dinner companion: overdressed, humorless patrons, a parade of tiny, over-engineered dishes he couldn't enjoy, and pretentious sommelier commentary, contrasted with the honest pleasure of a cheap, familiar meal like pizza or a hamburger at a neighborhood taverna.
- The chapter's title concept, "venenum in auro bibitur" (poison is drunk from a golden cup, referencing Seneca's Thyestes), captures Taleb's point: thieves and poisoners target the wealthy, so historically the rich faced hidden dangers ordinary people were spared. He extends this into a broader claim that the rich are prime targets for a different, often worse, category of exploitation: being sold needless complexity by financial consultants, diet gurus, lifestyle coaches, and similar "experts" who thrive on convincing rich clients that simple, cheap, time-tested things (like a good hamburger) are inferior to costly, engineered ones.
- In "Large Funeral Homes," Taleb argues that wealthy people are pressured into buying oversized, isolating mansions instead of staying in warm, close-knit neighborhoods, even though most people are happier in modest, social surroundings; he cites the French moralist Vauvenargues on how affection has scale limits (Rome was easier to love as a small village than as an empire), and notes that truly comfortable, low-profile wealthy people often just keep living in their original, modest homes.
- He suggests that losing money or status (as he predicts could happen to oil-dependent Saudi Arabia) might actually improve well-being if it strips away manipulative "options" that aren't really freeing: his test for whether wealth is working is whether it's expanding your genuine range of choices or just narrowing them under the guise of luxury.
- In "Conversation," Taleb observes that truly rich people often have to hide both their money and their intelligence to keep real friendships, because genuine social conversation (per Castiglione's Book of the Courtier) depends on rough equality between participants; a community, he suggests, is a space where competitive hierarchy is temporarily suspended for the sake of the group.
- In "Nonlinearity of Progress," Taleb generalizes the restaurant example to societal wealth itself: he'd happily pay $200 for pizza and refuse a $6.95 fancy meal for free, illustrating that increased sophistication or spending can produce "negative utility" past a certain point. He proposes that societal wealth follows an inverted-U curve, where growing GDP initially helps people but eventually produces net harm once "constructed preferences" (imposed by salespeople) replace natural ones, and warns that as long as a society keeps getting richer, someone will keep selling it degradation.
