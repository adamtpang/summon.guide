# Apple Stores and the Digital Hub

> **Key principle:** Jobs recruited retail executive Ron Johnson to design Apple's own stores because generic retailers couldn't explain what made Apple's products different, and he built a secret full-scale prototype in a Cupertino warehouse for six months before opening a single real store. In parallel, he decided the personal computer's future was as the organizing "digital hub" for a growing family of digital devices.

*Synthesized from Chapters 29 and 30, "Apple Stores" and "The Digital Hub," of Steve Jobs by Walter Isaacson.*

## Key lessons

- Jobs hired Ron Johnson from Target in 2000 after complaining that big-box clerks cared only about their commission ("spiff") and couldn't explain why Apple's higher price was worth it. Johnson argued Apple's stores should be as large and prominent as its brand deserved, invoking Mike Markkula's old maxim that a company must "impute" its values through everything it touches, including packaging and stores.
- Against board skepticism (Gateway's own stores were failing at the time, and members like Art Levinson and Ed Woolard voiced open doubts), Jobs and Johnson secretly built and rebuilt a full-size prototype store in a Cupertino warehouse for six months, workshopping the layout every Tuesday.
- A late, painful pivot: weeks before launch, Johnson realized the store was wrongly organized around Apple's four product lines rather than around what people wanted to do (edit video, manage photos). Jobs initially erupted — "I've worked my ass off on this store for six months, and now you want to change everything!" — then agreed within minutes and delayed the January 2001 rollout by three or four months to redo it, saying, "We've only got one chance to get it right."
- Jobs insisted on authentic materials over cheaper substitutes, sourcing gray-blue Pietra Serena sandstone from a family quarry outside Florence for store floors (the same stone as Florence's sidewalks) rather than using concrete replicas.
- The "Genius Bar" came from Johnson's team studying Ritz-Carlton service training; Jobs initially called the idea and the name "crazy" ("They're geeks. They don't have the people skills") but had Apple's counsel trademark "Genius Bar" the very next day.
- Skeptics were proven wrong fast: Gateway's stores averaged 250 visitors a week, while Apple's averaged 5,400 per week by 2004, with $1.2 billion in store revenue that year, a record pace for a retail chain to reach $1 billion.
- Jobs's "digital hub" strategy, unveiled around 2001, reimagined the Mac not as an endpoint but as the coordinating device for cameras, camcorders, and eventually music: "the personal computer... would become a 'digital hub' that coordinated a variety of devices." A pivotal grievance drove this: when Adobe refused to build a Mac version of Premiere in 1999, Jobs concluded Apple must never depend on outside software for core functions again, and Apple built its own suite instead — Final Cut Pro, iMovie, iDVD, iPhoto, GarageBand, and iTunes.
- Jobs framed Apple's advantage as end-to-end integration no rival could match: "We're the only company that owns the whole widget — the hardware, the software and the operating system... We can do things that the other guys can't do." Notably, Apple initially "missed the boat" on music (focusing on video and DVD-burning first) and had to "catch up real fast" once it saw the CD-burning trend.
