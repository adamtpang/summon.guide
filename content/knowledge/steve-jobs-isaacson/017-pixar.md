# Pixar

> **Key principle:** Pixar began as a hardware and software acquisition — the Pixar Image Computer and the RenderMan rendering language — that Jobs poured roughly $50 million into while both product lines lost money. The animation shorts John Lasseter made mostly to demo the technology, culminating in the Oscar-winning Tin Toy, turned out to be the actual asset, even though Jobs himself said "life kind of snookered me into doing that."

*Synthesized from Chapter 19, "Pixar," of Steve Jobs by Walter Isaacson.*

## Key lessons

- Alan Kay introduced Jobs to Ed Catmull's computer division at George Lucas's Skywalker Ranch in the summer of 1985; Lucas, in the middle of a divorce, needed to sell it quickly, and after Catmull and colleague Alvy Ray Smith sought an investor rather than a buyer, Jobs negotiated to buy a controlling 70% stake for $10 million while letting Catmull and Smith keep running it.
- Lucas warned Jobs directly that "these guys are hell-bent on animation," and Jobs later admitted, "I think in his heart he bought the company because that was his agenda too" — the January 1986 deal split the remaining 30% among Catmull, Smith, and the other 38 founding employees, down to the receptionist.
- The company took its name from its flagship hardware, the $125,000 Pixar Image Computer, sold mainly to medical imaging and intelligence customers (including the NSA, which required Jobs to get a security clearance); Jobs pushed unsuccessfully for a cheaper, mass-market version designed by Hartmut Esslinger.
- On the software side, Pixar built the Reyes rendering engine and the RenderMan language, which Jobs hoped could become "for everyman" like a spreadsheet program — marketing director Pam Kerwin recalled being persuaded by his whiteboard pitches and then, after he left, asking "What the heck was he thinking!"
- Pixar's most durable software contract was mundane by comparison: the CAPS system built for Disney to computerize hand-drawn animation coloring, first used on The Little Mermaid's final scene in 1988.
- John Lasseter, a Disney-trained animator who'd been fired for pushing too hard on quality, made Luxo Jr. for the 1986 SIGGRAPH conference; it won a standing ovation and best-of-show, and Jobs — thrilled by the fusion of art and technology — became personally committed to funding a new animated short every year even during brutal budget cuts.
- Jobs's worst internal clash was with cofounder Alvy Ray Smith, who saw Jobs as controlling and "like a televangelist"; the two got in a screaming match over control of a whiteboard during a board meeting, and Smith eventually resigned to start a company Microsoft later bought.
- As all three Pixar businesses — hardware, software, and animation — bled money, Jobs ordered deep, immediate layoffs with no severance; Pam Kerwin had to talk him into even two weeks' notice, and he shot back that the notice would be "retroactive from two weeks ago."
- A separate mentoring thread ran through Andy Grove of Intel, who twice pushed back on Jobs's demands (once over an Intel employee, once over Jobs wanting payment for engineering suggestions) — Jobs relented both times, once writing back, "I have many faults, but one of them is not ingratitude."
- In spring 1988, despite an ongoing round of cost-cutting, Jobs agreed to fund Lasseter's next short after watching him pitch the story with full passion; the result, Tin Toy, won the 1988 Academy Award for animated short film, the first computer-generated film to do so, and Jobs's only instruction had been, "All I ask of you, John, is to make it great."
