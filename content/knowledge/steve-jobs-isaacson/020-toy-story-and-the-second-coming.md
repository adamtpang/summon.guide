# Chapters 22 and 23, "Toy Story" and "The Second Coming"

> **Key principle:** Jobs bet Pixar's future on a single movie, undercapitalized and fighting Jeffrey Katzenberg's damaging story notes along the way, then rode Toy Story's success into an IPO that made his stake worth $1.2 billion and let him renegotiate Disney into a real co-branded partnership. Almost simultaneously, NeXT's collapsing hardware business became an unlikely asset: Apple, desperate for a new operating system under floundering CEO Gil Amelio, bought NeXT for roughly $400 million and brought Jobs back inside the company as an "advisor."

*Synthesized from Chapters 22 and 23, "Toy Story" and "The Second Coming," of Steve Jobs by Walter Isaacson.*

## Key lessons

- Jobs pushed Disney's Jeffrey Katzenberg for a real ownership stake rather than a work-for-hire deal, and the May 1991 agreement gave Disney outright ownership of the film and characters but let Pixar keep about 12.5% of ticket revenue and creative involvement — Katzenberg warned, "If that's what you want, we can just quit talking," but conceded the point.
- Katzenberg's insistence on making Woody "edgier" nearly wrecked the film: by November 1993 a Woody who threw toys out windows and called Slinky "spring-wiener" was deemed unwatchable, production halted, and Lasseter's team reworked the script over three months into the Woody-Buzz dynamic that shipped.
- A budget fight followed, with Jobs demanding more money to fix the damage from Katzenberg's notes and Katzenberg insisting Pixar had agreed to a fixed budget — Ed Catmull, more diplomatic than either man, brokered a compromise.
- Toy Story opened in November 1995 to $362 million worldwide and 100% positive reviews on Rotten Tomatoes; Jobs staged a rival San Francisco premiere the next night (Disney's own was in Los Angeles) to stake Pixar's claim as more than a Disney contractor.
- The Pixar IPO came exactly one week after the film's opening; Jobs overruled bankers' caution and priced shares at $22 instead of $14, and the stock spiked to $49 on day one — his 80% stake ended up worth about $1.2 billion, roughly five times what he'd made from Apple's 1980 IPO.
- Using that new leverage, Jobs forced a renegotiated Disney deal: Pixar would fund half of future films' costs in exchange for half the profits and full co-branding, after Jobs threatened to take future films to a rival studio and warned that Disney making unauthorized Toy Story sequels "would have been like molesting our children."
- Meanwhile NeXT kept failing commercially — 1990 revenue of $28 million against Sun Microsystems' $2.5 billion — and Jobs was forced against his instincts to license NeXTSTEP software to other hardware and eventually abandon making NeXT hardware altogether, laying off half the staff and selling the factory to Canon.
- Larry Ellison floated a hostile takeover of Apple with Jobs as restored CEO during a Hawaii walk in December 1995; Jobs declined — "I decided I'm not a hostile-takeover kind of guy" — but by 1996 Apple's market share had fallen to 4% and new CEO Gil Amelio was searching for a replacement operating system.
- Apple staged a head-to-head "bakeoff" on December 10, 1996 between NeXT and Jean-Louis Gassée's company Be; NeXT's polished, complete demo (with Jobs at his most seductive) beat Be's overconfident, unprepared pitch, and Amelio chose NeXT.
- The deal closed at $10 a share (about $400 million), with Jobs personally taking $120 million in cash and $37 million in stock; when Amelio pressed him for a role, Jobs — after a long, tired silence — said only "advisor to the chairman," a title announced to cheering Apple employees on December 20, 1996.
