# NeXT

> **Key principle:** NeXT was Jobs unbound: he recruited five Apple colleagues within weeks of his ouster, triggering an Apple lawsuit; he paid Paul Rand $100,000 for a single, non-negotiable logo; and he chased an absolutely perfect black cube so obsessively that it required custom molds and a $150,000 sanding machine. The 1988 launch was theatrical triumph and commercial disaster in one event — a $6,500 machine, two years late, that would sell about 400 units a month against a factory built for 10,000.

*Synthesized from Chapter 18, "NeXT," of Steve Jobs by Walter Isaacson.*

## Key lessons

- The company was seeded by disgruntled Apple exiles: engineer Rich Page called Jobs after Gassée killed the "Big Mac" workstation project, and over Labor Day weekend 1985 Jobs recruited Bud Tribble, George Crow, Susan Barnes, and university-relations man Dan'l Lewin to join him.
- Jobs told the Apple board on September 13, 1985 that he was starting a new, non-competing company and would take only "low-level people you won't miss"; the board initially offered to invest 10% and keep him on as a director, but when his resignation letter named five senior departures — including an Apple Fellow and the university sales lead — Sculley, Campbell, Markkula, and Arthur Rock all felt lied to and betrayed.
- Apple sued Jobs for breach of fiduciary duty, alleging he'd secretly planned a competing company and lured away key employees while still chairman; Jobs sold nearly all his roughly $100 million in Apple stock (11% of the company), keeping just one share to attend shareholder meetings.
- Jobs paid legendary designer Paul Rand $100,000 for a single logo with no alternate options — "I will solve your problem, and you will pay me... either way you will pay me" — and Rand delivered the tilted cube logo and the "NeXT" name (with the lowercase "e") in two weeks; Jobs hugged him on the spot.
- The out-of-court settlement with Apple (January 1986) restricted NeXT to selling a high-end workstation directly to universities, barred a Mac-compatible OS, and forbade shipping before March 1987 — a deadline the company would blow past by two years.
- Design perfectionism overrode manufacturing sense: Jobs insisted on a flawless magnesium cube with no "draft angles," requiring $650,000 molds cast in Chicago (he personally flew there over one visible seam) and a $150,000 sanding machine, plus expensive plating on screws no one would ever see.
- Texan billionaire Ross Perot invested $20 million for 16% equity after seeing NeXT profiled on PBS — "If you ever need an investor, call me" — and became an effusive public cheerleader, even introducing Jobs to King Juan Carlos of Spain at a Getty dinner ("I sold him a computer," Jobs joked afterward).
- Bill Gates refused to write software for NeXT, calling the prototype "crap" in front of Jobs and publicly declaring "Develop for it? I'll piss on it," setting up a philosophical clash: Jobs's closed hardware-software integration versus Gates's belief that compatibility across vendors would win.
- Jobs briefly pursued IBM as a counterweight to Microsoft, licensing NeXTSTEP to IBM's workstation unit after a chance meeting with CEO John Akers at a Katharine Graham party in 1987 — but the deal lapsed once IBM's negotiator changed and stopped returning Jobs's calls.
- The October 12, 1988 launch at San Francisco's Symphony Hall was pure theater: a black-veiled cube unveiling, a live Bach duet with a violinist, jokes about his own "mercurial" reputation using the machine's built-in dictionary — but the $6,500 price (with a $2,000 printer extra) blindsided his own academic advisors, and the "1.0" software release was pushed indefinitely.
- When the NeXT computer finally shipped in mid-1989, the automated factory was built to produce ten thousand units a month; actual sales ran about four hundred a month, and NeXT continued to burn cash.
