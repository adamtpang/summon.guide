# The iTunes Store

> **Key principle:** "We believe that 80% of the people stealing stuff don't want to be, there's just no legal alternative," Jobs said. Rather than lock music down or ignore piracy, he spent months personally persuading record executives and building the iTunes Store, then designed the iPod around the insight that hardware should stay simple by putting complexity ("Simplify!") into iTunes on the computer instead.

*Synthesized from Chapter 31, "The iTunes Store," of Steve Jobs by Walter Isaacson.*

## Key lessons

- Jon Rubinstein found the enabling hardware, a tiny 1.8-inch, 5-gigabyte Toshiba drive the company didn't yet have plans for, on a routine Japan supplier visit in February 2001; he told Jobs that night, "I know how to do it now. All I need is a $10 million check," and Jobs authorized it immediately.
- At the pivotal April 2001 design meeting, Tony Fadell presented Styrofoam mockups; Jobs rejected a removable-memory-card version and a volatile-RAM version but was intrigued by the hard-drive option. Phil Schiller then produced his own model with a scroll wheel for navigating playlists by thumb; Jobs shouted "That's it!" on the spot.
- Jobs's standing design rule for the iPod's interface: any song or function had to be reachable in three clicks, and each click had to be intuitive. He also insisted the device have no on/off switch at all, a decision that "astonished his colleagues."
- To fund the iTunes Store, Jobs pitched record labels using Apple's tiny Mac market share (about 5%) as a low-risk test case, proposed 99-cent per-song pricing (70 cents to labels) instead of subscriptions, and insisted songs be sold individually rather than bundled into albums, over artist objections such as Trent Reznor's ("There's a flow to a good album").
- Sony proved Jobs's central thesis by failing: it had the Walkman, a top record label, and design talent, but its electronics and music divisions never coordinated, so it could not match Apple's single-company, one-P&L integration. Sony's Andy Lack wanted a per-iPod royalty for music companies; Jobs would privately agree with him then undercut him with rival executives, leading Lack to call him "pathological, which can be useful in negotiations. And he's a genius."
- The iTunes Store launched April 28, 2003, and sold a million songs in six days (versus a six-month projection); Apple later ported iTunes to Windows in October 2003 despite Jobs's initial resistance ("over my dead body"), reversing course only after his own executives (Schiller, Rubinstein, Robbin, Fadell) built a spreadsheet proving no Mac cannibalization scenario outweighed iPod sales gains.
- Jobs refused to license Apple's FairPlay copy-protection format to other device makers, unlike Microsoft's more open licensing approach; when RealNetworks reverse-engineered FairPlay for its "Harmony" service in 2004, Jobs called it hacker tactics and released an iPod software update that broke Harmony-purchased songs.
- The iPod Mini (2004) and iPod Shuffle (2005, built around Jobs's idea to remove the screen entirely and rely on random shuffle) extended Apple's dominance; by 2007 iPod sales were half of Apple's revenue, and the iTunes Store had sold its billionth song by February 2006.
