# Design Principles

> **Key principle:** "Simplicity isn't just a visual style," Ive said. "It's not just minimalism or the absence of clutter. It involves digging through the depth of the complexity... You have to deeply understand the essence of a product in order to be able to get rid of the parts that are not essential."

*Synthesized from Chapter 26, "Design Principles," of Steve Jobs by Walter Isaacson.*

## Key lessons

- Jony Ive, then Apple's design head and about to quit over Gil Amelio's profit-first culture, decided to stay after Jobs's first pep talk as iCEO: "I remember very clearly Steve announcing that our goal is not just to make money but to make great products."
- Both men were influenced by Dieter Rams's Braun-era maxim "Less but better" (Weniger aber besser); Jobs traced Apple's own version to its first brochure line, "Simplicity is the ultimate sophistication," and said achieving it "takes a lot of hard work... to truly understand the underlying challenges and come up with elegant solutions."
- Jobs described Ive as close to a co-founder of ideas: "If I had a spiritual partner at Apple, it's Jony... Jony and I think up most of the products together." He gave Ive more operational power than anyone else at Apple except himself, reporting directly to Jobs rather than through the normal hierarchy.
- Jobs told Fortune that design was misunderstood: "In most people's vocabularies, design means veneer. But to me, nothing could be further from the meaning of design. Design is the fundamental soul of a man-made creation."
- A shared story crystallized their philosophy: in a French kitchen supply store, both men independently rejected a well-designed knife the instant they noticed a tiny bead of glue between the handle and blade — manufacturing carelessness ruining an otherwise pure design.
- Before Jobs returned, Apple's process was engineer-driven ("Here are the guts... then it would go to the designers to put it in a box," per marketing chief Phil Schiller); Jobs reversed that, having engineering serve the design intent instead, though this sometimes backfired, as with the iPhone 4's antenna issues from an all-aluminum edge.
- Ive's design studio, where Jobs would wander most afternoons, had no formal design reviews; ideas iterated daily through physical foam models and prototypes rather than presentations, because, as Ive put it, "he doesn't like to read complex drawings. He wants to see and feel a model."
- Jobs is listed as an inventor on 212 U.S. patents as of early 2011, including ones for the MacBook's power brick, its magnetic connector, and even product packaging (such as the iPod Nano box) — evidence of how far his and Ive's obsession with design extended past the visible product itself.
