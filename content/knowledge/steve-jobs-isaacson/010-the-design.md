# The Design

> **Key principle:** At the 1981 Aspen design conference Jobs discovered the Bauhaus maxim "less is more" and abandoned Sony's dark high-tech look for clean, simple, mass-producible forms. He carried that obsession into the Mac's physical case, its onscreen graphics, its typography, and even its internal circuit board — insisting that beauty had to be "carried all the way through," seen or not.

*Synthesized from Chapter 12, "The Design," of Steve Jobs by Walter Isaacson.*

## Key lessons

- Jobs's love of clean industrial design traced back to his father's admiration for the styling of cars and to the Eichler tract homes he grew up in; he studied Sony's brochures obsessively before pivoting at Aspen in 1981 toward Bauhaus principles ("God is in the details," "Less is more") preached by Herbert Bayer, Walter Gropius, and Mies van der Rohe.
- In a 1983 design-conference talk he explicitly rejected "Sony's high-tech look... gunmetal gray" in favor of products that were "bright and pure and honest about being high-tech," aiming for what he called Museum of Modern Art quality: "Let's make it simple. Really simple."
- The case design went through endless plaster-model iterations with Jerry Manock and Terry Oyama; Jobs wanted it to look like a Porsche or a friendly human face, complained about "the radius of the first chamfer" and "the size of the bevel," and studied a Cuisinart at Macy's for inspiration.
- When Bill Atkinson demoed a fast circle-drawing algorithm, Jobs demanded rounded-corner rectangles too; Atkinson thought them nearly impossible, but Jobs walked him around the block pointing out seventeen real-world examples (windows, signs, a No Parking sign) until Atkinson agreed — rounded rectangles became a fixture of the Mac and Lisa interface.
- Font design (led by Susan Kare, recruited by Hertzfeld) drew on Jobs's Reed College calligraphy class; he renamed the initial Philadelphia-suburb-themed fonts ("little cities nobody's ever heard of") to world-class cities: Chicago, New York, Geneva, San Francisco.
- Chris Espinosa built "The Steve Jobs Roll Your Own Calculator Construction Set" after enduring endless nitpicking on a calculator app, letting Jobs directly tweak line thickness and button size himself — the version Jobs settled on in ten minutes shipped on the Mac and stayed standard for fifteen years.
- Jobs hired German designer Hartmut Esslinger (codename "Snow White") to create a unified "California global" design language across Apple's product line, resulting in the white, rounded, thin-grooved look first used on the Apple IIc and the "Designed in California" label.
- His father's carpentry lesson — "you're not going to use a piece of plywood on the back... even though nobody will ever see it" — drove him to demand a beautifully laid-out printed circuit board no consumer would ever view, and to have the whole Mac team sign a sheet of paper whose signatures were engraved, unseen, inside every Macintosh.
