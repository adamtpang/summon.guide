# Gates and Jobs

> **Key principle:** Isaacson frames Gates and Jobs as a "binary star system" defining the personal computer age: Gates methodical, practical, and open to licensing widely; Jobs intuitive, controlling, and committed to tightly integrated hardware and software. Their Macintosh-era partnership — Microsoft building Excel, Word, and BASIC for the Mac — curdled into open conflict once Gates revealed Windows, a graphical interface Jobs considered stolen.

*Synthesized from Chapter 16, "Gates and Jobs," of Steve Jobs by Walter Isaacson.*

## Key lessons

- The two, both born in 1955, came from opposite backgrounds: Gates the Seattle lawyer's son at Lakeside prep school who wrote a class-scheduling program (angling for the right girls' classes) versus Jobs the countercultural dropout; Hertzfeld observed "Steve generally treated Bill as someone who was slightly inferior... Bill looked down on Steve because he couldn't actually program."
- Jobs pitched Gates on writing software for the Mac by describing an automated California factory pumping out computers — Microsoft's team code-named the secret project "Sand," reverse-engineered as an acronym for "Steve's amazing new device."
- Gates was unimpressed by early demos ("That was the only app that ran") and put off by Jobs's sales style ("kind of the sales mode that also says, 'I don't need you, but I might let you be involved'"), while Mac engineer Bruce Horn concluded Gates couldn't "understand or appreciate the elegance of a Macintosh" after Gates assumed the flicker-free cursor needed special hardware.
- Jobs made a secret trade: Microsoft would build Excel exclusively for the Mac for two years (skipping IBM PCs) in exchange for Apple killing its own in-house BASIC project and licensing Microsoft's instead — a deal that "infuriated the Apple team" and handed Microsoft future leverage.
- Gates witnessed the reality distortion field firsthand: after Gates told an Apple team dinner that 600 Xerox Stars had sold, Jobs told the same room the next day it was 300, and "his whole team starts looking at me like, 'Are you going to tell him that he's full of shit?'" — Gates stayed quiet.
- In November 1983 Gates announced Windows, a graphical OS for IBM PCs, at a lavish Helmsley Palace event; Jobs summoned him to Cupertino and shouted "You're ripping us off! I trusted you, and now you're stealing from us!" in front of ten Apple staff.
- Gates's famous rejoinder: "I think it's more like we both had this rich neighbor named Xerox and I broke into his house to steal the TV set and found out that you had already stolen it" — delivered coolly while Jobs alternated between fury and, per Gates, "almost crying."
- Windows 1.0 shipped in 1985 as an inferior, tiled-window product that reviewers mocked, but persistence eventually made it dominant; courts later sided with Gates legally, and Jobs never let go of the grievance, telling Isaacson decades later that Microsoft "just have no taste... they don't bring much culture into their product."
