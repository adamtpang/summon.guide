# The Restoration

> **Key principle:** Jobs returned to Apple in January 1997 nominally as an unpaid part-time advisor, but within months he had installed Avie Tevanian and Jon Rubinstein in top posts, killed the Newton politically, told board chair Ed Woolard that Gil Amelio was "the worst CEO I've ever seen," and forced the entire board except Woolard to resign — all before the board officially fired Amelio in July 1997 and Jobs, still refusing the CEO title, effectively took control.

*Synthesized from Chapter 24, "The Restoration," of Steve Jobs by Walter Isaacson.*

## Key lessons

- At the January 1997 Macworld, Amelio delivered a rambling, three-hour keynote that embarrassed the crowd; when Jobs was finally brought onstage he got a minute-long standing ovation and declared, "We've got to get the spark back... The Mac didn't progress much in ten years. So Windows caught up."
- Jobs immediately placed loyalists in key roles: Avie Tevanian to run software engineering and Jon Rubinstein (pulled off vacation on the Isle of Skye) to run hardware, sidelining chief technologist Ellen Hancock, who had favored Sun's Solaris over NeXT's software.
- He pushed hard to kill the Newton handheld — "You ought to kill Newton... It doesn't matter what it costs. People will cheer you" — but Amelio resisted at first, and the division was only spun off starting that May.
- By late February, Financial Times reporter Louise Kehoe was already writing that "Mr. Jobs has become the power behind the throne," describing him as directing which parts of Apple to cut while formally holding no executive title.
- After watching Amelio bomb at the shareholders' meeting, board chair Ed Woolard called Jobs directly and asked his opinion; Jobs decided to tell the truth rather than lie by omission: "This guy is the worst CEO I've ever seen... if you needed a license to be a CEO he wouldn't get one."
- Larry Ellison kept publicly floating a hostile takeover with Jobs as CEO, at one point claiming he'd raise $1 billion; Jobs never denied it publicly, which further destabilized Amelio even as Jobs told reporters "my role at Apple is to be an advisor."
- Jobs secretly sold 1.5 million Apple shares once his required six-month holding period ended in June 1997, then denied it to Amelio directly before an SEC filing exposed the truth — a pattern Isaacson frames as part genuine secrecy, part the same willingness to bend truth that also let him deliver brutal honesty when it suited him.
- Woolard privately calculated the board's odds for an executive session: 10% survival chance keeping Amelio, 60% if they fired him and got Jobs to return, 40% if they fired him and searched for someone else — the board authorized Woolard to ask Jobs back, but Jobs again declined the CEO title, agreeing only to be an unpaid advisor and board member.
- After Amelio was fired in July 1997, Jobs used his leverage to force nearly the entire board to resign (keeping only Woolard and one other director), reprice underwater employee stock options over board objections, and recruit new directors including Larry Ellison and Bill Campbell; he also personally asked longtime mentor Mike Markkula to step down.
- The chapter closes with the August 1997 Boston Macworld keynote, where Jobs — still officially "chairman and CEO of Pixar" — announced a landmark truce with Microsoft: a $150 million investment, a commitment to keep making Mac Office, and Internet Explorer as the default browser, undercut by a widely panned staging gaffe (Bill Gates's face looming on a giant screen, evoking the "1984" ad); Apple's stock still jumped 33% in a single day.
