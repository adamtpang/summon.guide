# Pixar's Friends

> **Key principle:** Jobs behaved differently at Pixar than at Apple, restraining his need for creative control and deferring to Lasseter, but his relationship with Michael Eisner deteriorated over a stolen-idea grievance (Antz vs. A Bug's Life) and a leaked disparaging email, until incoming CEO Bob Iger candidly told Jobs Disney needed Pixar and the two negotiated Disney's $7.4 billion acquisition of Pixar in 2006.

*Synthesized from Chapter 33, "Pixar's Friends," of Steve Jobs by Walter Isaacson.*

## Key lessons

- Jobs let Pixar be Lasseter's domain: "the tone at each place was set at the top, by Jobs at Apple, but by Lasseter at Pixar." Jobs's main contribution was deal-making, where his intensity was an asset rather than a liability.
- The Antz/A Bug's Life feud: Jobs accused Jeffrey Katzenberg of stealing Pixar's insect-movie idea after leaving Disney for DreamWorks. Lasseter had, in fact, described the concept to Katzenberg directly in a casual visit; Katzenberg later admitted he rushed Antz into production specifically to force Disney to change A Bug's Life's release date, calling it retaliation, not theft. A Bug's Life still outgrossed Antz roughly two to one worldwide.
- Jobs commissioned architect Peter Bohlin to build Pixar's Emeryville headquarters around a single central atrium (over Lasseter's initial preference for a traditional bungalow-style studio), deliberately forcing "random encounters": "Creativity comes from spontaneous meetings, from random discussions." He even insisted on only two shared bathrooms for the whole building, provoking a real internal fight before a compromise was reached.
- The Eisner relationship broke down publicly after Eisner criticized Apple's "Rip. Mix. Burn." iTunes ads before a Senate committee in 2002, then, months later, sent a board email dismissing the yet-unreleased Finding Nemo as inferior to Pixar's earlier films — an email that leaked to the Los Angeles Times right before Nemo became Pixar and Disney's biggest hit ever ($868 million worldwide).
- Jobs made Eisner a deliberately one-sided renewal offer (Pixar keeping full ownership of its own films and characters, paying Disney only a 7.5% distribution fee) that was effectively designed for rejection, then in January 2004 publicly announced he was cutting off negotiations, calling Disney's recent animated films "embarrassing duds."
- Bob Iger, who succeeded Eisner as CEO in 2005, reopened talks by openly admitting Disney's weakness rather than negotiating from strength: Jobs recalled, "He just blurted it out... 'We're screwed.' I immediately liked the guy, because that's how I worked too." A successful trial run, licensing ABC shows Desperate Housewives and Lost to the new video iPod, built trust between them within a week.
- Iger's own internal analysis (prompted by realizing at a Hong Kong Disneyland parade that every recent parade character had come from Pixar, not Disney) concluded Disney animation had lost money for a decade and needed Pixar to survive; he pitched the board on acquiring Pixar outright rather than negotiating another distribution deal.
- Eisner, no longer CEO, tried to derail the $7.4 billion deal at a Disney board meeting, arguing Disney already owned 85% of Pixar's value through existing distribution rights; the board rejected his argument and approved the acquisition. Ed Catmull became head of the combined animation unit, Lasseter its chief creative officer, and Jobs became Disney's largest individual shareholder at roughly 7% of the company.
