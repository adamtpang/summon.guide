# Icarus

> **Key principle:** The chapter tracks a full arc from triumph to expulsion: Jobs merges the Lisa and Macintosh divisions and purges "B players," obsesses over a factory and an ad campaign while Mac sales collapse from underpowered hardware, loses a string of loyal engineers to his own cruelty, and finally loses a direct board vote to Sculley during a coup attempt he staged while Sculley was traveling to China.

*Synthesized from Chapter 17, "Icarus," of Steve Jobs by Walter Isaacson.*

## Key lessons

- Fresh off the Macintosh launch, Jobs merged the Lisa and Macintosh divisions under himself and told the combined teams bluntly, "You guys failed... You're a B team. B players," laying off a quarter of the Lisa staff — the incident that crystallized his stated philosophy that "A players like to work only with other A players," so a leader can't indulge B players.
- Sculley's first-anniversary toast to Jobs — "Apple has one leader, Steve and me" — read as a warning sign to Arthur Rock and other directors watching Jobs's face; Rock later said, "Sculley was so eager for Steve's approval that he was unable to stand up to him."
- Jobs's controlling perfectionism turned destructive at the new Fremont Macintosh factory: he demanded machines be repainted in bright colors (one, dubbed "Steve's folly," broke and never worked right), drove manufacturing director Matt Carter to quit, and told successor Debi Coleman he wanted the floor clean enough to eat off of.
- His behavior abroad worsened: he shut a car door on his assistant Joanna Hoffman to skip a scheduled dinner with French developers, coldly told Apple's Italian manager "you don't deserve to sell the Mac," and needed Jean-Louis Gassée to physically grab his lapel and threaten him before he'd back off inflated sales-forecast demands — Gassée called it being an "assaholic."
- Sales cratered in the second half of 1984 because of Jobs's own design dogma: 128K of RAM (versus the Lisa's 1,000K), a single floppy drive with no hard disk, and no cooling fan, which caused component failures and earned the machine the nickname "the beige toaster."
- Desperate at year-end 1984, Jobs authorized rebranding unsold Lisa inventory as the "Macintosh XL," a product he didn't believe in; marketing lead Debi Coleman resigned in protest of the "Lemmings" Super Bowl ad depicting blindfolded managers marching off a cliff, which flopped with the public.
- A wave of key engineers quit rather than keep absorbing Jobs's abuse: Andy Hertzfeld left after a bonus dispute and a blunt "you don't matter as much as you think you do" from Jobs; Burrell Smith and Bruce Horn resigned soon after. Steve Wozniak also quietly departed the Apple II division, telling reporters "Apple's direction has been horrendously wrong for five years," and Jobs retaliated by blocking frogdesign from working on Wozniak's new remote-control startup.
- The board delivered a joint warning to both men in early 1985, and as Mac sales stayed at roughly 10% of forecast, marketing chief Mike Murray privately urged Sculley to strip Jobs of the Macintosh division and later wrote a memo accusing Jobs of running an "impenetrable power base" amid "confusion, fear, and dysfunction."
- At the April 11 board meeting, Sculley asked for authority to remove Jobs from operations; Arthur Rock — fed up with both men — told Jobs bluntly he'd been "acting like a petulant brat," and the board unanimously backed Sculley, giving him authority to act whenever he judged the timing right.
- Jobs plotted an open coup during Sculley's planned Memorial Day 1985 trip to China, telling colleagues "I'm going to launch a coup while John is in China," but Gassée warned Sculley at a barbecue at counsel Al Eisenstat's house, and Sculley canceled his trip to confront Jobs directly.
- At the May 24 executive staff meeting, Sculley polled the room — "it's me or Steve" — and one by one Del Yocam, Al Eisenstat, Regis McKenna, and Bill Campbell all sided with Sculley; Jobs walked out in tears. Sculley's wife then confronted Jobs in a restaurant parking lot: "When I look into your eyes, I see a bottomless pit, an empty hole, a dead zone."
- After a failed reconciliation walk in the Stanford hills on May 26 and Mike Markkula's refusal to back a counter-revolt, the board formally stripped Jobs of all operating authority on May 28, leaving only a chairman's box on the org chart "connected to no lines"; Jobs called it feeling "punched, the air knocked out of me," and named the loss of Arthur Rock's support as especially painful — "I never thought he would abandon me."
