# Atari and India

> **Key principle:** After eighteen months drifting around Reed, Jobs answered an Atari want ad ("Have fun, make money"), refused to leave the lobby until hired, and was put on the night shift to keep him away from coworkers who complained about his hygiene and abrasiveness. He then convinced Atari founder Nolan Bushnell and engineer Al Alcorn to fund a trip to Europe and India in search of a guru, and came back seven months later with a shaved head, dysentery-thinned body, and a firmer conviction that intuitive, experiential wisdom mattered more than intellectual analysis.

*Synthesized from Chapter 4, "Atari and India," of Steve Jobs by Walter Isaacson.*

## Key lessons

- Atari founder Nolan Bushnell, known for driving a Rolls-Royce and holding meetings in a hot tub, hired Jobs as a $5-an-hour technician despite his unkempt appearance; when engineer Don Lang complained Jobs was "a goddamn hippie with b.o." and impossible to work with, Bushnell solved it by assigning Jobs to the night shift rather than firing him.
- Jobs's fruit-heavy diet led him to believe, incorrectly, that he didn't need deodorant or regular showers, a recurring friction point throughout his early career. Despite the friction, Bushnell liked him: "Steve was prickly, but I kind of liked him," and enjoyed debating free will versus determinism with him.
- Jobs befriended Atari draftsman Ron Wayne, who had already started (and failed at) his own company; Wayne became the first person Jobs knew personally who had tried starting a business, planting an idea that would matter within the year.
- Wanting to fund a spiritual trip to India that Robert Friedland was urging him toward, Jobs told Atari he was quitting to find a guru; Al Alcorn instead offered to pay his way to Europe to fix a PAL/NTSC interference bug in Atari's Munich-built machines, on the logic it would be "cheaper to get to India from there."
- In Munich, Jobs solved the technical problem but alienated the German managers with his appearance and rudeness; in Turin, by contrast, he had "a wonderful couple of weeks" being treated to unmenued Italian dinners by Atari's distributor.
- In New Delhi, Jobs contracted severe dysentery from unfiltered water at a cheap hotel, dropping from 160 to 120 pounds in about a week. He recovered partway at the Kumbh Mela festival in Haridwar (over ten million attendees) before heading to the Himalayan foothills near Nainital, only to find that the guru he sought, Neem Karoli Baba, had already died.
- A young Hindu holy man at a Himalayan gathering singled Jobs out of the crowd, called him "just like a baby," and then shaved his head with a straight razor, telling him he was "saving my health." Jobs met Daniel Kottke in India and, when Kottke's sleeping bag and traveler's checks were stolen, covered his food and travel expenses and gave him his remaining $100.
- Jobs returned to his parents so transformed in appearance (shaved head, sun-darkened skin, Indian robes) that they walked past him several times at the airport before recognizing him. He later described the trip's real lesson as an appreciation for Indian villagers' intuition over Western intellect: "Intuition is a very powerful thing, more powerful than intellect, in my opinion. That's had a big impact on my work."
- Back in California, Jobs found a steady teacher close to home, Kobun Chino Otogawa of the Los Altos Zen Center, meeting with him almost daily; seventeen years later Kobun would officiate Jobs's wedding. Jobs also underwent Arthur Janov's primal scream therapy at the Oregon Feeling Center (run out of a hotel managed by Robert Friedland), driven partly by unresolved feelings about his adoption; he later called it "too oversimplistic," though friends said it left him calmer and more confident for a while.
- When Jobs returned to Atari in early 1975 wearing a saffron robe and asking for his old job back, Alcorn rehired him on the spot. Later that year, Bushnell tasked Jobs with designing a single-player Pong variant (Breakout) in four days with a per-chip bonus for minimizing components; Jobs secretly enlisted Wozniak, who did the design work over four sleepless nights using only forty-five chips, but Jobs later paid Wozniak only half the base fee and did not disclose or share the chip-reduction bonus, a discrepancy Wozniak did not learn about for a decade and that became a lasting sore point between them.
- Bushnell later described the Atari period as formative for Jobs's business style: "I told him, 'Pretend to be completely in control and people will assume that you are.'"
