# The Apple II

> **Key principle:** Realizing personal computers needed to come as a complete, ready-to-run package rather than a hobbyist kit, Jobs assembled the pieces the Apple I lacked: Jerry Manock's molded plastic case, Rod Holt's fanless switching power supply, Mike Markkula's business plan and $250,000 credit line for a one-third equity stake, and Regis McKenna's branding (including the bitten-apple logo). The resulting Apple II, launched at the April 1977 West Coast Computer Faire, sold close to six million units over sixteen years and made Apple a real company, complete with a hired president, Mike Scott, brought in specifically to manage Jobs.

*Synthesized from Chapter 6, "The Apple II," of Steve Jobs by Walter Isaacson.*

## Key lessons

- Jobs's insistence on an integrated, ready-to-run machine ("My vision was to create the first fully packaged computer") was validated when the hotel technician who watched a secret late-night Apple II demo at the Atlantic City computer festival said it was the one he'd buy.
- Atari's president Joe Keenan threw Jobs out of a pitch meeting over his bare feet on the desk, and Commodore's leadership, after getting a demo and an offer to buy Apple for "a few hundred thousand dollars," declined and instead rushed out its own inferior competitor, the Commodore PET, which Wozniak called "a real crappy product."
- The Commodore episode exposed tension over whether Jobs and Wozniak were equal partners; Wozniak's father Jerry confronted Jobs directly ("You don't deserve shit... you haven't produced anything"), reducing Jobs to tears, but Wozniak chose to keep the 50-50 partnership, recognizing Jobs's role in turning his designs into a business.
- Jobs rejected Ron Wayne's utilitarian Plexiglas case design in favor of a sleek molded-plastic look inspired by Cuisinart food processors, hiring consultant Jerry Manock for $1,500. He also insisted, over Wozniak's objection, on limiting expansion slots to two rather than eight, an early instance of Jobs privileging a seamless closed system over hacker-style extensibility (Wozniak won that particular argument).
- Rod Holt, a "chain-smoking Marxist" engineer Jobs recruited via Al Alcorn, designed a switching power supply that cycled thousands of times per second instead of sixty, eliminating the need for a fan; Jobs later said, "Every computer now uses switching power supplies, and they all rip off Rod's design."
- Venture capitalist Don Valentine declined to invest but referred Jobs to Mike Markkula, a 33-year-old retired Intel/Fairchild millionaire, who wrote the actual Apple business plan (Jobs kept missing his own deadlines), personally guaranteed a $250,000 line of credit for a one-third equity stake, and predicted "We're going to be a Fortune 500 company in two years." Markkula's one-page "Apple Marketing Philosophy" (empathy, focus, and "impute") shaped Jobs's approach to product and packaging for the rest of his career.
- Wozniak nearly refused to leave his secure HP job to join Apple full time; Jobs cried, pleaded, and enlisted Wozniak's parents before family friend Allen Baum convinced Wozniak he could join without becoming a manager, letting him "stay at the bottom of the organization chart, as an engineer."
- Publicist Regis McKenna, whose Intel ad campaigns had impressed Jobs, initially clashed with a prickly Wozniak but agreed to represent Apple; his team, led by art director Rob Janoff, replaced Ron Wayne's ornate Victorian logo with the simple bitten-apple mark, choosing the bite specifically so it wouldn't be mistaken for a cherry, under the maxim "Simplicity is the ultimate sophistication."
- At the April 1977 West Coast Computer Faire launch, Jobs paid $5,000 for a prime booth, dressed the display in black velvet with backlit Plexiglas signage, padded out the display with empty boxes to look better-stocked, and put himself and Wozniak in ill-fitting three-piece suits at Markkula's insistence; Apple took three hundred orders.
- Markkula brought in Mike Scott as president specifically to rein in the 22-year-old Jobs, whose temper, hygiene complaints, and perfectionism (fighting over Pantone beige shades, insisting on a one-year warranty against Scott's preferred ninety days, demanding badge #1 over Wozniak) were becoming disruptive; Jobs later said, "I never yelled at anyone more than I yelled at Scotty."
- The Apple II's success was cemented by early developer VisiCalc, the first spreadsheet program, which for a time ran only on the Apple II and made the machine credible for business use; investor Arthur Rock, initially unimpressed by Jobs's appearance and smell, joined the board after evaluating the machine itself.
