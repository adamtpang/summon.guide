# Music Man

> **Key principle:** Jobs's iPod library, heavy on Dylan and the Beatles with almost nothing after the mid-1970s, revealed how thoroughly his own musical taste, formed in the sixties and seventies, shaped the emotional register of Apple's marketing and his personal relationships with musicians like Bono, Dylan, and Yo-Yo Ma.

*Synthesized from Chapter 32, "Music Man," of Steve Jobs by Walter Isaacson.*

## Key lessons

- Jobs's iPod held all six volumes of Dylan's bootleg series plus fifteen more Dylan albums, but stopped at 1989's Oh Mercy; he argued that little after Blood on the Tracks (1975) matched Dylan's early work. The Beatles filled seven albums, the Rolling Stones six (but only a few cuts each, since Jobs believed in disaggregating most albums except Dylan's and the Beatles').
- Asked which he'd save from a fire, the Beatles or the Stones, Jobs said Beatles, and framed Dylan and the Beatles as irreplaceable: "Somebody else could have replicated the Stones. No one could have been Dylan or the Beatles."
- He described being "tongue-tied" only around Dylan, whom he met backstage in 2004 while recovering from his first cancer surgery; Dylan told him of songwriting, "They just came through me... That doesn't happen anymore, I just can't write them that way anymore. But I still can sing them."
- A bootleg of the Beatles workshopping "Strawberry Fields Forever" through multiple takes became, in Jobs's own words, "the musical score to his philosophy" of iterative perfectionism: "They kept sending it back to make it closer to perfect... The way we build stuff at Apple is often this way."
- Bono personally pitched Jobs on U2 appearing, unpaid, in an iPod ad for "Vertigo," an unusual reversal since artists traditionally were paid for commercials; the deal nearly collapsed twice over a special black-and-red iPod and royalty terms before James Vincent and Jony Ive (sent to Dublin to show Bono the device in person) rescued it.
- Getting the Beatles onto iTunes took decades, tangled in a 1978 trademark lawsuit settlement (Apple Computer paid Apple Corps $80,000, with each side agreeing to stay out of the other's business) that string Apple's later music ventures kept violating; it was only resolved in 2007 when Apple paid Apple Corps $500 million for the Apple name rights.
- Yo-Yo Ma was one of the few classical artists Jobs revered as a person; on one visit to the Jobs home, Ma played Bach on his 1733 Stradivarius, prompting Jobs to say his playing was "the best argument I've ever heard for the existence of God." Jobs later asked Ma to play at his funeral.
- Jobs's musical loyalty had limits: he found rap largely inaccessible (though Dr. Dre praised the iTunes/iPod integration), was ambivalent about John Mayer's discipline despite liking him personally, and used Joni Mitchell's two widely spaced recordings of "Both Sides Now" the way he referenced Glenn Gould's two "Goldberg Variations" recordings, as a lens for reflecting on aging.
