# Xerox and Lisa, and Going Public

> **Key principle:** Jobs traded Xerox a chance to buy 100,000 shares of pre-IPO Apple stock for full access to Xerox PARC's research, and after seeing its bitmapped, mouse-driven graphical interface in December 1979, declared "We've got to do it!" and redirected the Lisa project (named, he eventually admitted, after his daughter) around it. His hands-on meddling on Lisa got him stripped of operational control in a September 1980 boardroom reorganization by Markkula and Scott, just months before Apple's oversubscribed December 1980 IPO made him a multimillionaire at twenty-five and revealed the harder edges of his loyalty, most starkly in his refusal to give Daniel Kottke any stock options.

*Synthesized from Chapters 8 and 9, "Xerox and Lisa" and "Going Public," of Steve Jobs by Walter Isaacson.*

## Key lessons

- After the Apple III shipped in May 1980 and flopped due to overheating and connector failures caused partly by Jobs's rigid case design, Jobs sought a machine that would be entirely his own rather than seen as "Wozniak's masterpiece," landing on the Lisa project.
- Jobs negotiated Xerox's venture capital arm buying $1 million in Apple stock (worth $17.6 million by the IPO) in exchange for open access to Xerox PARC's research; engineer Adele Goldberg initially resisted showing Apple the full technology, calling it "incredibly stupid, completely nuts," but a call to Xerox's Connecticut headquarters overruled her.
- The December 1979 demo of Smalltalk and the bitmapped, mouse-driven graphical interface stunned Jobs's team; he called it "like a veil being lifted from my eyes" and told Bill Atkinson on the drive back, "This is it! We've got to do it!"
- Apple's engineers meaningfully improved on what they saw: they commissioned IDEO to redesign Xerox's clunky $300, three-button mouse into a $15 single-button model, and added capabilities Xerox's own Alto and Star never had, like dragging windows and dropping files into folders. Xerox's own 1981 commercial product, the Star, flopped at $16,595 with only 30,000 sold, demonstrating that ideas alone weren't enough. Jobs summarized the whole episode with a line he attributed to Picasso: "good artists copy, great artists steal."
- Programmer Bill Atkinson became central to translating the PARC ideas into Lisa, building overlapping draggable windows through complex "regions" coding he wasn't sure was possible ("I got a feeling for the empowering aspect of naïveté... because I didn't know it couldn't be done, I was enabled to do it") and pushing, with Jobs's backing, for a white WYSIWYG screen background over hardware engineers' objections.
- Jobs's direct, out-of-channel calls to Lisa engineers at 2 a.m. and his open contempt for "buttoned-down HP types" like Lisa division head John Couch created enough friction that Markkula and Mike Scott secretly reorganized the company in September 1980, putting Couch in undisputed charge of Lisa and making Jobs non-executive chairman with no operating control. Jobs said, "I was upset and felt abandoned by Markkula... I brooded about it a lot."
- Apple's December 12, 1980 IPO, priced at $22 and closing its first day at $29, was the most oversubscribed offering since Ford's 1956 IPO, valuing Apple at $1.79 billion and creating roughly three hundred new millionaires. Jobs, 25, was suddenly worth $256 million.
- Daniel Kottke, Jobs's close friend from Reed and India through the Apple garage years, received no stock options because he was classified as an hourly technician rather than a salaried engineer; Jobs refused to override this even when engineer Rod Holt offered to personally match any options Jobs contributed, and Jobs simply replied, "I will give him zero." Wozniak, by contrast, sold cut-rate options to dozens of midlevel employees and later gave shares outright to Kottke and others, prompting a coworker's joke poster reading "Woz in 1990" over a United Way ad.
- Jobs made sure his child-support settlement with Chrisann Brennan was finalized before the IPO so it wouldn't complicate the offering, while his own reflections on newfound wealth stressed that he never let money define his life: "I made a promise to myself that I'm not going to let this money ruin my life." His biggest personal gift was roughly $750,000 in stock to his parents, who used it only to pay off their mortgage.
- Fame followed quickly: Inc. put him on its cover in October 1981 ("This man has changed business forever"), and Time profiled him in February 1982 as a young entrepreneur who "practically single-handed created the personal computer industry," while Jobs, now wealthy, still performed his counterculture identity, once asking a Stanford business class, "How many of you are virgins? ... How many of you have taken LSD?"
