# The Mac Is Born

> **Key principle:** The Macintosh began as Jef Raskin's idea for a cheap, underpowered appliance for the masses. Jobs, freshly ejected from the Lisa project, took it over, clashed with Raskin over cost versus capability, pushed the machine toward a more powerful chip and a mouse-driven graphical interface, and forced Raskin out — turning a minor skunkworks project into his vehicle for revenge and reinvention.

*Synthesized from Chapter 10, "The Mac Is Born," of Steve Jobs by Walter Isaacson.*

## Key lessons

- Jef Raskin, an eccentric ex-professor who once flew a hot air balloon over his chancellor's house to announce he was quitting, started the Macintosh project in 1979 as "Annie," renamed for his favorite apple variety, envisioning a $1,000 appliance with a five-inch screen and a weak Motorola 6809 chip.
- Raskin and Jobs split on first principles: Raskin wrote that "starting with the abilities desired is nonsense," insisting on a fixed price target, while Jobs told him to "focus on building what he repeatedly called an 'insanely great' product" and ignore cost.
- Jobs secretly had engineer Burrell Smith build a prototype on the more powerful Motorola 68000 chip (the same one used in the Lisa) over a three-week nonstop sprint, which let Jobs force the switch and undercut Raskin's cost-conscious design.
- Raskin hated mice and icons — "an icon is a symbol equally incomprehensible in all human languages" — while Jobs and recruits like Bill Atkinson pushed hard for both, splitting the team into two camps.
- Mike Scott's showdown between Raskin and Jobs, called at Markkula's office, ended with Jobs in tears and Raskin sent on leave; Raskin's own later Canon Cat, built to his original underpowered spec, "was a total flop," according to Atkinson.
- Jobs recruited by drama, not just pitch: he unplugged Andy Hertzfeld's Apple II mid-code to walk him straight to a new desk on the Mac team, and won over Xerox PARC's Bruce Horn by showing him hand-drawn plans for how the plastic case would mold together.
- The team briefly renamed the project "the Bicycle," after Jobs's "bicycle for the mind" metaphor for computers, but the engineers simply refused to use it and it died within a month.
- Jobs's rise was completed when he helped push out Apple president Mike Scott after a rough round of layoffs, leaving Markkula as a passive interim president and Jobs with free rein over the Mac division.
