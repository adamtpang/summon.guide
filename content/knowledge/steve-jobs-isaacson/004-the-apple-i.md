# The Apple I

> **Key principle:** Chapter 5 traces the fusion of Bay Area counterculture and hacker culture through the Homebrew Computer Club, where Wozniak, inspired by seeing a microprocessor spec sheet, sketched what became the Apple I and got it working on June 29, 1975. Jobs pushed Wozniak to sell circuit boards rather than give away the designs for free, landed a 50-unit order from Paul Terrell's Byte Shop that had to be fully assembled, and turned the Jobs family garage into an assembly line to fill it, officially founding the Apple Computer partnership with Wozniak and Ron Wayne on April 1, 1976.

*Synthesized from Chapter 5, "The Apple I," of Steve Jobs by Walter Isaacson.*

## Key lessons

- The Homebrew Computer Club, founded by Gordon French and Fred Moore after the 1975 Popular Mechanics cover story on the Altair kit, embodied Stewart Brand's Whole Earth Catalog ethos that "technology could be our friend"; Jobs was a devoted Whole Earth Catalog reader and later borrowed its farewell line, "Stay Hungry. Stay Foolish," for his 2005 Stanford commencement speech.
- At Homebrew's first meeting in March 1975, Wozniak saw a microprocessor spec sheet and had an insight: he could put a minicomputer's capability into a desktop terminal. "This whole vision of a personal computer just popped into my head," he said, and he began sketching what became the Apple I that same night, choosing the cheaper MOS Technologies 6502 chip ($20) over Intel's ($much more).
- The club's ethos, in Wozniak's words, was "Give to help others"; he wanted to give his schematics away for free. Jobs disagreed and convinced him to instead sell assembled printed circuit boards, reasoning that most hobbyists lacked time to build from schematics: "Why don't we build and sell printed circuit boards to them?"
- To fund the initial run of about fifty boards (roughly $1,000 in parts), Wozniak sold his HP-65 calculator for $500 and Jobs sold his Volkswagen bus for $1,500. Jobs told a skeptical Wozniak, "Even if we lose our money, we'll have a company... For once in our lives, we'll have a company," which Wozniak found more compelling than any promise of getting rich.
- They named the company Apple Computer on the drive back from Robert Friedland's apple farm, partly because it "sounded fun, spirited, and not intimidating" and would beat Atari alphabetically in the phone book.
- Ron Wayne, the Atari engineer, was recruited as a tie-breaking 10% partner and drew Apple's first (ornate Victorian-style) logo; he drafted the three-page partnership agreement (45%-45%-10% split) signed April 1, 1976, but grew fearful of personal liability for Apple's debts and withdrew just eleven days later for $1,500 total, a stake that would have been worth roughly $2.6 billion by the end of 2010.
- Byte Shop owner Paul Terrell, after watching a Homebrew demo, agreed to order fifty Apple boards, but insisted on fully assembled units at about $500 apiece cash on delivery, rather than bare circuit boards. Jobs, after being turned down by a bank and by parts suppliers, convinced Cramer Electronics to confirm the order was real and front the roughly $15,000 in parts on 30-day credit.
- The Jobs family garage and kitchen became the assembly line: Jobs, Wozniak, Daniel Kottke, Elizabeth Holmes, and Jobs's pregnant sister Patty built and tested boards, with Paul Jobs clearing out his own workbench and building a heat-lamp "burn box" to test them overnight.
- Jobs set the Apple I's retail price at $666.66, a roughly 3x markup on production cost and 33% above the $500 wholesale price, unaware (until complaints arose, especially after the film The Omen) that 666 carried "number of the beast" connotations. In 2010 an original Apple I sold at auction for $213,000.
- At the first Personal Computer Festival in Atlantic City (Labor Day 1976), Wozniak's prototype for the Apple II was tested privately, but the bare-board Apple I looked "scruffy" next to competitors like the sleek, cased Processor Technology SOL-20, a contrast that shaped Jobs's next move: build a fully integrated, packaged machine.
