# Building the Mac

> **Key principle:** Jobs cast the Mac's development as a holy war against IBM ("Welcome, IBM. Seriously") while simultaneously undercutting Apple's own Lisa team, driven by his belief that great products must be tightly integrated "whole widgets," closed to outside tinkering. The chapter culminates in the Twiggy disk-drive crisis, where his engineers secretly defied his orders and were thanked for it.

*Synthesized from Chapter 13, "Building the Mac," of Steve Jobs by Walter Isaacson.*

## Key lessons

- When IBM launched its PC in 1981, Apple mocked it as a "half-assed, hackneyed attempt," and Jobs framed the coming fight as a "spiritual struggle" against an "evil empire," later telling Isaacson decades on that "IBM was essentially Microsoft at its worst... a force for evil."
- Jobs turned his own bet against the Lisa team, whose Rich Page burst into a demo shouting "The Macintosh is going to destroy the Lisa!... Jobs wants to destroy Lisa because we wouldn't let him control it" — a rivalry rooted partly in Jobs's resentment over being ousted from that project.
- His "whole widget" philosophy meant the Mac would have no expansion slots, a case that couldn't be opened with a normal screwdriver, and no cursor arrow keys (forcing mouse-only navigation) — deliberately closed, unlike the slot-friendly Apple II that Wozniak had insisted on.
- Marketing director Mike Murray proposed licensing the Mac OS to Tandy to build an industry standard; Jobs was "congenitally averse" and killed it, a decision that later left the Mac OS without the market share IBM clones gave Microsoft.
- Jobs believed he'd be Time's 1982 Man of the Year and was devastated to instead get a harsh profile beside "the Computer" as Machine of the Year, one that quoted Jef Raskin calling him someone who "would have made an excellent King of France" and publicly outed his daughter Lisa Brennan.
- At team retreats Jobs posted maxims on flip charts — "Don't compromise," "It's not done until it ships," "The journey is the reward," and later "Real artists ship" and "It's better to be a pirate than to join the navy" — building an identity around a Jolly Roger flag flown over the Bandley 3 building.
- The Twiggy disk-drive crisis was the chapter's turning point: Apple's in-house Twiggy drive had a failure rate over 50%, so engineer Bob Belleville secretly kept working with Sony (hiding its engineer Hidetoshi Komoto in a janitorial closet whenever Jobs appeared) after Jobs had explicitly ordered Sony work stopped in favor of Alps Electronics.
- When Alps admitted it couldn't deliver, Belleville revealed the secret Sony work; Jobs's reaction was "You son of a bitch!" delivered with a grin, and he "swallowed his pride and thanked them for disobeying him and doing the right thing" — exactly what he said he'd have done in their place.
