# Odd Couple and The Dropout

> **Key principle:** Chapter 2 traces how Bill Fernandez introduced Jobs, still in high school, to Steve Wozniak, five years older and a Homestead High electronics legend, and how their shared love of pranks and Bob Dylan led to building and selling illegal "Blue Box" phone phreaking devices, which Jobs later called the direct precursor to Apple: "If it hadn't been for the Blue Boxes, there wouldn't have been an Apple." Chapter 3 covers Jobs's year and a half at Reed College, where he stopped paying tuition but kept auditing classes, including the calligraphy course that later shaped the Mac's typography, while going deep into Zen Buddhism, extreme diets, and LSD.

*Synthesized from Chapters 2 and 3, "Odd Couple" and "The Dropout," of Steve Jobs by Walter Isaacson.*

## Key lessons

- Wozniak's engineer father, Jerry, a Lockheed rocket scientist, taught him electronics from first principles and instilled a rule Woz says he never broke: "My dad believed in honesty. Extreme honesty." He also urged his son to avoid extreme ambition and "be in the middle," a contrast Jobs's own upbringing didn't produce.
- Wozniak was building sophisticated projects years ahead of his age (an eighth-grade calculator with 100 transistors that won an Air Force-run contest, a "Cream Soda Computer" built in Bill Fernandez's garage), and after Fernandez introduced the two Steves, Wozniak recalled, "We had so much in common... I liked him right away."
- Their partnership crystallized around the "Blue Box," built after Wozniak read Ron Rosenbaum's Esquire article "Secrets of the Little Blue Box" about phone phreakers replicating AT&T's routing tones; they broke into the closed Stanford Linear Accelerator library on a Sunday to find the technical journal with the frequencies, then Wozniak built a digital version in his Berkeley dorm.
- It was Jobs's idea to sell the devices rather than just use them for pranks (like the fake call to the Vatican pretending to be Henry Kissinger asking for the pope). They sold about 100 units at $150 apiece (parts cost roughly $40), using handles "Berkeley Blue" (Wozniak) and "Oaf Tobark" (Jobs), until a buyer at a Sunnyvale pizza parlor pulled a gun and robbed Jobs of one at gunpoint.
- Wozniak later summed up the lasting lesson of the Blue Box adventure: "it gave us a taste of what we could do with my engineering skills and his vision," establishing the template that would define Apple: Wozniak as the inventor happy to give things away, Jobs as the one who packaged, marketed, and monetized them.
- At Reed, a small, expensive liberal arts college in Portland known for its "turn on, tune in, drop out" ethos, Jobs's parents were hurt when he refused to let them even walk onto campus at drop-off, an act he later called something he was "really ashamed about."
- Jobs quickly stopped attending required classes but couldn't bear the guilt of spending his working-class parents' savings on an education he didn't want, so he formally dropped out while Reed's dean, Jack Dudman, quietly let him keep auditing classes and sleeping in friends' dorm rooms for free.
- The calligraphy class he audited after dropping out taught him about serif and sans-serif typefaces and letter spacing; he later said directly, "If I had never dropped in on that single course in college, the Mac would have never had multiple typefaces or proportionally spaced fonts."
- Jobs immersed himself in Eastern spirituality with friend Daniel Kottke, reading Be Here Now, Zen Mind Beginner's Mind, and Autobiography of a Yogi, building a meditation attic, and adopting extreme diets (fruitarian regimens, week-long apple-only stretches) inspired by books like Diet for a Small Planet and Mucusless Diet Healing System.
- He fell under the influence of Robert Friedland, a charismatic older student and convicted LSD dealer who had just returned from meeting guru Neem Karoli Baba in India; Kottke said Friedland "taught Steve the reality distortion field" and that Jobs "became more like that after spending time with Robert." Jobs worked on Friedland's All One Farm apple commune, pruning Gravenstein trees, before souring on its increasingly materialistic, unpaid-labor culture.
- Jobs later reflected that LSD was "one of the most important things in my life," reinforcing "my sense of what was important, creating great things instead of making money, putting things back into the stream of history and of human consciousness."
