# How This Book Came to Be and Childhood

> **Key principle:** Isaacson explains that Jobs approached him in 2004 to write his biography, promised no editorial control and never asked to read it in advance, and later admitted the book existed largely because Laurene Powell told Isaacson, after Jobs's second cancer leave in 2009, "If you're ever going to do a book on Steve, you'd better do it now." Chapter 1 then argues that Jobs's adult personality (his need for control, his belief he was destined for greatness, his capacity for cruelty) traces to how he processed being given up for adoption as an infant and being told by his parents, at age six or seven, "We specifically picked you out."

*Synthesized from the Introduction and Chapter 1, "Childhood," of Steve Jobs by Walter Isaacson.*

## Key lessons

- Isaacson had known Jobs since 1984 and had repeatedly declined to write his biography; Jobs pursued him for years, including pulling him aside after a book event to make the pitch again. Isaacson only agreed after Laurene Powell's 2009 warning, learning only then that Jobs had been secretly battling cancer since a first operation years earlier.
- Jobs gave Isaacson unrestricted access, telling him "It's your book. I won't even read it," and encouraged more than forty interviews with him plus over a hundred with friends, rivals, and former colleagues, including people he had fired or hurt. His only intervention was demanding input on the cover art.
- Jobs was born February 24, 1955, to unmarried graduate students Joanne Schieble and Abdulfattah "John" Jandali, and placed for adoption on the condition (from Joanne) that the adoptive parents be college graduates; when the intended lawyer-and-wife couple backed out because they wanted a girl, he went instead to Paul and Clara Jobs, a mechanic who never finished high school and a bookkeeper, after Joanne initially refused to sign until they pledged to fund his college education.
- Told at six or seven that he was "chosen" rather than that his birth parents "didn't want him," Jobs built his self-concept around the triad "Abandoned. Chosen. Special." Colleagues and girlfriends later linked this to his controlling streak: Del Yocam said "he wants to control his environment... he sees the product as an extension of himself," and Chrisann Brennan said it left him "full of broken glass": "He who is abandoned is an abandoner."
- Jobs's father Paul, a machinist and repo man, gave him a workbench in the garage, took him on junkyard parts runs, and taught him to finish even the unseen backs of cabinets and fences properly: "He loved doing things right. He even cared about the look of the parts you couldn't see," a lesson Jobs later applied to circuit-board layout.
- Growing up in a subdivision built by developer Joseph Eichler, Jobs absorbed a taste for "clean design and simple taste" brought to lower-income buyers, which he later cited as the founding vision for Apple and the first Mac.
- A formative moment of disillusionment came via neighbor Larry Lang's carbon-microphone demonstration, which contradicted what his father had told him; Jobs said the realization that he was "smarter than his parents" was "burned into my mind" and brought both shame and a growing sense of separateness.
- Bored in school, Jobs was a prankster (an exploding device under his third-grade teacher's chair, switched bike locks) until fourth-grade teacher Imogene "Teddy" Hill bribed him with a lollipop and $5 to complete workbooks; he later credited her as "one of the saints of my life" and said "if it hadn't been for her I'm sure I would have gone to jail."
- After testing at a high-school-sophomore level, he skipped a grade into rough, gang-troubled Crittenden Middle School; when he was bullied, he gave his parents an ultimatum, and they moved to Los Altos so he could attend better Cupertino schools, buying a $21,000 house at 2066 Crist Drive, the future birthplace of Apple.
- At thirteen, after confronting his Lutheran pastor with a Life magazine cover of starving Biafran children and being told God still knew about their suffering, Jobs rejected organized religion, though he spent years afterward studying Zen Buddhism.
- At Homestead High, Jobs joined the HP Explorers Club, cold-called HP CEO Bill Hewlett out of the phone book at around age twelve to get parts for a frequency counter (and got a summer job on the assembly line out of it), took John McCollum's legendary electronics class, and began smoking marijuana and later LSD and hash during his sophomore and senior years.
- He developed a lasting self-image at the intersection of technology and the humanities, reading Shakespeare's King Lear and Melville's Moby-Dick alongside his electronics work, foreshadowing the "humanities and sciences" framing he used throughout his career.
