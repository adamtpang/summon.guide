# The iMac and CEO

> **Key principle:** Jobs specified an all-in-one home computer under $1,200 with a distinctive brand statement; Ive's team delivered a curved, translucent bondi-blue shell that "felt like it's just arrived on your desktop," and its launch in May 1998, followed by Jobs finally accepting the permanent CEO title in January 2000, marked Apple's turn from crisis to profit.

*Synthesized from Chapters 27 and 28, "The iMac" and "CEO," of Steve Jobs by Walter Isaacson.*

## Key lessons

- Jobs told the team to "go back to the roots of the original 1984 Macintosh, an all-in-one consumer appliance," and, quoting Wayne Gretzky ("Skate where the puck's going, not where it's been"), had Jon Rubinstein cut the standard floppy drive.
- Ive's team designed a translucent, bondi-blue (named for an Australian beach) plastic case so customers could see the machine's internals, at more than triple the normal cost per case; Jobs approved it without demanding financial justification. A recessed handle Ive added was "more playful and semiotic than functional" — meant to make an intimidating machine feel approachable enough to touch.
- Jobs's temper flared repeatedly before launch: he exploded when he discovered the prototype had a CD tray instead of the slot drive he insisted he'd specified, and he obsessed over the exact shade of blue in Lee Clow's print ads, once reportedly screaming into his car phone outside a Palo Alto Whole Foods, "Not. Fucking. Blue. Enough!!!"
- Launched May 6, 1998 at $1,299, the iMac sold 278,000 units in six weeks and 800,000 by year's end, the fastest-selling computer in Apple history; 32% of buyers were first-time computer purchasers. Bill Gates dismissed it publicly as mere "leadership in colors," which enraged Jobs.
- Once CEO, Jobs became a genuine operations manager: he cut Apple's bloated inventory in half within months and, after firing an ineffective operations head, hired Tim Cook from Compaq in 1998. Cook eventually cut supplier count from a hundred to twenty-four and inventory from roughly two months' worth down to about two days'.
- Jobs instituted "deep collaboration" and "concurrent engineering," having departments work in parallel rather than in sequence, and used a collaborative hiring process modeled loosely on Oppenheimer's Manhattan Project team-building, warning against what he called "the bozo explosion" of hiring C players.
- Ed Woolard pressed Jobs for over two years to drop "interim"; Jobs took only $1 salary and declined stock initially ("I don't want the people I work with at Apple to think I am coming back to get rich"), instead requesting a Gulfstream jet, before finally accepting the permanent CEO title at the January 2000 Macworld alongside the launch of Mac OS X, built on NeXT's Unix-based kernel.
- The stock option negotiation that followed (Jobs asking for twenty million options against a board authorization of fourteen million, later restructured after the dot-com crash) would, the book notes, "come back to haunt the company" in later years.
