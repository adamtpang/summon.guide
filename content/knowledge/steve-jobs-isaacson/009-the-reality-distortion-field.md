# The Reality Distortion Field

> **Key principle:** Bud Tribble coined the term, borrowed from a Star Trek episode where aliens "create their own new world through sheer mental force," to explain why Jobs's team kept hitting deadlines that looked impossible: "in his presence, reality is malleable... it wears off when he's not around." It was not simple lying — Jobs bent facts to fit his purpose and, according to Bill Atkinson, believed his own distortions enough to convince others.

*Synthesized from Chapter 11, "The Reality Distortion Field," of Steve Jobs by Walter Isaacson.*

## Key lessons

- Andy Hertzfeld was warned about the field on his first day by Bud Tribble, who called it "a confounding mélange of a charismatic rhetorical style, indomitable will, and eagerness to bend any fact to fit the purpose at hand" — and noted it worked "even if you were acutely aware of it."
- Wozniak, "congenitally honest" by contrast, described it functionally: "His reality distortion is when he has an illogical vision of the future... you realize that it can't be true, but he somehow makes it true," citing Jobs convincing him he could design Breakout in a few days.
- Debi Coleman called it "self-fulfilling": "You did the impossible, because you didn't realize it was impossible," and credited it with letting the Mac team outpace Xerox and IBM on a fraction of their resources.
- Jobs's binary worldview split people into "enlightened" or "an asshole" and work into "the best" or "totally shitty," per Bill Atkinson, who said being a favored "god" on the team still meant living in fear of being knocked off the pedestal.
- He could reverse himself without acknowledgment — Bruce Horn recalled pitching an idea Jobs called crazy, then hearing Jobs pitch it back as his own a week later; the team developed an "audio" coping strategy they literally called a "low pass filter" to smooth out his swings.
- The field wasn't emotional numbness — Joanna Hoffman said Jobs "had the uncanny capacity to know exactly what your weak point is... to make you cringe," using that insight to alternately crush and elevate people.
- The Mac team gave an annual (half-joking, half-serious) award for whoever best stood up to Jobs — Joanna Hoffman won it twice, once after threatening (in front of Apple's corporate counsel) to "take a knife and stab it into his heart" over changed marketing projections — and pushing back successfully, per Bill Atkinson, tended to earn Jobs's respect rather than his anger.
- The clearest concrete example: Jobs told engineer Larry Kenyon that shaving ten seconds off Mac boot time, multiplied across five million users, would save "at least one hundred lifetimes" a year — and Kenyon delivered a boot time cut of twenty-eight seconds weeks later.
- Wozniak, reflecting later, argued the terrorizing style wasn't necessary and burned people out, but even critics like Debi Coleman said being screamed at "like an hourly occurrence" was still worth it: "I consider myself the absolute luckiest person in the world to have worked with him."
