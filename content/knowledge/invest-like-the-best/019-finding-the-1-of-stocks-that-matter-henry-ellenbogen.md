# Finding The 1% of Stocks That Matter | Henry Ellenbogen Interview

> **Key principle:** Henry Ellenbogen built Durable Capital around a single empirical finding from studying 50 years of a small-cap fund's history: over any rolling 10-year period, only about 40 of the roughly 4,000 public US stocks (about 1%) compound wealth at 20% a year or better, and roughly 80% of those started their run as small companies. His firm's entire structure, research process, and even the practice of buying more of a winning stock as its price rises are built to maximize the odds of catching one of those 40.

*Henry Ellenbogen on Durable Capital's investment philosophy, finding enduring compounders, and adapting to AI, Invest Like the Best · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=plx6Foxoam4) · 115 min · published 2025-12-16.*

## Key lessons

- The founding insight came from a costly near-miss: while studying 50 years of T. Rowe's New Horizons fund archives, Ellenbogen found that Walmart had been sold from the portfolio after its IPO; had the position been held, it alone would have been worth more than the entire roughly $8 billion fund he later managed, a single sale that mathematically would have outweighed every other good decision made across five decades.
- His "act two" thesis favors backing founders repeating a category they already won once: Workday's Aneel Bhusri and Dave Duffield (who built PeopleSoft before Oracle's takeover) and Max Levchin (PayPal, then Affirm) are his examples of entrepreneurs who bring proven pattern-recognition, cleaner organizational alignment, and known resilience under stress to a second act, and Durable itself was nearly named "Act 2 Capital" for the same reason.
- His "good to great" pattern for the AI era looks outside pure tech: he cites Domino's Pizza as the best-performing Russell 2000 growth stock of the 2010s not because of explosive revenue growth, but because it invested early in app-based convenience and a direct customer relationship, compounding a modest product edge into dominant share; he draws the same parallel to Walmart, Costco, and Amazon collectively capturing 62% of retail once the two incumbents adapted to Amazon's cost curve.
- He frames the physical-world analog to AI's "digital Kaizen" through Danaher's Mitch Rales, whose 40-year Danaher Business System (an Americanized Toyota-style process-improvement discipline) compounded the company at roughly 20% annually and produced more than a dozen Fortune 500 CEOs, arguing AI is now doing to white-collar and IP-heavy processes what Danaher's system did to factory floors.
- He estimates the cost curve for robotics and physical automation could decline 15-20% a year going forward (versus the roughly 3-5% annual cost deflation that let Amazon build an uncatchable logistics advantage over 20 years), meaning early movers on the robotics curve could open a gap that a competitor investing the same dollars a few years later, even with equally good people, cannot close.
- As a concrete AI-productivity data point from his own portfolio, he cites Duolingo CEO Luis von Ahn building the Duolingo Chess product with a team that started at two people for six months and grew to six, shipping in nine months total, a timeline von Ahn told him would have needed four to six times the headcount and roughly four times the time before AI tools.
- Durable's underwriting rule for early-stage growth positions is to only buy in the first place if the firm can write an investment memo saying it would want to buy more at a higher price once the thesis plays out; if the only bull case is "it gets acquired," they pass, and in practice this led them to buy more of already-public but unprofitable names like Duolingo through the 2022 selloff rather than exit.
- The firm runs deliberate accountability rituals against its own theses: quarterly "operating reviews" walk the entire portfolio position by position among the whole investment team, and every holding gets a formal three-year look-back comparing what was underwritten against what actually happened, explicitly to catch when a small quarterly deviation from the thesis has, over 12 straight quarters, become undeniable.
- As T. Rowe's Netflix investor during its 2011-2012 DVD-to-streaming pivot, Ellenbogen personally called Reed Hastings to warn that subscriber-loss scenarios could force a capital raise; Netflix stock had fallen from about $280 to $70 (and continued to the $50s a year later) before Ellenbogen led a roughly $4.5 billion PIPE recapitalization that helped fund the transition.
