# Why The Laws of Startup Physics Have Changed | Ben Horowitz Interview

> **Key principle:** Ben Horowitz argues that AI has broken the core assumption venture investing was built on, that throwing money at a software problem never works because a great small team with a head start can't be caught. Now, given enough data and GPUs, a well-funded entrant can close a multi-year gap in months, which forces investors to rethink both how defensible a lead really is and how large a market can become.

*Ben Horowitz on how AI is changing venture capital, company building, and firm strategy, Invest Like the Best · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ID27juNvAfc) · 62 min · published 2026-02-03.*

## Key lessons

- The founding law of software company building, that you "cannot throw money at the problem" and a small team with a head start can't be caught by a bigger funded rival, no longer reliably holds; Horowitz points to Elon Musk assembling a frontier AI lab in a short window using capital, a strong data-center design, and a small team of strong engineers, something he says "would have never happened in the past."
- Cursor is his go-to example of the new economics: he says it took prior-generation IDE products something like 12 to 15 years to reach a comparable revenue level, while Cursor crossed a billion dollars in revenue in a fraction of that time, evidence that AI-native products can compound distribution and revenue far faster than past technology cycles.
- Elite AI researcher compensation (packages Horowitz frames as running into the hundreds of millions or beyond) makes sense once you treat the skill as scarce and nearly unteachable in school: building a frontier model successfully on a first real attempt is, in his words, "a little bit alchemistic," so a firm paying up is effectively bidding for one of a very small number of people worldwide who have actually done it before.
- a16z was named after its founders deliberately, not out of ego: raising its first fund in 2009 during the financial crisis, LPs' biggest objection was that Horowitz and Marc Andreessen, both former operators, would abandon the fund to go build another company; putting their names on the firm was Horowitz's fix, a signal that they were staying.
- The firm's original wedge against reputation-based incumbents (Sequoia, Benchmark, Kleiner) was to build "a better product for entrepreneurs" by requiring every investor to have founded or run a company, and to market the firm loudly and publicly at a time when top VCs deliberately stayed quiet and let track record speak for itself.
- Fund performance was uneven in the firm's early years: fund one (roughly $300 million) returned strongly on bets including Skype, Slack, Okta, and Stripe; fund two underperformed; fund three went through a period Horowitz calls genuinely "scary" before turning into a strong fund on the back of Coinbase, Databricks, Lyft, and GitHub. The multi-team, sector-platform structure the firm now runs on was only formalized starting around the 2018 crypto fund.
- His operating definition of culture, credited to Bushido (the way of the warrior), is that culture is a set of enforced behaviors, not stated values: at a16z that meant fining people $10 a minute early on for being late to entrepreneur meetings, requiring a clear "no" with an explained reason to every founder who's passed on, surveying rejected founders afterward to confirm they had a good experience, and firing anyone who publicly disparages a founder to make themselves look good.
- The firm-funded technology deployment with the Las Vegas Police Department (drones, AI cameras, license-plate and gunshot detection with a roughly 90-second drone response) coincided with crime down over 50% and shootings of suspects by police down close to 75%, while Vegas already posted a 94% murder clearance rate against a national average Horowitz cites as below 60%; he frames the mechanism as removing false-identification risk (matching the real car, not a wrong description) as the main safety gain, not raw surveillance volume.
