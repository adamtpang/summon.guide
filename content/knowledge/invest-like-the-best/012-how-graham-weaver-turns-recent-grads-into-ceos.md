# How Graham Weaver Turns Recent Grads Into CEOs

> **Key principle:** Graham Weaver built Alpine Investors around installing Alpine's own young, first-time CEOs into nearly every company it buys rather than backing the existing founder or management team. The firm treats CEO development as its actual product: thirty in-house coaches, a standardized first-90-days playbook, and employee/customer Net Promoter Score tracked as the leading indicator of deal success, on top of a stated 5x-MOIC objective for every fund.

*Graham Weaver on Alpine Investors' operator-development system, Invest Like the Best · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=u2nHljZeMQ8) · 100 min · published 2025-05-27.*

## Key lessons

- Alpine states an explicit, unusual objective function: 5x MOIC on every fund, not IRR, because Weaver believes chasing IRR pushes firms to sell winners early to boost fundraising optics; Alpine has hit or is on pace for 5x on its last three to four funds.
- Around 2010 Alpine made a deliberate, "brutal" strategic break: it stopped backing existing founders and started installing its own CEO on essentially 100% of platform deals, rebuilding its entire sourcing engine and brand around that promise, since investment banks generally won't sell management-less companies.
- The HVAC/plumbing roll-up built by CEOs AJ Brown and Will Matson (brand: Apex) is Alpine's marquee proof point: started from an $8 million-EBITDA business with $50 million of equity, it grew to roughly $500 million of EBITDA with no additional equity injected, staffed partly through an internal training pipeline that now runs about 80 military veterans as general managers.
- Alpine's CEO-in-training program began accidentally: Weaver, guest-lecturing at Stanford GSB, placed a Stanford student named Laura Walsh into a portfolio company CEO role by personally guaranteeing to refund her salary if she didn't work out within a year; she succeeded, and the program grew purely by word of mouth into what Weaver says was the single most applied-to job at Harvard, Stanford, and Kellogg business schools last year.
- The screening criteria for these young CEOs, in order: a "white hot will to win," grit (having been knocked down and gotten back up, which Weaver says is rarer at elite schools than expected), emotional intelligence/self-awareness, and a bias for action over analysis-paralysis.
- Alpine measures employee Net Promoter Score and customer NPS at acquisition and every six months after, publishing results firm-wide and holding CEOs accountable to it, treating it as the top leading indicator of deal outcomes (Weaver cites roughly 40,000 portfolio employees and a stat that 70% of US workers are disengaged from their jobs).
- Alpine underwrites individual deals to a 3x return over about five years using debt and operational growth rather than multiple expansion, and relies on portfolio-level asymmetry (a handful of outlier platforms per fund) to reach the 5x target; Weaver compares this to Warren Buffett having made roughly half his early wealth from just two stocks, Geico and the Washington Post.
- Weaver's own path to conviction took over two decades: 21 years into his career before his first carry check (due to a European fund waterfall), with a $100,000 salary at that point, after funding his earliest deals in his 20s partly through Capital One 0%-APR balance-transfer checks mailed to him.
- His worst deal was a slot-machine manufacturing business that swelled to $170 million invested inside a $68 million fund; Alpine survived it by betting early that Illinois would legalize bar-based slot machines and locking up bar relationships years ahead of competitors, but Weaver says the discomfort of the industry (recounting seeing a child waiting outside a casino while a parent gambled) taught him never to back a business he isn't personally proud of, regardless of the underlying economics.
