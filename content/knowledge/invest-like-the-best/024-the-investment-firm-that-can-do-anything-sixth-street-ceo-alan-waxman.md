# The Investment Firm That Can "Do Anything" | Sixth Street CEO Alan Waxman

> **Key principle:** Waxman built Sixth Street's edge on a single discipline learned at Goldman: strip every deal down to a common "risk unit vs. return unit" so a real estate investment, a corporate loan, and a sports-stadium joint venture can be compared on the same footing. Paired with a flexible pool of capital that isn't boxed into one asset class, that discipline lets the firm design bespoke instruments on a whiteboard rather than forcing every deal into a standard structure.

*Alan Waxman on building Sixth Street into a go-anywhere investment firm, Invest Like the Best · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zPFor81KJx0) · 83 min · published 2025-07-15.*

## Key lessons

- Waxman's formative training ground was Goldman's Special Situations Group (nicknamed the "Navy SEALs" of finance by the Wall Street Journal), a principal-investing unit that at its peak deployed roughly $25 billion of Goldman's own balance sheet across any asset class, sector, or duration, under one constraint: don't lose money.
- The group's core method, which became Sixth Street's method, is to reduce every investment to three variables so wildly different deals can be compared: the quality of the business and sector, where the capital sits in the structure (its "attachment point"), and the documents/covenants governing it, letting them weigh a leveraged consumer buyout against a 15-year data-center contract on the same scale.
- Sixth Street runs 15 to 25 live investment "themes" at any given time, drawn from a rotating pool of 50 to 60 candidate themes surfaced by its 16 sector teams; Waxman says a good theme has a shelf life of roughly 12 to 36 months before it gets crowded and overleveraged, so the firm deliberately migrates capital away from decaying themes rather than riding them down.
- On top of ten specialized investment platforms (each with its own modestly sized fund), Sixth Street runs a roughly $30 billion flexible vehicle called TAO, which Waxman describes as a "synthetic Goldman Sachs balance sheet": it lets the firm write billion-dollar-plus checks on deals too large for any single platform's fund, without having to bloat every platform's fund size to match.
- The 2016 Spotify investment was a $1 billion convertible structured instrument capped near a $25 billion valuation, designed specifically because Spotify didn't want to sell common equity below its last funding round amid competitive pressure from Amazon and Apple; the deal was built as a bridge to Spotify's eventual direct listing.
- The March 2020 Airbnb deal, a $1 billion loan-plus-warrants financing done jointly with Silver Lake, closed in seven days using Sixth Street teams in Asia, the US, and Europe handing off work around the clock; the underwriting bet wasn't just on Airbnb's business model but on a liquidity-runway analysis assuming the company needed four to five years of cash to survive until "there would be a cure."
- Sixth Street's entry into sports (partnerships with Real Madrid, FC Barcelona, the Dallas Cowboys, the New York Yankees, and the San Francisco Giants) grew out of a COVID-era thesis that live experiences and historically local sports brands were going global via streaming and phones; the Real Madrid deal was structured as an equity joint venture co-owning stadium assets (the Bernabéu renovation) rather than as debt, reached through repeated whiteboard sessions rather than a standard term sheet.
- Waxman credits a mentor, Steven Plus (now Sixth Street's chief risk officer), who spent roughly two years personally answering his daily written questions on risk analysis early in his Goldman career; that relationship led directly to the business plan for Goldman's first middle-market direct-lending unit, a business Waxman says has since grown past $50 billion, and it shaped his own insistence that "you have to authentically care" as the starting point for developing people.
