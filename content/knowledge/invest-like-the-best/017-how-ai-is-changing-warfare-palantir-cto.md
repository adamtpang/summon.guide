# How AI Is Changing Warfare | Palantir CTO

> **Key principle:** Shyam Sankar argues that real institutional progress comes almost exclusively from "heretics," disagreeable insiders who fight their own bureaucracy to ship something that works, and that America's postwar shift from a broad industrial base to a narrow defense-specialist base is why the country lost deterrence over the last decade. His fix is cultural and structural: reward risk-taking founders inside institutions, rebuild dual-use manufacturing, and use AI as an asymmetric multiplier for the American worker rather than another layer of bureaucratic process.

*Shyam Sankar on Palantir, AI, and the future of defense technology, Invest Like the Best · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EB9V8d3ZYbc) · 89 min · published 2026-03-10.*

## Key lessons

- Sankar's core historical model is the "heretic": people like Hyman Rickover (who built the first nuclear submarine in seven years after Oppenheimer told him it would fail, and whose first office on the project was literally a women's restroom as a humiliation tactic), Andrew Higgins (who built the landing boat that became 92% of all Allied boats in WWII despite the Navy repeatedly rejecting it), and Billy Mitchell (court-martialed and died penniless for advocating an air force, vindicated only after death). His pattern: heretics are punished by their institutions in real time and only become heroes retrospectively, because nothing that goes through the standard bureaucratic process ever actually delivers.
- Palantir's "forward deployed engineering" model, which Sankar pioneered, sends engineers to work directly with end users (the factory floor, the foxhole) rather than selling from Palo Alto, because the real feedback loop isn't "will someone buy this" but "did it deliver the outcome." He splits engineers into two types: those who build the right thing (MacGyver-style, judged by whether the problem got solved) and those who build things the right way (artist-style, judged by architectural elegance), and says a business needs both vectors aligned or it doesn't work.
- Sankar's diagnosis of America's defense-industrial decline: in the pre-Cold War era, only 6% of major weapons spending went to defense-only specialists (94% went to dual-purpose companies like Chrysler building both minivans and missiles, General Mills building torpedoes, Kodak supplying the Corona spy satellite); today that ratio has flipped to 86% defense-specialist spending. He traces the break to the 1993 "Last Supper" dinner, where the Secretary of Defense told 15 of the 51 prime contractors the budget was being cut and they should consolidate or exit, triggering a merger wave that took primes from 51 down to 5 and pushed the era's best engineering talent out of defense and into tech.
- On lost deterrence: Sankar cites the 2014 annexation of Crimea, China's militarization of the Spratly Islands after telling Obama it wouldn't, the 2023 pogrom in Israel, and the invasion of Ukraine as evidence the US stopped deterring conflict over the past decade, but argues deterrence has started returning in the last year, citing the Midnight Hammer strike and operations against Maduro (whose Chinese and Russian weapons "don't seem to work at all") as recent proof points.
- His argument for re-industrialization: innovation is downstream of production, not separate from it, using the example that Google's "Attention Is All You Need" paper (which became the basis of modern LLMs) grew out of an incremental effort to improve Google Translate by 3%, meaning the innovation followed from production work, not the reverse. He cites Patrick McGee's book on Apple and China, noting Apple spent the inflation-adjusted equivalent of two and a half Marshall Plans building manufacturing talent and capacity in China over five years, and asks why the US isn't attempting even one Marshall Plan domestically.
- Concrete numbers he uses to argue supply-chain fragility: 80% of US generic drugs (or their active ingredients) come from China in some form, and the US spent roughly $2 billion solving its rare-earths dependency problem after 15 years of inaction, a sum he calls "a rounding error" relative to overall defense spending.
- On talent and organizational design: Sankar's "Superman" framework says highly talented people misjudge their own superpowers because effortless skills don't feel like achievements, so the real skill is easy for them and thus underrated by themselves; the job of a leader is to help someone find the "narrow sliver" that is genuinely effortless for them and steer them away from their "kryptonite" (a weakness that can't be trained away, only avoided). He also describes deliberately putting new hires on very high-stakes, under-resourced problems ("gamma ray moments," referencing the Incredible Hulk origin) as Palantir's primary mechanism for accelerating growth, arguing that a structured career ladder only creates the feeling of progress, not the reality of it.
- On AI adoption inside Palantir: the earliest and most productive AI adopters were the newest, youngest hires rather than tenured staff, and Sankar deliberately let their visible productivity gains create internal pressure rather than trying to convince skeptics directly. He says the time for Palantir to deliver a transformative outcome for a customer has compressed from roughly eight weeks two years ago to about one week now.
