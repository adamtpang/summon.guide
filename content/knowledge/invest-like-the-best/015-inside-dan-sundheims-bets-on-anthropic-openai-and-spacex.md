# Inside Dan Sundheim's Bets on Anthropic, OpenAI, and SpaceX

> **Key principle:** Dan Sundheim, founder and CIO of D1 Capital, argues that in genuinely new industries the numbers can't tell you what a business will become, so he leans on reading founders' own writing and judging the clarity of their thinking as a proxy for future execution. He applies the same discipline he used to expose an accounting fraud at 24 to companies now worth hundreds of billions: build the model yourself, and trust what doesn't reconcile.

*Dan Sundheim on D1 Capital's private bets on Anthropic, OpenAI, and SpaceX, Invest Like the Best · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mBtmTmCC0ZM) · 87 min · published 2026-02-24.*

## Key lessons

- D1 first invested in OpenAI at its $125 billion valuation round, when the market was still split on whether large language models were even a viable business, comparable to airlines: a technology everyone would use but where competition erodes returns to the cost of capital.
- Sundheim's decision to back Anthropic over the more established OpenAI came down almost entirely to reading Dario Amodei's essays and hearing him on podcasts; he weighed clarity of written thought as a signal of CEO quality on par with what he saw rereading Jeff Bezos's 1997 shareholder letter, a document he says he missed acting on the first time and calls one of his career's costliest misses.
- He frames the leading AI labs as a hybrid of Netflix and Spotify: Netflix-like in that massive upfront capital builds a fixed asset (a trained model) sold afterward at very high incremental margins, and Spotify-like in that the differentiation isn't raw model quality but personalization, the more a model learns about a user's life, the stickier and harder to leave it becomes.
- He now believes hyperscalers (AWS, Azure, GCP) are structurally weaker long-term businesses even as their near-term growth accelerates, because once Anthropic, OpenAI, and peers turn cash-flow positive in five to ten years, he expects them to insource compute the way Meta already has, since he judges the labs to already be better than hyperscalers at running GPU clusters and inference workloads specifically.
- On the SpaceX versus Rivian decision, he made similarly sized bets on both around the same time: Rivian's electric-vehicle thesis (autos as software, à la smartphones displacing Nokia/Motorola) underperformed because auto manufacturing is brutally capital-intensive and scaling was slower than modeled, while SpaceX was "obvious" to him because it had already proven the hardest engineering problem (reusable rockets) at a low revenue multiple with minimal cash burn; he now sees Starlink's falling bandwidth-delivery costs expanding SpaceX's addressable market to the entire global telecom industry, not just remote connectivity.
- He publicly pushed OpenAI's executives to adopt advertising roughly a year and a half before this conversation, arguing that any company that will eventually need ads should start the cultural shift early rather than delay, using Netflix's own reluctant pivot to advertising as the precedent.
- Surviving the 2021 GameStop short squeeze meant telling his team their that year's compensation calculation would exclude January, and by the trough of the 2022 drawdown he held D1's scheduled four-night LP dinner series anyway (against his president Jeremy's advice to cancel it) to announce the firm would shift its whole portfolio construction toward "singles and doubles" instead of concentrated risk.
- His single biggest fear for the next decade isn't AI safety in the abstract, it's a US-China collision over Taiwan's semiconductor dominance (Taiwan produces the vast majority of the world's most advanced chips); he compares the stakes to global oil dependence and says he sees no outcome where China, Taiwan, and the US are all satisfied, only ones where the economy avoids depression-level damage.
- His formative story as an investor: as a 24-year-old at Bear Stearns in 2002, prepping a case study on Orthodontic Centers of America for a hedge fund interview, he found the unit economics didn't reconcile, identified what he believed was expense capitalization fraud, posted the writeup anonymously on Value Investors Club, and watched the stock crater 20-30% within a day as mutual fund analysts who'd heard about it through his own network started calling Bear Stearns asking what was going on.
