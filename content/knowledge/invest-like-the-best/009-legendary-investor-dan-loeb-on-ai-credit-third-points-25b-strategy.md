# Legendary Investor Dan Loeb on AI, Credit, & Third Point's $25B Strategy

> **Key principle:** Third Point's Dan Loeb built his career finding value other investors were too rigid, too specialized, or too scared to price correctly, from cracking a nearly unreadable Drexel Burnham bankruptcy disclosure as a junior analyst to buying XAI's cash-flow-negative debt when most credit desks wouldn't touch it. His firm has evolved from a 1990s event-driven playbook of spin-offs and demutualizations into a roughly $25 billion multi-strategy shop that is now majority credit by assets, built specifically so it can express a view anywhere in a company's capital structure rather than being confined to one lane.

*Dan Loeb on Third Point's evolution from deep-value credit investing to a $25B AI-era strategy, Invest Like the Best · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vhTi_8QwXjg) · 73 min · published 2026-05-28.*

## Key lessons

- Third Point's hedge fund started with $3 million and now runs about $9 billion, but that is only one piece of a roughly $25 billion firm that also includes a CLO business with about $7 billion, an insurance arm managing roughly $1 billion in credit plus a separate pool of asbestos liabilities, and a newly launched private credit business, together making the firm about 60% credit by total assets despite Loeb being best known for equity activism.
- Loeb's early framework came directly from Joel Greenblatt's "You Can Be a Stock Market Genius": buying newly created securities from spin-offs, demutualizations, and privatizations, which tended to be mispriced because index and mutual funds dumped them regardless of fundamentals, management teams sandbagged early guidance to set low compensation targets, and the businesses themselves were run inefficiently inside their parent companies; this was Third Point's primary edge from the fund's 1995 founding through roughly 2013-2015.
- The firm's cross-capital-structure approach paid off directly in AI-adjacent names: Third Point bought Twitter's underwater leveraged buyout debt at 96-97 cents on the dollar yielding about 12%, when most credit investors were too scared of the equity story to touch it, and separately bought debt in XAI, a business with roughly $2 billion of revenue against a $20 billion enterprise value and no meaningful cash flow, precisely because the firm's private-investing knowledge let it underwrite the equity value well enough to get comfortable holding the debt when almost no other credit investors would.
- Loeb's most instructive investment was Danaher: his team got the company to compress its five-day DBS (Danaher Business System) training into a single day for them, and watching Danaher systematically shed lower-return businesses in favor of higher-margin, higher-ROIC ones (its shift from general industrials toward healthcare) taught him what a genuine continuous-improvement culture looks like, including treating identified underperformance as fixable and worth celebrating rather than something to hide.
- His activist playbook is illustrated by Sotheby's: Third Point bought a 9.9% stake in a company he says was mismanaged and unprofitable despite being centuries old and carrying high social status, gave the incumbent CEO (who came from the rug division with no deep art-market relationships) a year to prove himself, then backed his replacement with Tad Smith from Madison Square Garden, who cleaned up operations and technology ahead of a profitable sale.
- The Sony campaign (once a roughly 7% stake) pushed for breaking up a conglomerate spanning film studios, semiconductors, life insurance, and consumer electronics; Third Point leaked its investment thesis to The New York Times (reporter Andrew Ross Sorkin) timed to an embargo until Japanese markets closed, and it took about five years of resistance before Sony began separating its semiconductor and financial-services businesses.
- On Japan more broadly, Loeb co-wrote a policy paper with Larry Lindsey and Niall Ferguson for the American Enterprise Institute arguing Japan's "three arrows" reform program (fiscal, monetary, structural) needed a fourth: corporate governance centered on return on invested capital; the paper was picked up as a Wall Street Journal editorial and, in Loeb's account, the Japanese government proved more receptive to the reform than entrenched incumbent management teams were.
- Loeb calls Third Point's investment in FTX its hardest lesson: the business looked verifiable on the blockchain, had credible co-investors, and appeared to be growing fast, but the fraud went undetected, and Third Point's diligence process now includes direct verification steps like checking bank balances that likely would have surfaced problems earlier.
- He argues the current AI-driven rally in semiconductors and hyperscalers is fundamentally unlike the dot-com bubble because the capex is being funded largely off real balance-sheet cash flow rather than speculative external financing, and he frames nearly all of current macro analysis through two dominant variables (oil/geopolitics and AI infrastructure spend) layered onto Jensen Huang's AI stack model of power, chips, models, and applications, naming Nvidia, Anthropic, and the collection of Elon Musk's companies as today's three most consequential businesses to track.
