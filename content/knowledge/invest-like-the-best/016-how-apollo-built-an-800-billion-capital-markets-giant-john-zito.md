# How Apollo Built an $800 Billion Capital Markets Giant

> **Key principle:** John Zito, Apollo's deputy co-president, describes a firm that rebuilt asset management around two moves: merging with an insurance and annuity company (Athene) to create a permanent, aligned balance sheet, and building its own origination machines so it controls the supply of loans and structured assets rather than competing to buy them. The result is a firm that can write multi-billion-dollar deals for investment-grade companies like Intel and BP, deals that would have been unthinkable for a "private credit" shop a decade ago.

*John Zito on how Apollo built an $800 billion private credit and capital markets giant, Invest Like the Best · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=17bGyLB00s0) · 86 min · published 2025-06-03.*

## Key lessons

- Apollo manages just under $800 billion, is growing roughly $150 billion a year, writes $1-2 billion of annuities per week, and originated $260 billion of investment-grade and private asset product last year; about 65% of its balance sheet sits in investment-grade assets, a scale Zito says is a full retooling of how capital markets function, not an incremental growth story.
- The core structural innovation was merging with retirement and annuity company Athene roughly three years ago, giving Apollo just over $300 billion of its own balance sheet (95% investment grade, 5% alternatives) that it invests directly: it writes an annuity at 4-5%, invests the proceeds at roughly 6.5%, and keeps the spread, turning Apollo from a pure fee-taking agent into a principal investor aligned with its own risk.
- From 2014 to 2022, Apollo spent nearly $10 billion of its own capital building origination businesses it now controls outright, including aviation finance platform PK Air, non-qualified mortgage lender NewFi, and a structured-products warehouse business (bought from Credit Suisse and rebranded Atlas), employing roughly 4,000 people just to originate assets for its own balance sheet.
- The Atlas deal illustrates the origination-platform playbook: it was Credit Suisse's top profit center for a decade with a $45 billion balance sheet and effectively no losses, spanning mortgages, solar, commercial real estate, and consumer loans; Apollo took on $28 billion of assets, brought in MassMutual and two sovereign wealth funds as equity partners, hired 180 people to restaff it as a standalone company, and is targeting growth to a $100 billion warehouse business across 280 separate underlying warehouses.
- Apollo did a landmark $11 billion deal for Intel structured as long-duration (30+ years), equity-like but debt-protected private capital, a structure Zito says investment-grade companies historically never accessed because their only channels were bank loans, public bonds, or equity; after the 2020 AB InBev deal, Zito says he got roughly 10 calls telling him "you'll never do a deal for an S&P 500 company again," a prediction contradicted by subsequent deals with BP, Air France, and Venovia.
- In the Carvana restructuring, Apollo organized 90% of a $5.5 billion bond position into a creditor "co-op" that blocked a coercive exchange offer from CEO Ernie Garcia's advisers; a personal relationship built over roughly 20 calls, several dinners, and even a chance vacation encounter in the south of France ultimately produced a negotiated deal, after which Carvana's stock rose from about $4 to $280 and the restructured bonds rose from 90 (post-exchange) to 120 within a year.
- In the Hertz bankruptcy (2020), Apollo went from lead lender in a 2022 syndicated loan to holding term loans acquired around 60 cents on the dollar, then became Hertz's largest secured lender: it provided the DIP financing, refinanced $4 billion of the company's used-car fleet financing during the crash, bought Hertz's fleet-leasing platform business (merged into its Wheels platform), and later provided $2.5 billion of exit financing that was taken out six months later at 130, deploying roughly $10 billion across the full capital structure over 12-18 months.
- Zito frames Apollo's edge as having "no walls" between fund silos: instead of a private equity team hunting only for 15%+ return deals, teams across credit, private equity, and hybrid strategies compare notes on a single client and route the deal to whichever pool of capital fits the company's actual risk, from 5% investment-grade paper to 20%+ opportunistic equity, which he contrasts with most firms' fund-by-fund, "hammer looking for a nail" structure.
- On the future of asset allocation, Zito predicts the traditional 20% "alternatives sleeve" of a portfolio will dissolve as private and public assets converge in liquidity and ratings (a private, asset-backed Intel bond rated the same as a public one should be judged on risk-adjusted return, not on being labeled "private"), and expects secondary marketplaces, tokenized funds, and 24/7 trading of fund interests to expand access, particularly for the roughly 91% of private wealth clients who currently hold no alternatives allocation at all.
