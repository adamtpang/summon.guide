# Uber CEO on AI, Autonomous Vehicles, and the Future of Transportation

> **Key principle:** Dara Khosrowshahi frames Uber's entire strategy, mobility, delivery, autonomous vehicles, and travel, around one structural insight carried over from Expedia: at Uber, supply comes first and demand follows automatically, the inverse of the demand-led travel business he came from. He treats AV partnerships the same way OTAs treat hotel chains: coexist and aggregate rather than pick a single winner, while personally driving deliveries and shadowing frontline work to keep his own view of the business unfiltered.

*Dara Khosrowshahi on Uber's bet on AI, autonomous vehicles, and the future of transportation, Invest Like the Best · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ThMtheE5eO0) · 71 min · published 2026-06-03.*

## Key lessons

- Uber's core operating insight, learned by contrast with Expedia, is that the business runs supply-first: recruiting drivers, restaurants, and couriers into a market comes before demand, whereas Expedia built demand first and sourced hotel inventory in response; Khosrowshahi applies this same logic to winning the AV era, prioritizing partnerships and fleet supply over consumer acquisition.
- Uber has over 30 AV partnerships (Waymo, Nuro, Lucid, Nvidia, Wayve, WeRide, Pony.ai among them) and treats the market like the LLM foundation-model space, no single winner, so it positions itself as the go-to-market layer: securing depots and charging infrastructure, arranging fleet financing (including a newly announced roughly $1 billion financing line with Santander for EV and AV fleets), and building autonomous insurance, while partners focus purely on the driving software.
- AVs on Uber's network run about 30% busier than comparable human-driven vehicles (trips and revenue per vehicle per day), a utilization gain Khosrowshahi says materially changes the ROI math on expensive autonomous vehicles; he also cites a Lucid midsize AV built with Nuro priced around $60,000-$70,000, with hardware costs typically falling 30-40% per generation.
- Uber blew through its entire annual AI compute budget in a single quarter as engineers adopted agentic tools; the company's response was to lean into exploration with frontier models (OpenAI, Anthropic) for new interactions while planning to shift proven experiences to cheaper or open-source models at scale, and to meter headcount growth as individual engineer output rises rather than cut AI spend.
- Uber 1 has grown to 50 million members, up 50% year-over-year, and Khosrowshahi describes the membership program as loss-making in a member's first year (subsidizing rides, free delivery, surge protection, hotel discounts) but solidly profitable by year two through four, a bet on lifetime value he says he modeled explicitly on Amazon Prime's early "valley of despair" economics rather than fixed-cost travel loyalty programs.
- Uber Eats now represents roughly 13% of bookings sourced through cross-sell from the mobility app, and gross bookings for Eats have grown from under $1 billion when Khosrowshahi joined to over $100 billion; Uber Reserve, a pre-booked ride product built on a re-engineered 99%-plus reliability backend, has scaled to a run rate exceeding $5 billion despite not existing five to six years earlier.
- Uber's newer hotel business (via a deal with his former employer Expedia) gives Uber 1 members roughly 10-20% off around 10,000 hotels by passing back most of the deal economics, part of a broader plan to extend the brand from on-demand into pre-planned travel, an open strategic question Khosrowshahi flags as not guaranteed given Uber's brand is built on immediacy, not planning.
- Khosrowshahi personally delivered food by e-bike and drove Uber rides in San Francisco for roughly two years to understand the driver and courier experience firsthand, noting drivers keep the app open 6-10 hours a day so bug severity that a rider might hit monthly, a driver hits weekly; he ties this to a stated company value of "building with heart" and structures his schedule around deliberately unstructured, random interactions with employees he calls "troublemakers," treating them as the mutations that keep a large organization from stagnating.
- On leadership and information flow, Khosrowshahi credits his 20-plus years working for Barry Diller (starting as the analyst who built the LBO model during Paramount's hostile-takeover fight) with teaching him to go directly to unfiltered source material rather than processed reports, and credits Allen & Company's Herbert Allen with the principle of betting on people over companies; he calls himself an immigrant from Iran who arrived at age nine after his family "lost everything," a formative pressure he channels into competitiveness without letting it define his self-worth.
