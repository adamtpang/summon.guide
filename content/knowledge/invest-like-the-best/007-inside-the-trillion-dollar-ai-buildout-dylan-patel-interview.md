# Inside the Trillion-Dollar AI Buildout | Dylan Patel Interview

> **Key principle:** SemiAnalysis founder Dylan Patel argues the AI capex race is not circular nonsense but a sequencing problem: compute has to be built before the business that rents it exists, which forces OpenAI, Nvidia, Oracle, and the hyperscalers into financing structures where each backstops the others' bets. The entire wager only pays off if model capability keeps compounding fast enough to justify the next order of magnitude of spend, and if it stalls even briefly, Patel says the US economy tips into recession because so much current GDP growth is already flowing through this one trade.

*Dylan Patel on the economics of the AI compute buildout, Invest Like the Best · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kAIVualeQjM) · 122 min · published 2025-09-30.*

## Key lessons

- The core mechanic of deals like Nvidia-OpenAI: one gigawatt of data center capacity costs roughly $50 billion to build, and Nvidia's $100 billion pledge breaks into $10 billion per gigawatt, leaving OpenAI to source the other $40 billion via debt or infrastructure funds; of the $50 billion spent, about $35 billion flows to Nvidia as revenue, and at Nvidia's roughly 75% gross margin that generates about $30 billion of gross profit, half of which effectively cycles back to OpenAI as the equity stake, so Nvidia is financing its own demand while still keeping the other half of the profit.
- OpenAI's committed five-year compute deals run $10-15 billion per gigawatt per year, meaning a single gigawatt of capacity locks in $50-75 billion of cash outflow over the contract term, and Sam Altman has reportedly said he wants more than 10 gigawatts, which is the scale problem forcing OpenAI to recruit balance-sheet partners (Oracle, Microsoft, SoftBank-adjacent infrastructure funds) rather than self-fund.
- Anthropic's revenue jumped from roughly $1 billion or less to a $7-8 billion run rate in under a year, which Patel calls the fastest revenue ramp he has seen for anything at this scale, and traces almost entirely to coding products (Claude Code, plus Cursor, GitHub Copilot, and Windsurf all running Anthropic models under the hood).
- OpenAI deliberately kept GPT-5 close to GPT-4o in size and serving cost rather than making it dramatically bigger, because Patel argues the real bottleneck for the industry right now is serving capacity and cost, not model quality or latency: a much bigger, smarter model (like the abandoned GPT-4.5 scale-up) becomes too slow and expensive to serve at any meaningful user volume, so shrinking cost per token at a given quality tier matters more than chasing the next tier.
- Patel frames "tokonomics" around token demand roughly doubling every two months while hardware capacity cannot double that fast, offset by cost per token at a fixed quality level collapsing with algorithmic gains, citing GPT-3-level output quality as roughly 2,000 times cheaper to serve today than at launch.
- Neocloud and inference-provider economics hinge entirely on counterparty balance sheets: Blackwell GPU capacity amortized over six years costs about $2/hour, but six-month spot contracts can fetch $3-4/hour; Nebius signed a $19 billion multi-year deal with Microsoft that Patel estimates nets roughly $6 billion of gross profit, versus contracts with less creditworthy customers like OpenAI, which carry real counterparty risk despite similar headline margins.
- AI data centers currently account for only about 3-4% of US electricity demand, but a single 2-gigawatt data center draws roughly as much power as the city of Philadelphia, and 500 megawatts of buildout alone runs about $25 billion in capex; Patel points to supply-chain bottlenecks in turbines, transformers, and electricians (whose mobile-worker wages have roughly doubled in build-out regions like West Texas) as the real constraint, not a lack of demand.
- Patel estimates China has funneled on the order of $400-500 billion into its semiconductor ecosystem over the past decade through state-owned enterprises, tax policy, and provincial government funds, prioritizing supply-chain self-sufficiency over dominating end-user AI applications, in contrast to the US strategy of racing to build the largest training clusters and own the consumer-facing layer.
- He argues rising AI inference costs (COGS) combined with persistently high customer-acquisition costs will keep many pure software-as-a-service businesses from reaching profitable scale, drawing a comparison to China, where SaaS never took off the way it did in the US because in-house software development was roughly a fifth of US labor cost, making build-versus-buy tilt toward building.
