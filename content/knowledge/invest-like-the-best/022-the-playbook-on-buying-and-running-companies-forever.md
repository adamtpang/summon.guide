# The Playbook on Buying and Running Companies Forever

> **Key principle:** Luca Ferrari built Bending Spoons on the observation that going from zero to one in a startup is mostly luck, no correlation existed between the most talented founders he knew and which ones succeeded, while running a digital business exceptionally well is a repeatable skill built on discipline and functional excellence. So instead of founding new products, Bending Spoons buys 100% of proven digital businesses to hold and operate forever, then applies the same deep operational rebuild (rewritten code, redesigned UI, restructured teams) to each one.

*Luca Ferrari on Bending Spoons' acquire-and-operate-forever model, evaluating and pricing deals, and building a European tech institution, Invest Like the Best · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uLSXhmRHpFU) · 86 min · published 2025-11-04.*

## Key lessons

- Bending Spoons started with 40,000 euros left over from a failed startup called Evertale (a self-writing AI life diary, too early for the machine learning of 2010) after the VC waived its liquidation preference; the first acquisition, a small iOS keyboard-personalization app, cost 10,000 euros and returned 20,000, seeding a compounding pattern that Ferrari says turned "half a million a year" into roughly 1.3 billion in current revenue, still growing at about 75% a year on a per-share basis.
- The firm's structural edge over standalone companies is fluid resource movement: because hiring, training, and shrinking teams is slow, Ferrari argues most single-product companies run years behind their optimal staffing level, while Bending Spoons can move R&D and marketing talent across its portfolio companies as opportunities open and close, avoiding both the under-resourcing and the bloated-team problems that plague standalone businesses.
- Talent density compounds as a moat: Bending Spoons received roughly 800,000 job applications in 2025 and hired about 250 people, a roughly 1-in-3,000 acceptance rate, an advantage Ferrari attributes to the appeal of fast growth, wide variety of technical challenges, and over a decade of deliberate employer-brand investment that a single-product company like a standalone Evernote could never match on its own.
- The Evernote acquisition (bid roughly 50% above the next-highest offer) shows the depth of the model's operational rebuild: over about two and a half years the team shipped roughly 250 significant product improvements, largely rewrote the codebase and cloud infrastructure so notes now sync in as little as 1 to 10% of their previous time, raised prices by around 60%, and still achieved record customer retention.
- Bending Spoons enforces a strict process discipline when pricing deals: the team debates and finalizes every input assumption before ever looking at the model's output, deliberately forbidding anyone from peeking at the resulting P&L first (to prevent post-hoc rationalization toward a nicer number), then runs a Monte Carlo simulation across assumption distributions to generate the IRR and NPV ranges that actually guide the offer.
- The firm's negotiating posture mirrors Warren Buffett's: it makes an early offer close to the maximum it's willing to pay rather than lowballing, on the theory that a reputation for fair, non-negotiable offers wins more deals over time; Ferrari says the firm has never lost a competitive bid it formally made, though it walks away entirely (rather than raise) when a seller wants more than its model supports.
- Bending Spoons' near-miss on Grindr in 2019, a nine-month, all-hands effort to acquire the LGBTQ+ dating app from its Chinese owner during a forced CFIUS divestiture, would have quadrupled the company's size but was lost to a higher bid after the firm had capped out its available capital; Ferrari says the deal absorbed so much focus that overall company growth stalled for roughly two years, teaching the firm not to concentrate its effort on a single acquisition again.
- The firm remains almost entirely self-funded: aside from moderate bank debt (up to about 3.5x trailing EBITDA) and roughly 10% total dilution from a handful of equity raises used mainly to fund liquidity events for employees (who can opt to take part of their cash pay as discounted equity), growth has been financed by reinvested earnings rather than outside venture or private-equity capital.
