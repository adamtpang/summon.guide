# GPUs, TPUs, & The Economics of AI Explained | Gavin Baker Interview

> **Key principle:** Gavin Baker reads the AI buildout as a cost-of-tokens war, not a capability race: whichever chip platform can turn power and capital into the cheapest inference wins, because once a company has a decisive cost advantage it can price aggressively to starve capital-constrained rivals. The physical mechanics of each chip transition (power draw, cooling, ramp time) explain the timing of AI progress just as much as any algorithmic breakthrough does.

*Gavin Baker on Nvidia versus Google TPU economics, chip transition mechanics, and why AI infrastructure runs on watts and margins, Invest Like the Best · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=cmUo4841KQw) · 88 min · published 2025-12-09.*

## Key lessons

- Nvidia's Blackwell rollout was delayed by sheer physical scale: racks jumped from roughly 1,000 pounds to 3,000 pounds and power draw went from about 30 kilowatts (the equivalent of 30 American homes) to 130 kilowatts (130 homes), forcing a shift from air cooling to liquid cooling. Real, scaled Blackwell deployments only began in the last three to four months before this conversation was taped.
- Because Blackwell arrived late, AI progress from mid-2024 through Gemini 3 leaned almost entirely on two newer scaling laws, reinforcement learning with verified rewards and test-time compute, rather than the original pre-training scaling law. Baker argues that without reasoning models bridging that gap, frontier AI progress would have stalled for roughly 18 months.
- Google trained Gemini 3 on its 2024-2025 era TPU v6 and v7 chips, which Baker considers a generation behind Blackwell, yet Google still became the lowest-cost producer of AI tokens. He believes that let Google run inference at close to a negative 30 percent margin to starve capital-constrained competitors, a strategy he expects to lose its edge once GB300 racks (a drop-in upgrade to existing GB200 data centers) ramp at scale.
- On the TPU supply chain: Google handles the front-end chip design while Broadcom manages the back end and the Taiwan Semiconductor relationship, at a 50-55 percent gross margin. On an estimated $30 billion of 2027 TPU revenue, that works out to roughly $15 billion paid to Broadcom, which Baker reads as the reason Google is now bringing in MediaTek, a warning shot on the road to eventually bringing chip production fully in-house.
- Baker cites Jensen Huang's line that nobody builds data centers faster than Elon Musk as the reason he expects xAI to train the first Blackwell-based frontier model in early 2026. Historically it takes six to nine months after a new chip generation ships before it performs as well as the prior, fully tuned generation (Hopper itself took six to twelve months to clearly outperform its predecessor).
- Baker points to CH Robinson's third-quarter earnings as the first hard evidence of AI-driven revenue lift at a non-tech Fortune 500 company: AI let the freight broker quote price and availability on 100 percent of inbound requests within seconds, versus roughly 60 percent of requests within 15 to 45 minutes before, and the stock rose about 20 percent on the results.
- Baker argues legacy SaaS companies are repeating the mistake brick-and-mortar retailers made with e-commerce. Traditional software runs 70-90 percent gross margins because it is written once and distributed cheaply, but AI has to recompute an answer on every single query, so AI-native competitors operate profitably around 35-40 percent gross margins. A SaaS company defending an 80 percent margin while trying to build an agent strategy is, in his view, guaranteeing it loses that business.
- His most speculative thesis: data centers belong in space. Satellites can sit in constant sunlight (about six times the solar irradiance available on Earth) so they need no batteries, they can radiate heat to near absolute zero for free instead of running complex liquid cooling, and they can link to each other with lasers through vacuum, which move faster than light through fiber-optic cable on the ground. He calls it the most important infrastructure shift of the next three to four years, gated mainly by how many Starship-class rockets exist to launch the hardware.
