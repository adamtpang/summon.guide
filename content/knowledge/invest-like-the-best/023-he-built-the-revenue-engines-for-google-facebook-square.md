# He Built The Revenue Engines for Google, Facebook & Square

> **Key principle:** Gokul Rajaram, who built ad and growth products at Google, Facebook, and Square, argues that AI's collapse of the cost to build software makes judgment, deciding what deserves to be built and whether the output is actually good, the one skill that stays scarce and valuable. He extends this into a durability framework for the current AI wave: businesses need a real moat (a scarce asset, a control point over money or data, network effects, or hardware) or they get eaten by whoever else can now build the same feature in a weekend.

## Key lessons

- Product management itself is being restructured by AI agents: PMs increasingly write and commit prototype code directly (a formal "prototyping interview" has appeared in hiring loops), the designer-to-engineer ratio is shifting from roughly 1-to-3 or 1-to-10 toward 1-to-20 as AI absorbs routine design-system work, and PMs now co-own "evals," building AI systems to judge AI output, because non-deterministic software means the same input can produce different results and someone has to define what "good" looks like.
- His durability framework for AI-native startups names five moats: ownership of a scarce asset (a license or unique regulatory position), a control point over money or data flowing through the product, hardware that's costly to replace, membership in an essential workflow, and network effects; he cites Sierra's access to Brett Taylor himself as an example of a scarce-asset moat and warns that a startup without one of these gets outbuilt by whichever foundation-model company or in-house team decides to copy the workflow.
- Legacy systems of record are actively blocking the AI companies trying to build on top of them: he cites Salesforce-owned Slack cutting off data access for Glean specifically to prevent it from "sucking the value" out of Slack, and says verticals from legal (Filevine, Clio) to healthcare (Epic) are now either blocking APIs, charging per call, or bundling their own agents free, forcing AI-native challengers to spend a year or two building their own migration tooling (he cites one portfolio company that hired an Eastern European engineering team for two years just to build a Salesforce-migration tool) rather than simply integrating.
- His risk framework splits incumbent software by pricing model: seat-priced, utility-based products like Zendesk are most exposed because an AI agent can sit alongside the product and quietly siphon off seats, while data-moat products like NetSuite's ERP are comparatively insulated because ripping out a system that "runs the whole business" is what he calls career-limiting for whoever approves it, regardless of how good a challenger's AI features are.
- From his time building AdSense at Google, he recounts Sergey Brin killing a half-built, headcount-heavy manual publisher-approval system by asking "why do you need to approve them at all," redirecting the team toward letting every publisher activate immediately and only reviewing a URL once it crossed 100 ad impressions, a reactive, transaction-level risk model he says Square later reused: accepting roughly 95% of small-business applicants up front and running machine-learning fraud checks per transaction instead of gatekeeping at signup.
- At Facebook, he traces the origin of "custom audiences," the lookalike-targeting concept now foundational to most ad systems, to a specific frustration from Zynga CEO Mark Pincus, whose games got the bulk of their revenue from a small number of high-spending "whale" players; Mark Zuckerberg personally proposed letting advertisers upload their own known best customers so Facebook could find similar users, an idea Rajaram says Zuckerberg generated himself after about a year of sitting in on the ads team's meetings to learn the domain from the inside.
- He identifies exactly three durable ways to make money in advertising: own a coveted first-party audience on a surface where users express intent (Google Search, Facebook, and now ChatGPT, which he argues uniquely combines intent and identity data that neither Google nor Facebook alone ever had); deliver a specific measurable outcome without owning any inventory (AppLovin, a company valued over $100 billion built almost entirely on driving mobile app installs); or become the exclusive distribution partner for a major demand source (The Trade Desk handling Procter & Gamble's non-Google, non-Facebook ad spend); building a middleman layer directly on top of Google or Facebook's own ad platforms, he says, reliably fails because those platforms absorb any capability a challenger proves out.
- His format for a weekly CEO email, adopted by roughly 15 CEOs he has advised, has three fixed sections: "top of mind" (getting 60-70% of the writing effort, covering whatever is genuinely on the CEO's mind across product, business, and team), a performance update, and a miscellaneous section for shout-outs and customer quotes; he argues more candor about real concerns is better than less because it invites the team to actually contribute solutions rather than just receive information.
