# Why the AI Boom Is Just Getting Started

> **Key principle:** Whale Rock Capital's Alex Sacerdote frames every major tech investment through an S-curve, competitive-advantage, underappreciated-earnings-power lens, and argues enterprise AI adoption is so early (he calls it an "L-curve," not an S-curve) that the market is still pricing linear growth into businesses about to compound exponentially. His firm's largest recent bet, Anthropic, was sized on the belief that coding alone could be a $500 billion market, while the firm has simultaneously gone underweight application-layer software because AI is inflating infrastructure spend faster than software vendors can monetize it.

## Key lessons

- Whale Rock's Anthropic thesis was built around coding economics: the firm learned that heavy internal users were spending roughly $100 a day on tokens (about $20,000-$30,000 a year), and against a global population of roughly 20 million professional coders, that implied a market north of $500 billion from coding use alone, even on what Sacerdote called "seven, eight, nine month old technology" at the time of the investment.
- Whale Rock invested in Anthropic's funding round at a $180 billion valuation in August 2025, and the firm's own projections at the time (going from roughly $1 billion of revenue toward a hoped $9 billion) were blown past faster than they expected, which Sacerdote cites as the fastest revenue ramp he has tracked in two decades of covering technology.
- The firm's investment framework has three legs: identify where a business sits on its adoption S-curve, confirm it holds a durable competitive advantage, and buy when the market underappreciates its future earnings power; using this framework, Whale Rock bought Nvidia in 2023 at about 4x earnings, Tesla in 2019 at about 5x earnings ahead of the EV S-curve, and effectively got Amazon's AWS business "for free" by buying the stock while the market still valued it as a retailer.
- Sacerdote calls enterprise AI adoption an "L-curve" rather than an S-curve because it's starting from under 1% penetration with no visible ceiling yet; he cites Google's Sundar Pichai putting real AI usage at roughly 10 basis points of the world's knowledge workers, against Anthropic's reported 14-15 million daily active users, implying most of that base is still in the "tinkerer" adoption stage.
- Whale Rock sold down almost all of its application-layer software holdings and went net short software entering the year that helped its returns in Q1, reasoning that AI is currently only 1-2% of most software companies' revenue while inference spend competes directly with software budgets for the same dollars, undercutting the pricing power and seat-based growth those companies relied on.
- The firm applies a modified "rule of 40" for hardware and infrastructure names: multiply the percentage of a company's revenue tied to AI by its market share in that category, and look for companies scoring high on both, which led it into names most investors had written off as commodity suppliers, including Celestica (which Whale Rock bought at roughly 8x earnings after discovering it was the sole supplier of Google's TPU servers) and Corning, whose fiber went into a single Microsoft data center build in a quantity enough to circle the globe about four and a half times.
- The thesis for hardware is "decommoditization": for roughly 40 years data center hardware barely changed (Intel x86 dominance, 7-year upgrade cycles), but AI workloads growing about 10x a year are pushing every physical layer, printed circuit boards moving from about 10 layers to 40 layers, high-bandwidth memory being stacked 10 chips high, networking upgrading annually instead of every seven years, past prior physical limits, converting former low-margin commodity suppliers into critical, hard-to-swap infrastructure vendors with rising prices and rising margins.
- Whale Rock's Stripe investment (a large secondary block bought during COVID in April 2020 at a roughly $35 billion valuation) was underwritten from partial disclosures: the firm knew Stripe had disclosed over $500 billion in total payment volume and estimated its take rate at 40-50 basis points versus competitor Adyen's 25-30 basis points, and actual figures later came in even stronger, with TPV closer to $1 trillion.
- On adoption speed, Sacerdote contrasts the radio S-curve (near-100% penetration in about 7 years, the fastest historical example the firm has studied) with categories like dishwashers or B2B software, which move much slower because they require being physically "plugged into" existing infrastructure; he flags this as a live risk for AI in security-conscious, slow-moving enterprises, versus its much faster uptake among individual consumers who can just open a browser.
