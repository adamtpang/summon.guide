# Legendary Trader Paul Tudor Jones on AI Risk, Bubbles and Buffett

> **Key principle:** Jones frames fifty years of trading as a boxing match against the market: mostly jabbing and gathering information, with occasional knockout openings created by too much leverage or a policy mistake. He pairs that with an unusually blunt warning that AI development is running on a build-break-iterate model with no regulatory brakes, applied to a tail risk that could cost millions of lives.

## Key lessons

- Jones draws a hard line between trading and investing: his fund has carried a -0.12 correlation to the S&P 500 across 40 years, meaning essentially all of its returns are alpha rather than market beta, the opposite of a buy-and-hold approach that just rides the index up.
- His clearest lesson in composure came from mentor Eli Tullis, a cotton trader who got wiped out one weekend when a drought broke and the market opened limit down; Tullis showed up to lunch that same day charming his wife's friends as if nothing had happened, teaching Jones that visible calm after a loss is what lets you come back the next day.
- He uses boxing as his working metaphor for markets: most of trading is jabbing and probing for an opening, but a handful of moments a year offer a real knockout shot, citing his 2020 Bitcoin trade and his 2022 short on two-year Treasury notes as examples.
- The 2022 two-year note trade was built on watching the Fed stay too accommodative for too long (partly, in his telling, because the Fed chair wanted reappointment), so once that reappointment happened he read it as the signal that policy normalization, and a short in short-duration rates, was coming.
- His live example of a developing trade is dollar/yen: the yen has been undervalued for roughly two years, Japan holds something like $4.5 trillion in net international investment position with most of it unhedged in dollars, and he sees a new, reform-minded Japanese prime minister as the catalytic event that finally triggers the currency's re-rating.
- On bubbles, he points to stock market capitalization relative to GDP as his key gauge: about 65% at the 1929 peak, 85-90% in 1987, 170% in 2000, and roughly 252% today, and warns that a wave of new IPOs and post-lockup unlocks could reverse a decade of buyback-driven share shrinkage just as capital-heavy tech spending eats into buyback capacity.
- His formative risk-management memory is watching Bunker Hunt's cornered silver position collapse in the early 1980s: Hunt drove silver from roughly $3-12 an ounce to $50 and briefly became several times richer than the world's next-wealthiest man, only for exchange rule changes to force liquidation and crash the price back under $10 within about eight weeks, cementing Jones's lifelong obsession with staying liquid.
- On artificial intelligence, Jones describes a private gathering of roughly 35-40 people, including modelers from the largest AI labs, where he asked how AI safety actually gets resolved and says the consensus answer was that meaningful action would likely wait until a catastrophic accident killed tens of millions of people; he argues for mandatory watermarking of AI-generated content as a first, achievable safeguard.
