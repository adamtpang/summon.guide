# The Gift and The Curse of Staying Private with Bill Gurley

> **Key principle:** Gurley argues that venture capital's usual boom-bust correction cycle got interrupted, first by a decade of zero interest rates, then by the AI wave arriving before the ZIRP-era excess could unwind, leaving roughly a thousand overvalued "zombie unicorns" still marked near their 2021 peaks with no one incentivized to write them down. The result is a private market that increasingly resembles a closed loop: companies stay private longer, late-stage funds buy access that used to belong to public markets, and LPs are left holding illiquid positions for twice as long as they underwrote.

*Bill Gurley on the state of venture and private markets, Invest Like the Best · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=o3rrGzTDH4k) · 87 min · published 2025-06-10.*

## Key lessons

- Gurley estimates roughly 1,000 private "zombie unicorns," companies that each raised over $1 billion during the zero-interest-rate era, sit on LP books at an estimated $3 trillion in aggregate value, still marked near their 2021 peak valuations; he notes the people responsible for setting those marks (fund managers, sometimes bonused on paper valuations) have little structural incentive to write them down.
- The liquidation-preference mechanic he flags as underappreciated: because the total dollars raised become the liquidation preference in an acquisition, a company that raised $300 million but is now worth $400 million could see liquidation preferences claim roughly 75% of a sale, which discourages both founders and investors from acknowledging a markdown even when everyone privately knows the price is wrong.
- Gurley cites underpricing data from economist Jay Ritter showing roughly 25 to 26% average IPO underpricing plus a roughly 7% bank fee, putting the effective cost of a traditional IPO near 33%, which he argues (alongside abundant private capital) explains why even fast-growing, successful companies increasingly choose to stay private rather than list.
- Late-stage "too good to refuse" tender offers, the model he credits Thrive Capital with popularizing (citing Databricks as a recent example), let a handful of investors buy stakes of around 30% in a hot private company, an allocation they could never get in an oversubscribed IPO (often 1-2%); Gurley calls this an effective way for those funds to "hoard" a company's most valuable compounding growth years and then pitch LPs that private access is now the only way in.
- He points to Yale University, whose endowment under David Swensen pioneered the "Yale model" of heavy illiquid-asset allocation (he cites a roughly 13% compounding return over 35 years), now itself shopping $6 billion of private-equity secondaries; Gurley reads this as a signal that a once-contrarian strategy stops working once every major institution has copied it.
- LP holding periods for venture funds have stretched from a historical 5-to-7-year window to roughly 10-to-15 years; combining that delay with ongoing employee equity dilution of 3-6% a year, Gurley calculates that $100 of expected return delayed five extra years would need to grow to roughly $250 just to match the return investors originally underwrote.
- On AI-specific valuations, Gurley flags that some reported AI company revenue may be resold compute counted multiple times across a stack of wrapper, model, and hosting layers, several running at negative gross margin, which makes revenue-multiple comparisons (some AI startups trading at 10-20x revenue) harder to trust at face value than they first appear.
- His closing advice to founders operating inside this environment: unit economics don't need to be solved today (since AI model costs per token have fallen roughly 100x across generations and pricing will likely follow that curve down), but design products so a customer acquired later gets a measurably better experience than an early customer, since that data-driven improvement loop is where durable network effects now come from.
- Gurley borrows Reed Hoffman's line that startups are "born as pirates" but eventually have to become a navy, arguing founders who treat operating discipline and governance as bureaucratic red tape rather than a leadership skill to be learned (as roughly 30 founders reportedly did under coach Bill Campbell) put their companies at risk once they cross the $100 million to $1 billion revenue range.
