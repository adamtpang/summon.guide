# Inside General Atlantic: How a $100B Growth Equity Firm Invests

> **Key principle:** Escobari frames investing as spearfishing: decide where to anchor, let small opportunities pass, and hold your breath until the big fish arrives, a patience he learned watching 3G Capital's founders wait five years to buy Brahma beer and then compound it into a $60 billion-plus position. General Atlantic institutionalizes that patience structurally, through evergreen fundraising and firm-wide profit sharing, rather than leaving it to individual willpower.

*Martín Escobari on General Atlantic's approach to global growth equity, Invest Like the Best · [watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=a34VyGpf8s4) · 73 min · published 2025-11-25.*

## Key lessons

- Escobari's foundational case study, drawn from working under 3G Capital's founders, is Brahma: they waited five years to buy the Brazilian beer company for roughly $80 million (closing in one week when its Swiss owners panicked before a 1989 election), then patiently timed follow-on acquisitions of Antarctica, Interbrew, and eventually Anheuser-Busch over the following two decades, compounding the position past $60 billion.
- General Atlantic's loss ratio on invested capital is about 4%, versus a typical 20-40% for venture and growth equity, which Escobari attributes to refusing "binary risk": the firm's worst-case underwriting scenario is that a company simply grows into the valuation already paid, which limits which deals and market moments it will touch rather than relying on picking winners.
- GA runs a hybrid evergreen fundraising model instead of the industry-standard five-year fund cycle, so it is never forced to deploy capital on a raising schedule or sell assets to return capital before the next fund; Escobari argues the standard cycle causes firms to be out of dry powder exactly when markets are cheapest.
- Firm compensation is pooled across all professionals based on total firm performance rather than individual deal performance, a structure Escobari initially resisted ("this communism didn't work in the Soviet Union") before concluding it forces genuine cross-team collaboration, since anyone not pulling their weight loses standing rather than just missing an individual bonus.
- Escobari's own trick for outperforming in Latin America for years was that General Atlantic deliberately never raised a dedicated Latin America fund, since a regional fund forces buying when a region is hot (near the top) and selling when it's out of favor (near the bottom); without that structural pressure, the firm could buy distressed and sell into strength.
- As of the taping, Escobari cites US public equities trading around 26x earnings (97th percentile of the last 25 years) for roughly 4% forecast growth, versus Europe at about 14x, Mexico at about 10x, and Brazil at about 9x, using that gap as the specific case for pushing global investors to diversify away from the roughly 90-95% US concentration he sees in many wealthy families' portfolios.
- General Atlantic has trained an internal AI system, nicknamed by staff, on 45 years of the firm's deal history to vote alongside its five human investment committee members on every proposed deal every Tuesday; after three years of live back-testing it has outperformed the humans, though Escobari cautions a system trained only on the past is, so far, mainly proven at predicting the past.
- His hiring-reference technique is to frame the call around the specific role and its stakes for both employer and candidate ("help me assess if this is a good risk for me and for him") rather than asking generic character questions, on the reasoning that people rarely volunteer negative information about a friend unless the framing makes the shared risk explicit.
- Escobari's broader career observation is that the youngest investors chase deal volume and "notches on the belt" for validation, while patience, the willingness to wait years for the right opportunity, is something that has to be learned with experience and is the single hardest trait to teach.
