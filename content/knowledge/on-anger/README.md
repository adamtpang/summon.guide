## Caveats

- Victorian translation (Aubrey Stewart). Chapter numbers follow this edition's Roman-numeral sections; modern editions subdivide further (for example 3.36.1-3), so locators are chapter level only.
- The Wikisource header gives the edition year as 1900 (Bohn's Classical Library printing); the translation first appeared in 1889. Stewart numbers these as the third, fourth and fifth books of the Dialogues.
- The definition of anger as a desire to punish an injury sits partly in a lacuna in Book I chapter 2 and is confirmed by Seneca's own restatement in chapter 3 and by the translator's footnote quoting Lactantius.
- Book II chapters 19 and 20 rest on the ancient four-element theory of temperament; note 003 flags this as period physiology.
- The text contains graphic cruelty (Cambyses, Harpagus, Lysimachus, Sulla, Caligula) and an endorsement of suicide as an escape from tyranny in Book III chapter 15; notes report these neutrally. Book I chapters 15 and 16 also accept capital punishment and the killing of weak newborns as acts of reason without anger; this is not carried into the lessons but a guide should not present Seneca's view of punishment as gentle.
- Seneca addresses boys, slaves and Roman household hierarchy as given; Book I chapter 20 calls anger a vice of women and children. Not included in the lessons.
- The translator's footnote marks an error in Book III chapter 23: Antigonus was not Alexander's grandfather. Note 006 does not repeat that claim.
- Historical anecdotes (Herodotus stories, Plato and Socrates sayings) are reported as Seneca tells them, not independently verified.
- Each note has at most one quotation under ten words, checked against the downloaded text. The last bullet of note 007 is labelled as a modern application.

No source text is stored here. Every lesson is a paraphrase.
