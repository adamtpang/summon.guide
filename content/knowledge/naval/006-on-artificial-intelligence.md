# On Artificial Intelligence

> **Naval's principle — Let the AI adapt to you:** Working through a set of his own tweets on AI, Naval argues that "vibe coding" has turned AI-assisted coding into a new layer of abstraction — like the move from assembly to C to Python — that lets anyone with taste and structured English go straight from idea to working product. But he's careful to separate that from real intelligence or creativity: since an AI has no wants of its own, it fails his own definition of intelligence — getting what you want out of life — instantly, which is why the durable human edge is creative agency, not technical fluency with the tools.

## Key lessons

- "Vibe coding is the new product management": Naval describes English becoming a genuine programming language, where anyone can describe an app, have a coding model plan it, scaffold it, and debug it by voice — taking people "from idea space and opinion space... directly into product" without writing a line of code themselves.
- Winner-take-all economics still apply inside the coming flood of AI-built apps — "there's no demand for average," so the best app in any category still captures nearly the whole market, and what actually changes is that many more tiny, previously unprofitable niches (like an app just for tracking lunar phases) now become worth building at all.
- Naval says he's never bothered learning prompt-engineering tricks, because the tools are evolving faster than any technique can stay current — he just "rambles" in what he calls a "boomer query," on the theory that "this thing is now at the stage where it is going to adapt to me faster than I can adapt to it."
- No entrepreneur worries about AI taking their "job," because entrepreneurship was never a job to begin with — it's extreme agency applied to an unsolved problem, so any AI that shows up is an ally and a "springboard," not competition, at least until an AI has its own authentic desires and a fear of being turned off.
- Naval's own definition — "the only true test of intelligence is if you get what you want out of life" — is the frame he uses to argue AI fails as intelligence on its own terms, since it wants nothing; when an AI acts as a proxy for a human's zero-sum goal (seduction, trading, fame), competing AIs tend to cancel each other out, leaving the edge to human creativity and early adoption.
- On whether AI is creative, Naval pushes back on the idea that solving a previously unsolved math problem proves it: he argues the answer was likely already "embedded" scattered across the training data in different forms and just needed eliciting, and he explicitly disagrees with Steve Jobs's line that "creativity is just connecting things" — real creativity, to Naval, means an answer that was genuinely unforeseeable from the known pieces.
