# The Beginning of Infinity, Part 1

> **Naval's principle — Good explanations over induction:** Naval opens a series with physicist and podcaster Brett Hall (host of the Theory of Knowledge podcast, not David Deutsch himself) to work through David Deutsch's *The Beginning of Infinity*, the book Naval credits with restructuring how he thinks more than almost anything else he's read. The core idea they build up together is that knowledge doesn't come from accumulating observations and extrapolating trends — it comes from creative conjecture, tested against reality by how hard the explanation is to vary and how narrow and risky the predictions it makes actually are.

*Naval Ravikant in conversation with Brett Hall.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jEmJIA0pEf0) · 66 min · published 2021-07-02.

## Key lessons

- Science is not induction — Brett and Naval use the boiling-water example: if you plot rising temperature on a stove and extrapolate the line, "Bayesian" induction would predict 1,000°C after two hours, but the real explanation (latent heat, escape velocity of particles) predicts a plateau at the boiling point, which is why explanation, not trend-extrapolation, is what actually predicts reality.
- A good explanation has to be "hard to vary" — the old Greek myth that Persephone's seasonal trips to Hades cause the seasons is testable in a loose sense but infinitely flexible (swap in any god), whereas the Earth's 23-degree axial tilt makes narrow, precise, falsifiable predictions about exactly when summer and winter start at each latitude.
- The double-slit experiment's interference pattern is explained, in Deutsch's "sober" multiverse reading of quantum theory, by particles in other universes — obeying the same laws of physics as ours — interacting with the ones we can detect; Naval and Brett frame this as simply extending a very old pattern in the history of science, where our picture of physical reality keeps getting larger.
- Knowledge, not raw material, is what makes something a resource: uranium sat inert inside the rock pitchblende for the planet's entire geological history until nuclear physics existed to explain what it was for, which is Deutsch's basis for arguing that resources are effectively unbounded because human creativity is unbounded.
- Even mathematics isn't certain — the "draw a unique straight line through two points" thought experiment collapses the moment you wrap the paper around a basketball and push a pen through both dots, illustrating that the felt certainty of a mathematical proof is not proof of the proof's truth, and that "all knowledge is conjectural."
- Optimists and pessimists differ mainly in their feedback loop: entrepreneurs get corrected by "mother nature" and free markets, while pessimistic intellectuals get rewarded socially for eloquent doom, which Naval argues is why pessimistic predictions have a poor track record and why he now regrets recording an earlier "doomsayer" podcast on human extinction.
