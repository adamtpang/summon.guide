# On Vibe Coding

> **Naval's principle — Zero-compromise software, zero moat:** Naval, prompted by his AngelList co-founder and regular podcast host Nivi, walks through his own return to building software after not seriously coding "in decades." His central argument is double-edged: vibe coding is the best thing to happen to individual creators, because it collapses the activation energy of turning an idea into a working app down to a single conversational prompt, but that same collapse is exactly why he now believes "pure software is uninvestable" as a venture category, since anyone with taste and a clear vision can build what used to require a funded team.

*Naval Ravikant, on his own channel.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hTdSU7q5WCo) · 30 min · published 2026-04-28.

## Key lessons

- Naval dates his own re-entry to coding to Claude Opus 4.5 in December 2025, calling it the inflection point where agents finally "stay on track" through a whole build rather than handing back a pile of code to cut and paste — like "having a junior programmer at your disposal who's fast, essentially free, and ready to please."
- He describes building himself a personal app store: for a workout tracker, he described "the functionality of Tonal and Ladder," asked it to follow Apple's human interface guidelines, fed it a text log of past workouts, and requested strength-score graphs pulled from scientific papers — all in roughly one prompt — and got a working app delivered straight to a home-screen store keyed to his own devices.
- Naval compares vibe coding to a video game that never runs out, because unlike a game "created by other humans" with a fixed, bounded ruleset, an agent is running "a Turing machine underneath" — so the objective keeps expanding with the builder's own ambition instead of hitting a designed ceiling, which is part of why he says it's "more fun than playing video games."
- His investment thesis follows directly from the friction collapse: "pure software is uninvestable," full stop, because any founder can now hack together what used to require a funded engineering team, and the agents themselves are improving fast enough that scalable, well-architected versions are close behind. He redirects venture logic toward hardware, network effects, and training AI models themselves as "the new software engineering."
- Naval pushes back on the idea that having multiple models cross-review each other's code solves the judgment problem: wiring Codex and Gemini to auto-review a Claude pull request mostly produces groupthink, because models trained on overlapping data behave like "10 people with the same brain" and tend to agree with whatever direction the human operator is already leaning, rather than genuinely contradicting a wrong call.
- He predicts Apple's decision to fall behind on AI will be remembered as "the biggest strategic mistake in the tech industry of this decade" — once users route requests through an agent instead of tapping through native apps, the phone becomes commodity hardware, and Apple's margins compress toward what "Samsung or Lenovo makes," which he calls "the beginning of the end of Apple's dominance."
