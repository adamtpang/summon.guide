# David Deutsch: Knowledge Creation and The Human Race, Part 2

> **Naval's principle — There is no final bucket of truths:** Naval and David Deutsch continue their conversation by dismantling the popular picture of science as a project that eventually finishes — a "bucket" of established truths we fill up and then retire to deckchairs while AI handles the rest. Deutsch's counter is that growth of knowledge is genuinely open-ended: every solved problem creates new ones, and even concepts we treat as neutral yardsticks, like simplicity, only make sense relative to whatever physics we currently believe, which means there's no vantage point outside the process from which to declare it complete.

## Key lessons

- Deutsch rejects the "sun chairs and cocktails" ending of science outright — Popper's one real idea, that everything begins with problems and there's no shortcut around solving them, means progress can't terminate in a finished set of truths because solving a problem always generates new ones.
- He draws a three-way distinction rarely made explicit: a demonstration shows how the world works with no rival theory at stake (pouring acid into a base for a class), an experiment adjudicates between two live competing explanations, and a measurement — like Cavendish weighing the gravitational constant after Newton's theory was already accepted — refines a theory nobody was doubting; Deutsch calls it "very rare" for a real experiment, in his strict sense, to actually happen.
- Simplicity is not a neutral, prior standard you can use to judge theories — Deutsch argues you can only define "simple" once you already have a theory of physics to define bits and encodings with, which is exactly why quantum computation made previously "complex" problems suddenly "simple": the physics changed, so the yardstick changed with it.
- Self-refuting ideas are what happens when you finally take a theory seriously instead of dismissing it — Deutsch uses solipsism as the example: if your dream contains a stranger in a yellow suit you never consciously invented, you now owe an explanation for where that came from, and building that explanation quietly destroys the solipsist position from the inside.
- Deutsch pushes back on Thomas Kuhn's "young iconoclasts overthrow old stick-in-the-muds" story of scientific revolutions, calling it fiction with no real historical example; instead he shows Newton's own theory already contained the problems — the instantaneous action of gravity, Olbers' paradox about a bright night sky — that Einstein's general relativity later solved, so new theories inherit and answer their predecessor's unsolved problems rather than replacing them wholesale.
- England's Enlightenment, in Deutsch's account, succeeded where continental versions collapsed because it was non-utopian: reformers extended existing privileges outward — "an Englishman's home is his castle" moving from aristocrats to ordinary homeowners — rather than abolishing institutions and starting from a blank slate, which he credits with Britain's fascist movement in the 1930s never winning a single seat in Parliament.
