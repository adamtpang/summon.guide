# How to Get Rich

> **Naval's principle — Wealth over status:** Naval's core claim across this marathon session is that getting rich is a learnable skill, not a lottery ticket: separate the positive-sum game of wealth creation from the zero-sum game of status, then stack four assets — specific knowledge nobody can train you for, leverage through code and media (plus capital and labor, used carefully), accountability under your own name, and judgment sharpened by clear thinking — until compounding does the rest. None of it works quickly; it works because you stay in the game long enough for one outcome, out of a thousand possible parallel-universe versions of your life, to land.

*Naval Ravikant, on his own channel.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1-TZqOsVCNM) · 216 min · published 2019-12-25.

## Key lessons

- Wealth is not status: wealth is "assets that earn while you sleep," while status is a zero-sum ranking game inherited from tribal hierarchies — attacking wealth creators is usually a bid for status dressed up as virtue.
- There are four kinds of luck, and only the fourth is worth chasing: blind luck, the luck of hustle, the luck of a prepared mind that can spot an opportunity others miss, and the rarest kind — luck that finds a uniquely built character, which Naval says eventually stops being luck and starts being destiny, quoting Disraeli: "we make our fortunes and we call them fate."
- You will not get rich renting out your time, because in almost any salaried job your inputs and outputs stay tightly coupled hour for hour; wealth requires owning equity in a business or product where one person's judgment can multiply into wildly disconnected output, the gap between a lumberjack and a software engineer who "can create Bitcoin."
- Specific knowledge can't be taught in a classroom — if it could, it could be mass-produced and paid minimum wage; Naval says his own specific knowledge was first spotted by his mother, who noticed as a teenager he was already critiquing the local pizza parlor's ordering process, long before he identified the pattern in himself.
- Leverage has moved from labor (messy, requires managing people) and capital (needs pre-existing credibility to raise) to code and media, the first truly permissionless forms of leverage in history — echoing Archimedes' "give me a lever long enough... and I will move the earth," anyone with a laptop can now command an army of servers that works all night for free.
- In an age of infinite leverage, judgment — "knowing the long-term effects of your decisions" — becomes the deciding skill, which is why Naval collapses the whole framework into two words, "productize yourself," and why he insists being ethical is simply "long-term greedy": trust is what lets you keep compounding with the same people for decades.
