# Full Episode: The AI Industrial Revolution

> **Naval's principle — From producer to verifier:** Talking with three founders who are each building their own factories rather than buying off-the-shelf parts, Naval argues that AI is simultaneously commoditizing software (any idea that can be specified in English can now be built) and, in China's case, hardware. What's left as a defensible moat is training the models themselves, plus the human taste and judgment needed to steer them — which is why he thinks the deepest change isn't job loss, it's that engineers, lawyers, and operators are all turning into people who sign off on AI-generated work rather than people who produce it directly.

*Naval Ravikant with Guillermo Rauch, Blake Scholl, and Max Hodak.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=v6MWNrVbM4E) · 70 min · published 2026-06-01.

## Key lessons

- Guillermo Rauch reframes what makes a great engineer in the agent era: it's no longer about shipping output directly, it's about "producing the factory that would produce multiplicative outputs B through Z." Naval extends this to argue that the old, controversial claim about "10x engineers" was actually an understatement in idea-domain work — the real gap between people like Satoshi Nakamoto or John Carmack and everyone else is "100x or a thousandx."
- Blake Scholl's regulatory-documentation story shows the compounding value of AI beyond coding: a 200-page lightning-strike certification test plan that used to take "a couple months" to write, and another two months to redo after any design change, can now be generated by a RAG system "in minutes." The third-order effect, he argues, is the bigger one — cheap iteration lets a company keep only "a small number of really creative" engineers instead of "not super bright" ones just filling out paperwork.
- Naval floats a real healthcare fix using the same logic he applies to AI's economics: make the first 20% of a person's annual income their healthcare deductible, scaled to zero for the poor and uncapped for the wealthy, to finally create a private market with real price signals — the way LASIK and cosmetic dentistry improved once patients paid directly, unlike the "restaurant bills sent to your insurer at the end of the month" system he says defines the rest of American healthcare.
- Guillermo describes a company-wide experiment: he stopped all project work for a week and told everyone, including the receptionist, to build whatever they thought was most valuable using AI. He expected "a large number of silly projects and a small number of needle movers" and instead got the reverse — including a shipping-and-receiving associate who automated her own inventory-notification job, which the company kept using.
- The panel argues over what creativity even means once models can generate endless plausible output. Max Hodak defines art as "meaningful out-of-distribution behavior," while Naval insists real creativity requires conveying an emotion a human actually felt, and reaches for Gödel's incompleteness theorem as proof that humans can step "completely outside" a formal system — something he doesn't think a model trained only within that system's data can do.
- Max predicts the returns to human work will flip from "70% intelligence, 30% agency" to "70% agency, 30% intelligence" as models keep improving. Naval takes the opposite bet — "99% intelligence, 1% agency" — because once you tell an agent to just go implement something, the agent supplies its own agency; what a human still uniquely brings is the judgment to point it in the right direction, which is why he says "I always want the most intelligent programmer... the most correct answer... the best judgment."
