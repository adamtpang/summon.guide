# The Deutsch Files III

> **Naval's principle — Knowledge outlasts rock:** In the third installment, Naval and David Deutsch trace one idea through physics, biology, and parenting: explanatory knowledge is the most durable thing in the universe, more resilient than the buildings, DNA, or institutions that carry it, because it's the only thing capable of repairing and re-creating itself. From there they work through why creativity can't be proven or reduced to recombination, why copying a mind isn't the free abundance people imagine, and why the usual objections to treating children as full people don't actually hold up against a society that already fails at coercive parenting.

*Naval Ravikant in conversation with David Deutsch.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fizPWAAo-lc) · 59 min · published 2024-02-17.

## Key lessons

- Deutsch opens by knocking down a viral misquote — a journalist claimed "Popper proves AI can't be super intelligent" — to make a broader point: "proving something about AGI is inherently impossible" without a working theory of AGI, since proof only applies within math's necessary truths, while physics and philosophy run on argument and conjecture instead.
- Against Steve Jobs's "creativity is just mixing things together," Deutsch argues the word "just" is doing all the false work: comparing Manhattan Island a thousand years ago to today shows nothing in the recombined ideas of rock-banging ancestors contained a skyscraper, exactly as no pterodactyl's flight was "already in the DNA" of the flightless dinosaurs it evolved from — evolution and creativity both work by variation and selection, not remixing.
- Explanatory knowledge is uniquely resilient, Deutsch says, because unlike a Shakespeare sonnet — "so long lives this, and this gives life to thee" — or Shelley's Ozymandias, cities' buildings survive erosion only because they're "constantly repaired... by explanatory knowledge"; he adds that the real signature of alien intelligence SETI should look for isn't a biomarker but evidence of asteroid deflection, since nothing but creativity does that.
- Duplicating a mind isn't free abundance, Deutsch cautions against the sci-fi assumption of infinite AGI copies: every copy needs its own memory and processor cycles, someone has to own or buy the hardware, and the resulting political questions — like whether copies share one vote — are really just a scaled-up version of problems society already manages with children, immigrants, and foreign residents who also lack full voting rights.
- Naval raises two "sophisticated" objections to Taking Children Seriously — irreversible harm (drugs, pregnancy) and the brain-plasticity window for learning language or music — and Deutsch counters that today's coercive, obedience-first education already fails to stop those exact harms at scale, and that plasticity, even if real, doesn't change the fact that "learning has to be a creative act in the mind of the recipient," never something poured in from outside.
- Deutsch closes with two conjectures he admits are "fanciful": that a genuinely creative AI would need to write new Mozart rather than mimic old Mozart, since "his next great work would not be within the space" of pastiche, and that historians have never systematically studied "the history of optimism" in eras like Renaissance Florence or ancient Athens — tying back to his view that people should "give up on the idea of ultimate explanation," since believing you've found one closes off the further delight of understanding.
