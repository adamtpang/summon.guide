# How to Angel Invest, Part 1

> **Naval's principle — Access beats analysis:** This episode, repackaged from the early Spearhead podcast, argues that the scarce resource in angel investing isn't capital or even judgment — it's access to the small number of deals that will produce almost all of a portfolio's return. Naval's prescription is to build an authentic brand inside a real technology hub, stay close to your own trusted network rather than chasing unfamiliar deals brought by strangers, and accept that returns are so non-linear that missing the one or two best companies in a portfolio is functionally the same as losing the whole game.

*Naval Ravikant, on his own channel.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YLFHVye3Q7w) · 39 min · published 2019-11-17.

## Key lessons

- Wealth still requires equity ownership, but investing scales further into life than founding does — Naval calls angel investing something "you can be doing when you're 50, when you're 60, when you're 70 years old," unlike the physically grueling and age-limited grind of running a startup.
- Geography is close to destiny for angels: real deal flow concentrates in roughly a dozen hubs worldwide, and investing outside them means paying "Silicon Valley prices" for weaker access, which is why he says local angels in thin markets eventually "surrender" and route their money into Bay Area deals through platforms like AngelList.
- Returns are almost entirely non-linear rather than broad-based: "one company out of a hundred or one company out of a thousand accounts for all the returns," so getting cut out of a portfolio's top one or two winners — not picking bad companies — is the real risk angels face.
- Access, more than judgment or capital, is what separates good angels from bad ones, and access comes from having "an authentic reputation... that tells other people around the table, yes, let's let this person invest" — Naval traces his own brand back to early growth-hacking work and pitching Twitter's Evan Williams on a whiteboard before the round was even open to him.
- Brand has to come from real, pre-existing capability rather than marketing effort bolted on afterward — he points to Paul Graham's essays, Reid Hoffman's books, and Elad Gil's *High Growth Handbook* as authentic expressions of expertise that happened to also build a brand, warning that "you're not going to build a brand because you want to build a brand."
- Stay a generalist and stay close to your own network: narrow "vertical" brands like the clean-tech wave that burned an earlier generation of investors collapse when the underlying trend doesn't materialize on schedule, and being invited into a stranger's deal is itself a red flag, since it usually means they've already exhausted their own trusted contacts.
