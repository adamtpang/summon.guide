# Matt Ridley: How Innovation Works, Part 2

> **Naval's principle — Innovation as freedom, not threat:** Continuing their conversation, Naval and Matt Ridley argue that the twentieth century's biggest failures of nerve — on GMOs, on pandemic response, on energy — came from treating innovation as a danger to be contained rather than the only mechanism that has ever actually protected people and the planet. Ridley's closing line, that innovation is "the child of freedom," becomes the thread tying together crypto, evolution, and the panic over automation: progress happens when people are free to try things and freely choose what they want, not when it's centrally planned or blocked by precaution.

## Key lessons

- Ridley calls the present an "innovation famine, not an innovation feast" outside of digital technology, pointing to genetically modified insect-resistant crops as proof: they're proven safe and adopted across India, Brazil, and North America, but rejected in Europe and Africa by environmentalist pressure, even though CRISPR-based gene editing may let the world "leapfrog" the objection since it doesn't insert foreign DNA.
- The "spaceship Earth" metaphor is popular but wrong, Ridley argues: treating the planet as a fragile, zero-sum vessel ignores that 7 billion people can only live on it "through innovation, through technology, and through modifying the environment" — the real task is doing that sustainably, and eventually extending it to terraforming other planets.
- Naval brings in Leonard Read's essay "I, Pencil" to make the case that even a simple object embeds knowledge "beyond the capability of one human mind to comprehend," and that humans are the only species that cooperates across genetic boundaries at scale — which is also why top-down models of complex systems fail, epitomized by his quip "who is London's lunch commissioner?"
- Crypto, Ridley and Naval agree, solves the Byzantine Generals problem without a state, and cryptography specifically hands the defender a rare structural advantage — "probably since the castle wall or the moat" — unlike the cannon, the machine gun's descendants, nuclear weapons, or airstrikes, all of which favor the attacker.
- Ridley dismisses the recurring panic that this round of automation (framed then as AGI) will finally destroy jobs for good, noting the exact fear has recurred every 50 years and always proved wrong; society instead becomes "more and more specialized in what we produce, but more and more diversified in what we consume," a line he traces back through his own reading to Naval, David Deutsch, or possibly Nassim Taleb — nobody's sure whose idea it originally was.
- On the "great man" theory of history, Ridley reminds Naval that Edison had 21 rival inventors racing him to the lightbulb, and that Facebook had MySpace and Friendster before it, and Airbnb had VRBO, HomeAway, and Couchsurfing before it — a point Naval concedes gave him "a mild case of Gell-Mann amnesia," since he'd cited those same companies elsewhere as singular innovators.
