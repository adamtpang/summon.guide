# Let Us Not Talk Falsely Now

> **Naval's principle — Design your own game:** Recorded as an open Twitter Spaces AMA with physicist-podcaster Brett Hall sitting in and dozens of listeners lining up to ask questions, this episode has Naval riffing live rather than delivering a prepared essay. The throughline is agency: web3 as the business model that finally lets open source get paid, life as a single-player game you get to interpret and design rather than one dictated to you, and clear, unclutted thinking as the underrated force multiplier behind every other decision — money, relationships, and how much of your own time you actually control.

*Naval Ravikant, live with Brett Hall, hosting a Twitter Spaces AMA.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=euzoOkBUzsQ) · 88 min · published 2022-04-26.

## Key lessons

- Web3 is, in Naval's framing (borrowing from Chris Dixon), "the business model for open source" — code stays open, but now data is owned by users and the network is owned by its contributors, which is what actually separates it from "web 2 plus a number."
- Life is a single-player game: so much of what looks like reality is your own interpretation, and since "you're gone in three generations and nobody cares," the absence of a built-in meaning is framed as freedom rather than nihilism — "inside every person is either a budding god or a budding demon," and you get to choose which one you interpret yourself as.
- The single-player frame is explicitly a cure for victimhood — Naval distinguishes real victims from the much larger group of "budding victims... raising their hand crying for victimhood" on social media, arguing that even real adversity is better played by rising past it, since that's "where character is built."
- Clarity of thought is a "cheat code" precisely because Naval estimates 99% of daily effort is inconsequential — a cluttered calendar buries the 1% of decisions that actually matter, so he keeps his own calendar deliberately empty and treats a messy desk or an 11-page rambling email as a visible signal of a messy mind.
- Naval describes ruthlessly stripping obligations out of his life in stages — first roommates, then commuting, then a scheduled calendar, and now trying to eliminate email entirely, checking it once every three days — and is blunt that this requires disappointing people constantly: "if you're not disappointing them... you're not really changing."
- True contrarianism isn't reflexively taking the opposite view — it's finding a belief nearly everyone holds that's actually false, like the idea that natural resources are finite; Naval's uranium example (a useless rock, pitchblende, until nuclear physics existed) is his case that resources are a function of knowledge, not scarcity, and that pessimism persists because "it's just easier to be a pessimist than an optimist."
