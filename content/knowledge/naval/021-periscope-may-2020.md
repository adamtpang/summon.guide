# Periscope - May 2020

> **Naval's principle — Think for yourself:** Recorded live and unscripted on Periscope during lockdown, Naval fields whatever the audience throws at him — COVID, envy, meditation, Twitter, wealth, grief, purpose — with no agenda beyond "I don't care about anything so I'm all good." Across dozens of unrelated questions the same move keeps recurring: strip an idea down until it's just you, alone, deciding for yourself what's true, since every prescription, guru, or borrowed answer is a way of outsourcing a thought you were supposed to have yourself.

*Naval Ravikant, on his own channel.* [Watch](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fdFxfOJW0gg) · 74 min · published 2021-02-26.

## Key lessons

- Complete freedom, Naval says, is a specific recipe he actually followed: make enough money that you stop thinking about it, keep your expenses low so that number is smaller, and then be willing to break social obligations and "disappoint people," since "if you're not willing to lose a bunch of friends and connections" you'll never actually be free to leave whenever you want.
- He describes conquering envy after "35 years" of it by realizing envy only makes sense if you'd take someone's entire life wholesale — their body, parents, and internal state included — not just the parts you'd like to cherry-pick; once he stopped envying people, admiration followed the same logic, becoming something closer to appreciating "a flower" than wanting to become one.
- He says he hasn't been lonely since committing seriously to yoga and meditation, because time alone became the best part of his day, making socializing "a net negative" compared to sitting at home — a distinct claim from boredom, which he says he still gets "a lot," which is partly why he's doing the Periscope in the first place.
- His meditation method is explicitly a non-method: sit for an hour a day, eyes closed, dark room, no app, "surrender to whatever happens," 60 days straight, with no goal to chase, because seeking a specific benefit "is going to ruin your meditation"; he separately warns against microdosing and nootropics as regularly "hacking your mind" in a way that can quietly break the very "meta programmer" you'd need to notice you're changing for the worse.
- Real persuasion, he argues, is a byproduct of living congruently rather than technique — he cites Robert Cialdini's six or seven persuasion levers but says people crave "authenticity" above all of them, using Jesus and Buddha as extreme cases: one persuasive because he "demonstrated his authenticity by dying for a bunch of random ungrateful people," the other because he genuinely "didn't want anything."
- Asked how to become wise, Naval collapses it to three words — "think for yourself" — and immediately flags the trap: repeating that advice from him is already failing to follow it; he pairs this with a closing technique for finding your baseline state, sitting and doing absolutely nothing until "whatever mood you end up with... is your default mood," since everything else in life is just "a covering or an escape from that mood."
