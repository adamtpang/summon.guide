# The Deutsch Files II

> **Naval's principle — Creation out of nothing:** Naval walks David Deutsch through the four strands of *The Fabric of Reality* — computation, epistemology, evolution, quantum physics — and pushes him to show how they interlock. The idea that anchors the whole conversation is that new explanatory knowledge, like Einstein's field equations, is genuinely created rather than assembled from what already existed; that act of creation is what free will actually is, and it's also the mechanism behind everything Deutsch calls wealth, optimism, and the growth of knowledge itself.

## Key lessons

- Turing's discovery of universal computation is a claim about physics, not mathematics, according to Deutsch — a computer can mimic any physical process, which is why "aliens with better computers than us" is close to incoherent: alien machines could be faster or hold more memory, but they can't compute anything a universal computer fundamentally can't, unless the laws of physics themselves turn out to be wrong.
- Deutsch reframes Popper's central concept: a "problem" is a clash between ideas that can't both be true — and crucially, both could turn out to be false — which is why confronting rival ideas in an argument is valuable "even if they are never resolved," since each side leaves having quietly refined its own view in response to the clash.
- Evolution is not survival of the fittest, it's differential replication of gene variants, and Deutsch points to a concrete tell: simulated ecosystems and evolved robot legs always improve and then plateau, while real biological evolution never stops branching, because real organisms keep encountering genuinely new problems instead of being handed one externally imposed goal like "learn to walk."
- Free will, in Deutsch's account, is the "quintessential act" of creating something that had never existed anywhere before — not in Einstein's brain, not implicit in the Big Bang — the moment he solved the specific problem of reconciling special relativity with gravity, which is why the act was unpredictable without being random.
- Wealth, Deutsch argues, "can't be quantified as a number" — it's the set of transformations a person or civilization could bring about, which is why he says you can't actually rank Mozart against the banker Nathan Rothschild on one scale: both created knowledge from nothing, in domains too different to overlap on a single measure.
- Asked what a person should do with all this, Deutsch points to Faraday, who invented electromagnetic induction not because he set out to save civilization but because he was "following the fun" of a problem that interested him personally — advice Deutsch extends to his own younger self as the safest way to end up doing something that matters.
