# Matt Ridley: How Innovation Works, Part 1

> **Naval's principle — Innovation is bred, not born:** Interviewing science writer Matt Ridley about his book *How Innovation Works*, Naval describes it as correcting a "serious error" he'd carried since arriving in Silicon Valley in 1996: the belief that lone geniuses invent breakthrough technologies. Ridley's actual argument is that innovation is a gradual, evolutionary process — closer to natural selection than to a eureka moment — that depends on a dense ecosystem of collaborators, trial and error, and above all freedom, which is why he calls it "the child of freedom and the parent of prosperity."

## Key lessons

- Ridley draws a sharp line between invention and innovation: invention is the prototype, innovation is "the business of turning a new device into something practical, affordable, and reliable" — Thomas Edison's own "1% inspiration, 99% perspiration" line describes himself as an innovator more than an inventor. Ridley illustrates the mismatch in credit with his rabbit-and-beaver fable at the Hoover Dam: the beaver says "I didn't build it, but it's based on an idea of mine," which is how he says inventors typically talk about the far larger effort of innovation.
- The green revolution's Nobel-credited "lone genius," Norman Borlaug, actually inherited his dwarf-wheat breakthrough through a documented relay: he heard about it from agronomist Burton Bayles at a bar in Buenos Aires, who'd seen Orville Vogel growing varieties in Oregon, which Vogel got from Cecil Salmon (who'd found them at Japanese agricultural stations while on Douglas MacArthur's staff), which trace back to breeder Gonjiro Inazuka's Korean-derived stock — a chain of incremental borrowing, not a single flash of insight.
- Naval credits the book with fixing his own framework: he'd assumed Silicon Valley was living through a diminished, teams-based era compared to a mythical golden age of solo inventors, and only realized after reading Ridley that collaborative, incremental innovation was always how it worked — it just looks different from inside the process than it does in retrospective history books.
- Geographic clustering isn't random, according to Ridley — it reflects genuine ecosystem dependency. His example is the Haber process for fixing nitrogen from air, which "couldn't have happened without all the other industries around it in Germany" producing the specific high-quality metals and chemicals it required, the same logic Naval applies to why Silicon Valley concentrates innovators, capital, and early-adopter customers in one place rather than spreading them globally.
- Ridley traces a repeating historical pattern he calls "chiefs, priests, and thieves" for how innovation hubs get shut down: Ming China's turn toward authoritarian restriction, the Abbasid world's religious-fundamentalist revival, and Bernard of Clairvaux burning books in Paris. He and Naval debate whether remote work and crypto's geographically distributed, sometimes-anonymous builders (Naval cites Satoshi Nakamoto and points out that "more than half" of his crypto investments are outside the Bay Area) could let future innovation escape into the cloud before local politics catches up to it.
- Both agree regulation punishes innovation asymmetrically: a regulator who approves something that later fails gets blamed, while one who blocks something valuable faces no visible cost, which Ridley argues explains why physical-world speed and infrastructure innovation has stagnated even as digital and crypto innovation — which can partially "escape into the cloud" — keeps accelerating.
