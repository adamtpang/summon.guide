# Vitalik: Ethereum, Part 1

> **Naval's principle — The trust machine:** In this three-way conversation with Ethereum creator Vitalik Buterin and Dragonfly Capital's Haseeb Qureshi, Naval frames Ethereum as a giant, deliberately inefficient shared computer — tens of thousands of machines each re-running the same simple logic so that no single company or government has to be trusted. The core argument is that this redundancy is the entire point: Ethereum sacrifices speed and throughput for a form of decentralization strong enough that ordinary users, not just powerful validators, can independently verify the chain, which is what makes it credible for high-value applications.

## Key lessons

- Vitalik distinguishes Ethereum from Bitcoin by calling it a "general-purpose blockchain" rather than money-only: publishing a transaction can create a smart contract object with its own code and memory, illustrated with a toy example of transferring company shares between Naval, Haseeb, and Vitalik on-chain.
- The core trade-off is efficiency for trust — every transaction gets verified redundantly by the whole network rather than by one company's server, which Vitalik compares to how a live video call needs far more bandwidth than the same conversation typed as text: "the things that happen in the blockchain are more similar to text."
- Sharding and rollups exist to fix that redundancy without losing security: instead of every node verifying every transaction, techniques let random subsets verify pieces of it, a pattern Vitalik compares to BitTorrent, where popular files get "a few hundred seeders" rather than every peer downloading everything.
- Decentralization is framed as a cultural commitment as much as a technical one — Ethereum insists a node should run on ordinary laptop hardware specifically so regular users keep verifying it themselves, because once "running a node is so easy that people are comfortable doing it by default," no small cartel of powerful validators can quietly push through a rule change.
- Zero-knowledge proofs let a chain keep both transparency and privacy at once — Vitalik describes proving a transaction is valid ("the numbers line up") without revealing the actual balances involved, pointing to Zcash as a live example of "moon math" already in production.
- Haseeb grounds the trade-off in his own benchmark data: Ethereum handles roughly 10 Uniswap-style trades per second against Solana's roughly 280, but argues that gap is largely the price of Ethereum's much higher decentralization — and that high-value applications will keep paying for it the way "Wall Street" pays for property rights and rule of law.
