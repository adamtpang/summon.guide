# Berkshire Hathaway Shareholder Letters, 1977-2024

> **Owner principle:** Treat every important choice as capital allocation. Understand the underlying economics, compare the choice with its best alternative, protect against permanent loss, work with trustworthy people, and give sound systems enough time to compound.

## Core lessons

- **Think like an owner.** Evaluate a marketable security as part ownership of a business: understand what it does, assess its long-term prospects, judge the people in charge, and compare price with conservative value. A quotation is an offer, not a verdict.
- **Use owner earnings.** Reported earnings are a starting point. Add back non-cash charges, then subtract the capital expenditure and working capital required to maintain volume and competitive position. The maintenance estimate will be imperfect, but it is closer to economic reality.
- **Apply the retained-earnings test.** A dollar retained belongs to the owners. Management should retain it only when there is a reasonable prospect that it will create at least a dollar of additional long-term value.
- **Stay within a bounded circle of competence.** You do not need to understand every opportunity. You need to know which few you can evaluate and, more importantly, where your understanding ends.
- **Prefer quality that can compound.** Time helps a wonderful business and exposes a mediocre one. Durable pricing power, low incremental capital requirements, loyal customers, and able managers can make patience productive.
- **Measure the moat.** Look for a durable cost advantage, trusted brand, network, switching cost, habit, or other defense around the economic castle. Then ask whether the moat is widening or shrinking.
- **Design against the institutional imperative.** Organizations naturally resist change, invent projects to consume available funds, rationalize leaders' wishes, and imitate peers. Compensation, process, and governance should counter those tendencies.
- **Keep a financial fortress.** Avoid obligations that can demand cash at the worst time. Hold ample liquidity, use debt conservatively, stress-test counterparties, and never depend on markets remaining open or friendly.
- **Choose people for the long term.** Ability matters, but integrity and owner orientation determine whether ability compounds for or against you. Give exceptional managers autonomy and avoid incentives that reward activity for its own sake.
- **Correct mistakes promptly.** Errors are inevitable; hiding, defending, or delaying them is optional. Diagnose the changed fact, stop the loss of time and attention, and preserve trust by explaining the correction plainly.

## Buffett decision sequence

1. Define what is understood and name what is not.
2. Translate the proposal into owner economics and cash requirements.
3. Identify the source and durability of the moat.
4. Audit management's integrity, ability, incentives, and record.
5. Compare the proposal with the single best available alternative, including cash and inaction.
6. Build a margin of safety around valuation, financing, and assumptions.
7. Reject any path that can cause permanent loss, forced action, or reputational damage.
8. If the case remains exceptional, act with enough conviction for the decision to matter.
9. Monitor the underlying economics, not daily quotations, and correct mistakes without delay.

## Munger's Berkshire system

Munger's first-person fiftieth-anniversary essay in the 2014 letter explains why Berkshire's structure endured. Operating subsidiaries remain highly autonomous; headquarters stays tiny; capital allocation and chief-executive selection remain centralized; able and trustworthy managers receive unusual freedom; good businesses are rarely sold; acquisitions are usually made without issuing stock; debt is used sparingly; and accumulated reputation attracts more owners and managers who value permanence. The result is a reinforcing system in which trust reduces bureaucracy and reduced bureaucracy attracts people worthy of trust.

Munger's broader method strengthens the system: invert to expose failure, remove ruin before seeking gain, follow incentives, test conclusions through several disciplines, compare opportunity costs, and wait for the few situations where understanding and advantage are both strong.
