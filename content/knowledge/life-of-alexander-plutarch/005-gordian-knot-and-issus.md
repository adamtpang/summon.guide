# The Gordian Knot and the Battle of Issus

> **Key principle:** Between the Granicus and Issus, Plutarch stacks up omens, a legendary knot, and a poisoning scare, all to make the same point: Alexander reads signs and takes risks the way a man who already expects to win does, and that expectation becomes self-fulfilling at Issus.

*Synthesized from Plutarch's Life of Alexander, translated by John Dryden, revised by Arthur Hugh Clough.*

## Key lessons

- After the Granicus, Sardis and most of the Anatolian coast surrendered without a fight; only Halicarnassus and Miletus had to be taken by force, and a spring at Xanthus conveniently overflowed and turned up an ancient inscription prophesying that Greeks would destroy the Persian empire, timed to buoy Alexander's confidence right when he was undecided about strategy.
- The march along the Pamphylian coast became legendary for the sea supposedly retreating to let his army pass, celebrated by the poet Menander as divine favor; Plutarch pointedly notes that Alexander's own letters describe nothing miraculous, just an ordinary route through a pass called the Ladders, a rare case of Plutarch flagging the gap between myth and the man's own account.
- At Gordium, tradition held that whoever untied the knot binding Midas's ancient chariot would rule the world. Plutarch gives two competing versions of what happened: most sources say Alexander simply cut the knot with his sword, while Aristobulus claims he solved it more cleverly, by pulling out the pin that held the yoke to the pole.
- Darius marched out from Susa overconfident, buoyed by a dream in which he saw the Macedonian army in flames and Alexander serving him in the humble dress of a royal courier before vanishing, which his flattering soothsayers read as favorable; Darius also mistook Alexander's delay in Cilicia for cowardice rather than illness.
- That illness nearly killed Alexander after he bathed in the icy river Cydnus. His physician Philip prepared a drastic remedy just as Parmenio's letter arrived warning that Philip had been bribed by Darius to poison him; Alexander drank the medicine without hesitation while handing Philip the letter to read, a deliberate display of trust that briefly left him near-unconscious before he recovered.
- A Macedonian defector named Amyntas, who knew Alexander's character, warned Darius to hold his ground on the open plains where his numbers would matter, and predicted Alexander would come looking for a fight rather than run. Darius ignored the advice and repositioned into Cilicia at the very moment Alexander advanced the other way into Syria, so the two armies passed each other in the dark without meeting.
- Realizing his mistake too late, Darius found himself boxed into cramped ground between the sea, mountains, and the river Pinarus, terrain that neutralized his cavalry advantage; Alexander, though outnumbered, extended his right wing past the enemy's left and personally led the charge that broke the Persian line.
- Alexander was wounded in the thigh during the fighting; Chares says Darius himself struck the blow in hand-to-hand combat, but Alexander's own letter to Antipater mentions only the sword wound, not who dealt it. The Persians lost over a hundred thousand men, and only Darius's own flight kept the victory from being total.
