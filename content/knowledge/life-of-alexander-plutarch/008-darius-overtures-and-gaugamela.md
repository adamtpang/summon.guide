# Darius's Overtures and the Battle of Gaugamela

> **Key principle:** Between Darius's peace offer and the battle that ends the Persian empire, Plutarch shows Alexander's confidence hardening into something almost serene, he turns down a fortune, sleeps through the morning of his biggest fight, and refuses a night attack on principle.

*Synthesized from Plutarch's Life of Alexander, translated by John Dryden, revised by Arthur Hugh Clough.*

## Key lessons

- Darius offered a thousand talents in ransom for his captured family, all Persian territory west of the Euphrates, and one of his daughters in marriage. When Parmenio said he would accept if he were Alexander, Alexander answered, "So would I, if I were Parmenio," and instead demanded Darius surrender himself entirely.
- Soon after sending that refusal, Darius's wife Statira died in childbirth, and Alexander visibly grieved losing the chance to extend her more kindness, giving her an extravagant funeral. The eunuch Tireus, who escaped to report her death, told Darius that Alexander had wept for her too and had shown her every honor in life and death.
- Darius, tormented by suspicion that Alexander's gentleness toward his wife implied some impropriety, pressed Tireus under oath to tell him the truth; Tireus insisted Alexander's restraint was genuine virtue, not disgrace, and Darius broke down and prayed to his gods that if Persia must fall, no one but Alexander should inherit Cyrus's throne.
- On the march toward the final confrontation, camp servants split into two mock armies naming their leaders "Alexander" and "Darius" for sport; the game escalated from thrown clods of earth to real fighting with stones and clubs, so Alexander had the two camp "generals" settle it by formal single combat, arming his own namesake himself, and rewarded the winner with twelve villages and the right to wear Persian dress.
- The true decisive battle was fought at Gaugamela, not Arbela as commonly said. A lunar eclipse fell eleven nights before the armies met; while Darius kept his men in arms all night under torchlight, Alexander spent the night performing rites with his diviner Aristander.
- When his senior commanders, alarmed by the vast Persian campfires covering the plain, urged a night attack to hide their numerical disadvantage, Alexander refused with the line "I will not steal a victory," reasoning that a night win would let Darius blame darkness rather than admit outright defeat.
- Alexander then slept unusually soundly, so long that Parmenio had to wake him and ask how he could rest before the most important battle of his life; Alexander answered that he already felt as good as victorious, since he was finally free of chasing Darius across a wasted country.
- In the battle itself Parmenio's wing nearly broke under a Bactrian cavalry charge and Mazaeus's raid on the baggage train, but Alexander refused to divert reinforcements, telling Parmenio that victorious soldiers inherit their enemies' goods while defeated ones have nothing left to protect anyway; Darius's chariot became stuck among piled corpses and he fled on a mare taken from her foal, and Alexander was proclaimed King of Asia.
