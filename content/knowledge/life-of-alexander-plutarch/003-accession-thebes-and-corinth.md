# Accession: Thebes Destroyed, Athens Spared

> **Key principle:** Rather than buying time with concessions to restless subject peoples, the twenty-year-old Alexander answers instability with immediate, overwhelming action, a lightning campaign to the Danube followed by the annihilation of rebellious Thebes, and then reverses course completely toward Athens, showing mercy as a deliberate second instrument rather than weakness.

*Synthesized from Plutarch's Life of Alexander, translated by John Dryden, revised by Arthur Hugh Clough.*

## Key lessons

- Alexander became king at twenty over a kingdom "beset on all sides" by hostile neighboring tribes and unsettled Greek city-states still resentful of Philip's incomplete conquest; advisers urged him to court the restless Greeks with patience and concession.
- He rejected that advice as weak, judging that any appearance of yielding would only invite more rebellion, and instead launched a rapid campaign north to the Danube, where he decisively defeated Syrmus and the Triballians.
- Hearing that Thebes had revolted with Athenian encouragement, he marched south through Thermopylae so fast that he made good on his boast to Demosthenes, who had mocked him as a "child" and a "youth," that he would appear a grown man at the walls of Athens.
- Alexander first offered Thebes a lenient settlement, demanding only the two ringleaders of the revolt and a general pardon for everyone else; the Thebans refused and counter-demanded two of his own officers, so he destroyed the city, killing roughly six thousand and selling some thirty thousand survivors into slavery, sparing only priests, known Macedonian sympathizers, and the poet Pindar's family.
- Amid the sack, a Theban matron named Timoclea, after being assaulted by a Thracian captain hunting for hidden treasure, lured him to a well under pretense of showing him her valuables and pushed him in, killing him with stones; brought before Alexander and asked who she was, she identified herself as the sister of Theagenes, who died fighting Philip at Chaeronea, and Alexander freed her and her children.
- Alexander then reversed his severity toward Athens, forgiving the city entirely despite its support for Thebes, and Plutarch notes Alexander later regretted the destruction of Thebes and blamed misfortunes like Clitus's murder on the wrath of Bacchus, patron god of Thebes.
- At the Isthmus of Corinth the assembled Greek states named Alexander general for the coming war against Persia; there the philosopher Diogenes, sunning himself and unimpressed by the visiting king, answered Alexander's offer of any favor by asking him only to "stand from between me and the sun," prompting Alexander to remark that if he were not Alexander, he would wish to be Diogenes.
- At Delphi, arriving on a day when the oracle was forbidden to speak, Alexander physically pressed the priestess toward the temple until, exasperated, she blurted "thou art invincible," a Delphic pronouncement he immediately declared sufficient and needing no further consultation.
