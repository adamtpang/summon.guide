# Crossing to Asia and the Battle of Granicus

> **Key principle:** Before he has won anything in Asia, Alexander strips his own royal estate to fund and reward his friends, telling Perdiccas that his hopes are all he has kept for himself; at the Granicus he then puts his life on the line in the same way, fording under fire and surviving only because Clitus intervenes at the critical instant.

*Synthesized from Plutarch's Life of Alexander, translated by John Dryden, revised by Arthur Hugh Clough.*

## Key lessons

- Alexander's invasion force was strikingly small and underfunded by different ancient accounts, ranging from thirty to forty-three thousand infantry and a few thousand cavalry, with as little as seventy talents in the war chest, barely a month's provisions, and by one account two hundred talents already in debt.
- Before embarking, Alexander gave away almost the whole of his royal property to his friends, farms, villages, and revenues from towns and harbors; when Perdiccas asked what he had kept for himself, Alexander answered "his hopes," and Perdiccas refused his own share, saying the soldiers would share in that hope with him.
- Crossing the Hellespont, Alexander went to Troy to sacrifice to Minerva and honor the tombs of the Homeric heroes, anointing Achilles's gravestone and running a ceremonial naked circuit around it with his companions, declaring Achilles fortunate to have had both a devoted friend in life and a great poet to celebrate him after death; shown Paris's lyre, he said he cared nothing for it but wished to see Achilles's instead.
- Darius's forces met him at the river Granicus, and despite advisers like Parmenio urging caution about the terrain, the season, and the lateness of the day, Alexander insisted on attacking immediately, saying he would disgrace the Hellespont if he now feared the Granicus.
- He personally led thirteen troops of horse across the river under a hail of missiles and up a steep, slippery bank into disordered hand-to-hand combat, recognizable to the enemy by his white-plumed helmet, his cuirass pierced by a javelin in the fighting.
- Two Persian commanders, Rhoesaces and Spithridates, attacked him simultaneously; Alexander broke his spear killing Rhoesaces with his dagger, while Spithridates's battle-axe blow sheared off part of his helmet and plume before Clitus ran Spithridates through with a spear, saving Alexander's life.
- The Macedonian phalanx crossed and routed the Persian line; Alexander then refused quarter to a body of Greek mercenaries who tried to surrender, and his insistence on destroying them cost more Macedonian lives than the rest of the battle combined, including his own horse (not Bucephalas) killed under him.
- Alexander sent captured armor back to Greece as offerings, three hundred suits to the Athenians in particular, inscribed as won by "Alexander the son of Philip, and the Grecians, except the Lacedaemonians," and sent the finest plate and purple robes taken from the Persians home as a gift to his mother.
