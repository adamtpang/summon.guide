# Birth, Boyhood, and the Taming of Bucephalus

> **Key principle:** Plutarch opens by explaining his method: he is not attempting to rival full histories of Alexander and Caesar but to capture the "marks and indications of the soul," which often show up in a small saying or gesture rather than in a siege or a battle line. Everything that follows in this section, the omens at his birth, his self-possession with foreign ambassadors, his mastery of a horse no one else could ride, is offered as exactly that kind of evidence.

*Synthesized from Plutarch's Life of Alexander, translated by John Dryden, revised by Arthur Hugh Clough.*

## Key lessons

- Plutarch states his purpose up front: he is writing lives, not histories, and will favor a revealing "expression or a jest" over an exhaustive account of great armaments and battles, the same way a portrait painter lingers on the face rather than the whole body.
- Alexander's birth is wrapped in omens: Olympias dreamed of a thunderbolt striking her body before conception, Philip dreamed of sealing her womb with the image of a lion, and diviners read both as signs of a lion-hearted son; a serpent later found sleeping beside Olympias reportedly cooled Philip's affection for her, amid rumors she practiced the ecstatic serpent-rites of Orphic and Bacchic worship.
- Alexander was born the same day the temple of Diana at Ephesus burned down, and Philip received news of his son's birth in the same hour as word of a victory over the Illyrians and of his own horse winning at the Olympic games, three simultaneous successes diviners took to mean the child would be invincible.
- As a boy, Alexander showed little interest in athletic contests for their own sake, saying he would only race if he could compete against kings, and he favored music, poetry, and hunting over boxing or the pancratium.
- Left to entertain visiting Persian ambassadors in his father's absence, the young Alexander impressed them not with childish talk but with pointed, substantive questions about travel distances, the character of the Persian king, and the size of his forces.
- Alexander tamed the horse Bucephalas, which no one else could mount, by noticing it feared its own shadow and turning it to face the sun before calmly mounting it; Philip, watching him ride back in triumph, wept and told him "Macedonia is too little for thee," urging him to seek a kingdom worthy of himself.
- Philip brought in Aristotle to tutor Alexander, rewarding him by rebuilding and repopulating Aristotle's demolished home city of Stagira; Alexander later wrote to Aristotle protesting the publication of his esoteric doctrines, saying he would rather excel others in knowledge than in power.
- Alexander's love of learning carried into adulthood: he kept Aristotle's annotated copy of Homer's Iliad under his pillow with his dagger, calling it a portable treasure of military knowledge, and he practiced enough medicine, learned partly from Aristotle, to prescribe remedies for sick friends.
