# The Conspiracies: Philotas, Parmenio, and Clitus

> **Key principle:** Two of the darkest episodes of Alexander's reign, the executions of Philotas and Parmenio and the killing of Clitus, both grew from the same root: absolute power making Alexander quicker to suspect betrayal and quicker to violence, whether calculated or drunken, against the men closest to him.

*Synthesized from Plutarch's Life of Alexander, translated by John Dryden, revised by Arthur Hugh Clough.*

## Key lessons

- Philotas, son of Parmenio, was admired for valor and generosity but also arrogant and showy with his wealth; his own father warned him it would be better to be "not quite so great."
- His mistress Antigone reported that, drunk, Philotas had boasted he and his father deserved credit for Alexander's victories while "the boy Alexander" merely reaped the benefit; Alexander, informed of this, had her continue reporting his pillow talk rather than act immediately.
- When the soldier Limnus plotted against Alexander's life and the plot was reported through intermediaries, Philotas twice failed to pass the warning to Alexander, claiming the king was too busy; Limnus was killed resisting arrest before he could be questioned, and Philotas's negligence, combined with his earlier boasts, became the pretext for accusing him of complicity.
- Philotas was tortured for a confession while Alexander listened from behind a tapestry, reportedly remarking with disgust at how abjectly Philotas pleaded with Hephaestion; after his execution, Alexander also had his father Parmenio killed in Media, despite Parmenio being the very counselor who had originally urged the invasion of Asia and having already lost two other sons in the campaign.
- The killing of Clitus began at a drunken supper: mocking verses were sung about Macedonian officers recently defeated, and Clitus, already drunk and hot-tempered, objected that Macedonians deserved better than ridicule in front of barbarians.
- Clitus escalated into taunting Alexander directly, reminding him that it was Macedonian blood, and Clitus's own sword arm at the Granicus, that had saved his life and built his power, not any divine parentage from Ammon; enraged, Alexander hurled an apple at him, called for his sword, and when Clitus returned through another door still defiant, ran him through with a spear.
- Alexander's grief was immediate and extreme, he tried to turn the spear on himself and had to be physically restrained, then lay for a day and night refusing food or speech until the seer Aristander reminded him of a prophetic dream that had foretold Clitus's death as fated.
- The philosopher Anaxarchus, unlike the gentler Callisthenes, ended Alexander's mourning with flattery rather than comfort, telling him that as supreme conqueror his actions were automatically just, like Jupiter's, a line of reasoning Plutarch says calmed the king but corrupted his character.
