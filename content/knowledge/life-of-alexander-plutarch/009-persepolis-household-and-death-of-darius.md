# Persepolis, the King's Household, and the Death of Darius

> **Key principle:** Wealth and power arrived faster than Alexander's judgment could metabolize them. He staged a burning of Xerxes's palace on a drunken whim he came to regret, while in the same season proving himself almost recklessly generous with money and status, and Olympias had to warn him he was making everyone else a king while leaving nothing for himself.

*Synthesized from Plutarch's Life of Alexander, translated by John Dryden, revised by Arthur Hugh Clough.*

## Key lessons

- Marching through Babylon toward Ecbatana, Alexander was struck by natural jets of flammable naphtha; to demonstrate its power, an attendant named Athenophanes doused the boy singer Stephanus with it as a "test," and Stephanus caught fire so completely that bystanders barely saved his life with bath water.
- At Susa, Alexander seized forty thousand talents in coined money plus vast stores of goods, including purple cloth dyed nearly two centuries earlier that had kept its color; Persia yielded even more, carried off on ten thousand mules and five thousand camels.
- Coming upon a toppled statue of Xerxes at Persepolis, Alexander addressed it directly, weighing aloud whether to leave it fallen for invading Greece or re-erect it for its former greatness, and after a long pause walked on without deciding either way.
- At a drinking party before departing Persepolis, the courtesan Thais, mistress to Ptolemy, urged Alexander to let her torch Xerxes's palace as revenge for the burning of Athens; he joined the procession himself with a torch, and accounts differ on whether it was impulse or plan, but all agree he soon regretted it and ordered the fire put out.
- His generosity became legendary in specific small scenes: turning a Paeonian soldier's joking claim to a gold cup (for a slain enemy's head) into a literal gift of wine in a golden cup, telling an exhausted mule-driver to keep the royal treasure he was carrying, and handing Proteas five talents as proof of restored friendship; Mazaeus's son modestly declined an extra province, saying Alexander was making "many Alexanders" instead of just one Darius.
- Olympias wrote to warn him he was elevating his friends to the rank of kings while leaving himself with nothing, and he kept her letters private except once sealing one with his ring pressed to Hephaestion's lips after letting him read it.
- When his favorites grew soft with luxury, wearing silver nail-studded shoes or importing camel-loads of powder for wrestling, Alexander rebuked them gently but firmly, arguing that comfort and dependency on servants were the marks of the very slavishness they had conquered, then set an example by resuming vigorous hunting and hardship himself.
- Pursuing the fleeing Darius through eleven days covering over three thousand furlongs, Alexander refused a helmet of water offered by his thirsty soldiers rather than drink while his men could not, and they found Darius near death in his chariot, betrayed and left by Bessus; Darius gave his hand in gratitude to Alexander through the soldier Polystratus and died, after which Alexander covered the body with his own cloak and later had Bessus torn apart between two bent trees.
