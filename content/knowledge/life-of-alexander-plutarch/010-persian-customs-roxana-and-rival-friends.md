# Persian Customs, Roxana, and Rival Friends

> **Key principle:** Alexander's adoption of Persian ways, his marriage to Roxana, and his careful balancing of two very different favorites all served one calculation: that goodwill and shared identity would hold a vast, mixed empire together better than force alone, even as it alarmed the Macedonians who had followed him from home.

*Synthesized from Plutarch's Life of Alexander, translated by John Dryden, revised by Arthur Hugh Clough.*

## Key lessons

- After Bucephalas was stolen by tribesmen in Hyrcania, Alexander threatened to kill an entire population, men, women, and children; they surrendered both the horse and their cities, and he rewarded them with kindness and even paid a ransom for his own horse.
- In Parthia he adopted a hybrid court dress, a middle path between Persian and Macedonian styles that avoided trousers, sleeves, and the tiara but still struck the Macedonians as troubling; he wore it privately at first, then openly, tolerating their visible grief because they still respected his other virtues.
- He kept exposing himself to real danger during this period, taking an arrow wound to the leg that shattered bone and a stone blow to the neck that dimmed his sight, and pressed on through a diarrhea-plagued pursuit of Scythians rather than slow down.
- The famous story of a visiting Amazon queen is reported by several sources but denied by others closer to Alexander, and Alexander's own letter to Antipater about this period mentions only a Scythian king offering his daughter in marriage, no Amazon at all; decades later, king Lysimachus reportedly laughed at the tale and asked where he himself had been at the time.
- Before pushing further into Asia, Alexander told his war-weary troops that any of the roughly twenty thousand foot and three thousand horse still with him were free to leave, framing it as merely wanting his men to see the truth: that alarming Asia was not the same as conquering it; all of them chose to stay.
- He began training thirty thousand native boys in Greek language and Macedonian military discipline, betting on cultural blending over compulsion as the way to hold the conquered territories once his army moved on.
- His marriage to Roxana began as genuine attraction, seeing her dance at a banquet, but also served his political goal of winning over the conquered peoples; notably, the famously self-controlled Alexander restrained himself until he could marry her properly rather than simply take her.
- He deliberately favored two very different men for different roles: Hephaestion, who embraced Persian customs, handled dealings with the Persians and was called "Alexander's friend," while Craterus, who held to Macedonian tradition, handled dealings with Greeks and Macedonians and was called "the king's friend"; when the two nearly came to blows in India, Alexander publicly shamed Hephaestion, privately rebuked Craterus, and forced a reconciliation under threat of death to whichever started future trouble.
