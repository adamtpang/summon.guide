# Regent, Chaeronea, and the Murder of Philip

> **Key principle:** Alexander earns real military credibility, as regent at sixteen and as the officer who broke the Theban Sacred Band at Chaeronea, before Plutarch pivots to the far more dangerous battlefield of his own household, where a drunken toast at Philip's new wedding unravels into exile, intrigue, and finally assassination.

*Synthesized from Plutarch's Life of Alexander, translated by John Dryden, revised by Arthur Hugh Clough.*

## Key lessons

- At sixteen, left in charge of Macedonia with his father's royal seal while Philip campaigned against Byzantium, Alexander crushed a revolt by the Maedi, stormed their chief town, expelled its people, and resettled it with a mixed colony under his own name, Alexandropolis.
- At the Battle of Chaeronea, Alexander is credited as the first to charge the Theban Sacred Band; Plutarch notes that in his own time an old oak by the river Cephisus was still pointed out as the spot where Alexander's tent had stood.
- The family rupture began at the wedding of Philip to Cleopatra: her uncle Attalus, drunk, called on the Macedonians to pray for a legitimate heir by her, and Alexander, taking this as an insult to his own legitimacy, hurled a cup at him and demanded "am I then a bastard?"
- Philip lunged at his son in fury but stumbled and fell, and Alexander mocked him openly for a man preparing to cross from Europe into Asia who could not cross a room without falling; Alexander and Olympias then left the court, she to Epirus, he to Illyria.
- The old family friend Demaratus of Corinth shamed Philip into reconciliation by pointing out the absurdity of worrying about unity in Greece while his own house was in open discord, and Philip recalled his son.
- The peace broke again over a marriage scheme: when the Carian governor Pixodorus proposed his daughter for Philip's other son Arrhidaeus, Olympias and Alexander's circle convinced Alexander it was a plot to hand him the succession, so Alexander secretly offered himself instead; Philip, discovering this, furiously accused Alexander of degrading himself by courting a barbarian's daughter and banished several of Alexander's friends, including Harpalus and Ptolemy, whom Alexander later restored to high honor.
- Pausanias, having been wronged by Attalus and Cleopatra and denied justice by Philip, assassinated the king; suspicion for encouraging the murder fell heavily on Olympias, and some suspicion even touched Alexander, who was said to have quoted a line from Euripides's Medea about vengeance on husband and father when Pausanias first complained to him.
- Alexander punished the actual conspirators severely and rebuked Olympias for her cruelty toward Cleopatra in his absence, a detail Plutarch offers as evidence against reading Alexander himself as complicit in his father's death.
