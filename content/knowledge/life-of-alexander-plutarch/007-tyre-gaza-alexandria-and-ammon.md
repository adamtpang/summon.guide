# Tyre, Gaza, Alexandria, and the Oracle of Ammon

> **Key principle:** Across a seven-month siege, a desert pilgrimage, and the founding of a city, Plutarch shows Alexander using prophecy as a tool for morale while keeping a clear, almost skeptical head about what he actually believes about himself.

*Synthesized from Plutarch's Life of Alexander, translated by John Dryden, revised by Arthur Hugh Clough.*

## Key lessons

- During the seven-month siege of Tyre, Alexander dreamed Hercules stood on the city walls beckoning him in, while the Tyrians dreamed their own god Apollo meant to abandon them for Alexander, so they lashed the god's statue down with ropes as though arresting a deserter.
- When the soothsayer Aristander declared the city would fall "that very month," and the soldiers laughed because it was the month's last day, Alexander simply ordered the day recounted as the twenty-third instead of the thirtieth, then pressed a harder assault than planned, one that actually did take the city that day, turning the prophecy true by decree.
- At Gaza, a bird dropped a clod of earth on Alexander's shoulder before getting tangled in the ropes of a siege engine, matching Aristander's prediction that Alexander would be wounded and the city taken, both of which happened.
- Founding Alexandria came from a dream of Homer reciting verses about the island of Pharos; Alexander went to inspect the site personally, judged it perfect, and had the city's outline traced on the ground in flour for lack of chalk, only to watch flocks of birds descend and eat all the flour, an omen his augurs reinterpreted as a sign the city would feed many nations.
- The march to the oracle of Ammon at Siwah risked death by thirst or sandstorm, the same desert that had swallowed fifty thousand of Cambyses's men; Alexander pushed on anyway, and the expedition was saved first by unexpected rain and then by ravens that guided lost stragglers back to the group whenever they wandered off at night.
- The high priest greeted Alexander as the son of Ammon; when asked whether Philip's murderers had all been punished, Alexander corrected the question to ask about his mortal father Philip, and the god's answer, that vengeance was complete and world dominion was his, left him satisfied, though he told his mother in a letter that some answers were secret and for her ears only.
- Alexander's own stated view of divinity was measured rather than grandiose: he approved a philosopher's line that all men are ruled by God because whatever commands in anything is divine, and held that God is father to all people but especially to the best of them; he played the god-descended king mainly for barbarians, not for Greeks.
- Two moments puncture any claim he actually believed himself divine: wounded and bleeding, he told his friends, "this, my friends, is real flowing blood, not Ichor," and when a thunderclap frightened everyone, he laughed off Anaxarchus's question about whether Jupiter's son could do the same, joking he had no wish to be as fearsome as the governors' heads Anaxarchus apparently wanted served at dinner.
