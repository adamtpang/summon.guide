# Turning Back: the Mutiny and the Wound Among the Mallians

> **Key principle:** After the costly win over Porus, Alexander's own soldiers balk at crossing the Ganges into an army reported at hundreds of thousands strong, and his fury gives way to grudging retreat; soon after, storming a Mallian town alone nearly gets him killed, and Plutarch closes the section with Alexander testing his own mind against Indian ascetics who refuse to be impressed by him.

*Synthesized from Plutarch's Life of Alexander, translated by John Dryden, revised by Arthur Hugh Clough.*

## Key lessons

- Having barely beaten a Porus who fielded only twenty thousand infantry, the Macedonians balked at Alexander's plan to cross the Ganges against reported forces of hundreds of thousands of infantry and cavalry, thousands of chariots, and thousands of elephants. Plutarch notes this wasn't exaggeration: a king named Androcottus who later ruled the region actually fielded armies and elephant herds of comparable scale.
- Alexander reacted first with rage, shutting himself in his tent and declaring that turning back would be an admission of defeat, but the pleading of his soldiers, weeping at his tent's entrance, eventually wore him down into agreeing to turn around.
- Unwilling to let the campaign's edge look like a limit, he left behind deliberately oversized armor, bits, and mangers, deceptive relics meant to convince later travelers that a race of giants had marched there, and built altars that Plutarch says local kings were still honoring with sacrifice generations later.
- Sailing down the rivers toward the ocean, Alexander besieged a Mallian town and, impatient with his men's pace, was first over the wall on a ladder that broke behind him, stranding him alone above the defenders. He jumped down into the enemy rather than wait, and his flashing armor briefly scattered the defenders before they closed in.
- Nearly overwhelmed, he took an arrow through his cuirass into his ribs and was almost finished off by a swordsman before Peucestes and Limnaeus reached him (Limnaeus died of his wounds); a club blow to the neck then forced him against the wall until Macedonian reinforcements broke through, carried him out fainting, and the camp briefly believed him dead. Surgeons had to saw off the arrow shaft and cut the head out of the bone.
- Later, questioning captured Gymnosophist philosophers who had incited revolt, Alexander staged a riddle contest, letting the eldest judge which answers were worst, with death threatened for the loser (a threat undercut by the closing exchange: told he must die first for judging every answer worse than the last, the philosopher pointed out Alexander had only promised to kill the worst answerer, not the judge). Answers included that the living outnumber the dead because "the dead are not at all," and that day is older than night "by one day at least."
- Alexander also sought out the ascetics Calanus and Dandamis: Calanus demanded he strip naked before he would speak to him at all, while Dandamis received him courteously, praising Socrates, Pythagoras, and Diogenes but faulting them for excessive deference to their own countries' customs and laws.
