# After Issus: Darius's Captured Family

> **Key principle:** With Darius's tent, treasure, mother, wife, and daughters all in his hands, Alexander treats the plunder as evidence of what royalty actually is, and treats the women as a test of his own discipline rather than a spoil to be claimed, a temperance Plutarch extends into a full portrait of his daily habits.

*Synthesized from Plutarch's Life of Alexander, translated by John Dryden, revised by Arthur Hugh Clough.*

## Key lessons

- Entering Darius's abandoned tent at Issus, with its gold bathing vessels, perfumed ointments, and lavish banqueting furniture, Alexander remarked simply, "This, it seems, is royalty," before being told that Darius's mother, wife, and two unmarried daughters had been taken prisoner and assumed him dead.
- He sent Leonnatus to reassure the royal women that Darius was alive, that Alexander made war only for dominion, and that they would keep every honor and comfort they had under Darius, including leave to bury their dead as they saw fit and pensions larger than before; Plutarch says they were treated with such care they seemed lodged in a temple rather than an enemy camp.
- Despite Darius's wife being renowned as the most beautiful woman of her time, Alexander sought no intimacy with her or any other captive, holding it more kingly to master himself than to conquer his enemies; his one exception before marriage was Barsine, Memnon's Greek-educated widow, whom he grew attached to partly at Parmenio's encouragement.
- When his lieutenant Philoxenus wrote asking whether he wished to purchase two beautiful boys for sale, and when Hagnon offered to buy him a handsome Corinthian youth, Alexander was genuinely offended by both offers and fired back sharp letters demanding to know what baseness in his character had invited such suggestions.
- He ordered Parmenio to execute two Macedonian soldiers, Damon and Timotheus, after they were found guilty of abusing the wives of some men in his pay, calling them beasts fit only for mischief; in the same letter he insisted he had never even sought to see Darius's wife's face, let alone heard anyone discuss her beauty in his presence.
- Alexander said sleep and sex were the two things that most reminded him he was mortal, framing both as forms of weariness and weakness rather than pleasures to chase.
- His diet was famously plain: he told his adoptive "mother" Ada, who kept sending him delicacies and offering cooks, that his tutor Leonidas had already given him the best seasoning there was, a hard night's march to sharpen the appetite for breakfast and a light breakfast to sharpen it for supper; Leonidas used to search his mother's gifts to strip out anything soft or extravagant.
- His days followed a fixed rhythm: sacrifice, breakfast, then hunting, writing, judging military questions, or reading, with dinner delayed until dark and capped by rule at ten thousand drachmas regardless of his growing wealth; he was slower to drink than his reputation suggested, since what people mistook for heavy drinking was really his love of long conversation over a single cup.
