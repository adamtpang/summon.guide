# The Long Road Home: Gedrosia, Susa Weddings, and the Mutiny at Opis

> **Key principle:** Between the retreat from India and the final years at Babylon, Plutarch stacks disaster (the Gedrosian desert march), excess (the week-long bacchanal through Carmania), reflection (Cyrus's tomb, Calanus's self-immolation), and crisis (the veterans' mutiny at the favor shown to Persian-trained troops) to show an empire being fused together by force, ceremony, and, at the end, an emperor's tears.

*Synthesized from Plutarch's Life of Alexander, translated by John Dryden, revised by Arthur Hugh Clough.*

## Key lessons

- Alexander sent his fleet under Nearchus around the Indian coast while marching the rest of the army home through the territory of the Orites. The march was catastrophic: of roughly 120,000 infantry and 15,000 cavalry that had gone into India, he brought back barely a quarter, lost mostly to disease, bad diet, heat, and above all famine in a barren country whose inhabitants survived on foul-tasting fish-fed sheep.
- Once the army reached Carmania and real supplies, Alexander threw a seven-day bacchanalian procession, feasting on a moving platform drawn by eight horses while soldiers traded weapons for drinking cups and the march dissolved into music and dancing "as in the rites of Bacchus." At a dance contest soon after, his favorite Bagoas won and crossed the theater to sit by him, and the cheering Macedonians pressed Alexander until he kissed him in front of them.
- Word of the disasters in India had encouraged revolt and corrupt behavior among provincial governors, and even Alexander's mother Olympias and sister Cleopatra split Antipater's regency between them at home. Alexander punished offending commanders on his way through Persia, in one case personally spearing a son of Abuletes and, when Abuletes brought coin instead of needed supplies, ordering the money thrown to the horses.
- Finding Cyrus's tomb robbed, Alexander executed the culprit and had its inscription, an address from Cyrus asking whoever found it not to grudge him "this little earth which covers my body," recut in Greek; Plutarch says the words visibly moved him with the instability of human fortune. Around the same time, the ascetic Calanus, ill with a bowel disease, requested a funeral pyre, said his farewells calmly, and burned himself alive without flinching, promising to see Alexander again at Babylon.
- At Susa, Alexander staged a mass wedding, marrying Darius's daughter Statira himself and pairing his officers with noble Persian women before roughly nine thousand guests, and separately paid off his soldiers' collective debts of nearly 9,870 talents. One officer, Antigenes, fraudulently claimed a debt he didn't owe using a fake creditor; when exposed, Alexander stripped his command but quietly let him keep the money out of respect for his past bravery and fear he might otherwise kill himself from shame.
- Alexander's newly trained corps of thirty thousand Persian youths impressed him on review, which alarmed the veteran Macedonians, who felt replaced; when Alexander then began discharging his unfit and wounded soldiers, they erupted, accusing him of favoring "dancing boys" over the men who had won his empire. Furious, he dismissed them and staffed his guard entirely with Persians.
- Shamed and frightened by the reversal, the Macedonians returned unarmed to weep outside his tent for two days until Alexander finally came out, wept himself, forgave them, and sent the discharged veterans home with honors, including a standing order that they receive front-row seats at public shows and that dead soldiers' children keep receiving their fathers' pay.
