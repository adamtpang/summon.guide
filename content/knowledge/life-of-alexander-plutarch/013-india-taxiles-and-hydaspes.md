# Into India: Taxiles and the Battle of the Hydaspes

> **Key principle:** Plutarch pairs Alexander's generosity toward a king who offers peace with his ruthlessness toward those who resist, then stages the campaign's set-piece battle, the Hydaspes crossing against Porus, as proof that neither storms nor rivers nor elephants could match his nerve, though the cost lands on his oldest companion, Bucephalas.

*Synthesized from Plutarch's Life of Alexander, translated by John Dryden, revised by Arthur Hugh Clough.*

## Key lessons

- Before pushing into India, Alexander noticed his army was slowed by plunder, so at dawn he burned his own baggage wagons first, then his friends', then ordered the rest of the army's burned too. Most soldiers, rather than resenting it, shouted and cheered and destroyed their own excess, and Plutarch notes Alexander grew markedly harsher afterward, executing his friend Menander for abandoning a garrison and personally shooting a rebel named Orsodates.
- King Taxiles, ruling territory Plutarch compares in size to Egypt, greeted Alexander by asking why they should fight at all if Alexander wasn't there to steal water or food, the only things worth a real war. Alexander was so charmed he embraced him, joked that Taxiles wouldn't escape a "contest of kindness" that easily, then returned Taxiles's gifts many times over, including a thousand talents in coined money, a generosity that irritated Alexander's old friends but won over many locals.
- The campaign's one lasting stain: after Indian mercenaries defending several cities negotiated a surrender and marched away under its terms, Alexander fell on them and killed them all, a betrayal Plutarch flags as breaking the just conduct he otherwise kept. He also hanged a number of Indian philosophers who had been urging free cities to resist him.
- At the Hydaspes river, Porus kept his elephants massed on the far bank facing Alexander's camp. Alexander staged nightly noise to mask his intentions, then crossed upstream in a violent storm, losing men to lightning before finally forcing a footing on unstable, flooded ground; Onesicritus's version has him joking to his men about the risks he ran "to merit your praise" from Athens.
- Alexander pushed ahead with cavalry alone, judging he could outrun any infantry response, and it worked: his advance guard destroyed a chariot force and cavalry screen before Porus arrived with his main army. Splitting his own force, Alexander struck the left wing himself while Coenus took the right, breaking both wings and crushing the Indian center against its own elephants in an eight-hour battle.
- Porus, a famously large man matched to an equally massive elephant that shielded him through the fight and gently tried to draw out arrows with its trunk once he was wounded, was captured and asked how he wished to be treated. His answer, "As a king," so satisfied Alexander that he let Porus keep his throne as a subject ruler and expanded his territory with fifteen additional nations.
- Soon after the battle, the aged Bucephalas died, either of wounds or simply of age at thirty. Alexander mourned him like a lost companion and founded a city, Bucephalia, on the Hydaspes in his memory, alongside another city named for his dog Peritas.
