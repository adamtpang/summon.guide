# Hephaestion's Death, Omens, and the Death of Alexander

> **Key principle:** Hephaestion's sudden death from an ordinary broken diet unhinges Alexander into massacre and grandiose mourning; a mounting run of bad omens at Babylon turns him newly superstitious and suspicious of his own circle; and the Life ends with the royal diary's spare, dated account of his last illness, followed at once by Roxana's murder of Statira and Perdiccas's seizure of power.

*Synthesized from Plutarch's Life of Alexander, translated by John Dryden, revised by Arthur Hugh Clough.*

## Key lessons

- Hephaestion, ill with a fever, ignored his physician's diet, ate a fowl and drank a large draught of wine while the doctor was at the theater, and died shortly after. Alexander's grief went past ordinary mourning: he cut the manes and tails of all the army's horses and mules, tore down city battlements, crucified the physician Glaucus, and, on direction from the oracle of Ammon, ordered Hephaestion honored with sacrifices as a hero.
- Seeking release from grief through war, Alexander fell on the Cossaean nation and put them to the sword as what Plutarch flatly calls "a sacrifice to Hephaestion's ghost," and he planned a tomb costing ten thousand talents, entertaining (though ultimately declining) the sculptor Stasicrates's earlier offer to carve Mount Athos itself into a colossal statue of a man holding a city in one hand.
- Approaching Babylon, Alexander ignored a warning from Chaldean diviners relayed by Nearchus, then met a run of bad signs: fighting crows falling dead near him, a sacrificial liver found without a lobe, a tame ass kicking his prize lion to death, and, strangest of all, a freed prisoner named Dionysius found sitting silently on the throne wearing the king's own robe and diadem, claiming Serapis had led him there; Alexander had him executed on his soothsayers' advice.
- The string of omens left Alexander distrustful of the gods' favor and suspicious of the people around him, particularly Antipater's family; when Antipater's son Cassander laughed at Persians prostrating themselves before the king, Alexander seized him by the hair and smashed his head against a wall, an encounter Plutarch says haunted Cassander so deeply that decades later, as king of Macedonia, the mere sight of Alexander's statue at Delphi left him shaking and dizzy.
- The Life's closing illness is told through the royal diary in dated entries: fever from the eighteenth of the month, bathing and dice with Medius, a worsening fever through the twenty-first and twenty-second, being carried to oversee sacrifices on the twenty-fourth, losing speech by the twenty-sixth, and the Macedonian soldiers forcing their way in unarmed to file silently past his bed once rumors spread that he was already dead. Messengers sent to ask Serapis whether to move him to the temple were told not to. He died on the evening of the twenty-eighth.
- Plutarch addresses the poisoning rumor directly: no one suspected it at the time, and though a story later circulated (Olympias reportedly executed people over it years afterward, and Aristotle was accused of supplying the poison through Antipater), Plutarch judges it most likely invented, noting the body stayed unusually fresh and untainted for days despite the summer heat, evidence against poison rather than for it.
- Immediately after his death, Roxana, pregnant and newly influential, lured Statira with a forged letter pretending Alexander still lived, murdered her and her sister, and hid the bodies in a filled-in well, with the complicity of Perdiccas, who used the nominally ruling but "weak of intellect" Arrhidaeus (Philip's son, his mind reportedly ruined by drugs Olympias had given him) as cover to seize real authority over the empire.
