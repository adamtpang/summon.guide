# Callisthenes and the Proskynesis Controversy

> **Key principle:** Callisthenes refused to perform the Persian ritual of prostration before Alexander when nearly everyone else complied, a principled stand Plutarch credits with sparing the Macedonians and Alexander a lasting disgrace, but his bluntness and lack of political tact turned the court against him and ultimately cost him his life.

*Synthesized from Plutarch's Life of Alexander, translated by John Dryden, revised by Arthur Hugh Clough.*

## Key lessons

- Callisthenes, a relative and former student of Aristotle, first drew Anaxarchus's irritation with a sharp joke at a dinner, noting that Anaxarchus needed three warm cloaks in the region he claimed was warmer than Greece, where one had always sufficed.
- His popularity with the younger Macedonians and his austere, disapproving manner at banquets made him enemies among the court's flatterers, who resented that he was admired for exactly the qualities they lacked.
- Challenged by Alexander to speak, Callisthenes first delivered a dazzling extemporaneous oration praising the Macedonians, then, needled by Alexander to prove his skill further, delivered an equally skilled oration cataloguing their faults, a reversal that won him lasting Macedonian resentment even though Alexander had set him up for it.
- At a banquet where guests drank from a cup, then bowed and kissed Alexander in the Persian manner, Callisthenes drank but stepped forward to kiss Alexander without having bowed; Demetrius Phidon publicly blocked him, pointing out he alone had refused to adore the king, and Callisthenes only remarked dryly that he would leave "with a kiss less than the rest."
- Hephaestion resented Callisthenes for allegedly breaking a private promise to perform the prostration like everyone else, and other courtiers, including Lysimachus and Hagnon, spread word that Callisthenes boasted of his resistance and was idolized by the young men as the sole man of courage among thousands.
- When the royal page Hermolaus was found conspiring against Alexander's life, Callisthenes was implicated, accused of telling Hermolaus that killing the "most illustrious man on earth" was the fastest way to become illustrious himself and reminding him Alexander was as physically vulnerable as anyone.
- Alexander's own letters give conflicting accounts, one to Craterus and others claims the young conspirators said under torture that they acted alone, while a later letter to Antipater accuses Callisthenes directly and threatens to punish both him and those who had sent him, an unmistakable jab at Aristotle, in whose house Callisthenes had been raised.
- Accounts of his death differ: some say he was hanged outright, others that he died of illness in prison, while Chares reports he was kept in chains for seven months, meant to be tried once Aristotle could be present, and died there of disease after growing severely overweight in captivity, his death falling around the time Alexander himself was wounded fighting the Malli in India.
