# PayPal Mafia Boss

> **Key principle:** X.com was Musk's bet that "bankers are rich and dumb," built on an arbitrage insight he'd noticed years earlier as an intern. He nearly lost everything to two separate leadership revolts, first from cofounder Harris Fricker and then from a 2000 boardroom coup that made Peter Thiel CEO while Musk was on a plane to his honeymoon, yet he still walked away from the eBay sale roughly $250 million richer than when he started.

*Synthesized from Chapter 5 of Elon Musk: Tesla, SpaceX, and the Quest for a Fantastic Future by Ashlee Vance.*

## Key lessons

- The core insight behind X.com traced back to Musk's internship at the Bank of Nova Scotia in the early 1990s, where he calculated that U.S. government-backstopped "Brady bonds" made distressed Brazilian debt worth about 50 cents on the dollar while it traded at 25 cents. He pitched the arbitrage to his boss and the bank's CEO, who rejected it out of caution, a moment Musk later said gave him confidence that big incumbents run off cliffs together.
- Musk incorporated X.com in March 1999, before Zip2's sale to Compaq had even closed, and put about $12 million of his roughly $22 million Zip2 payout into it, leaving himself only around $4 million after taxes for personal spending, even as he also bought a $1 million McLaren F1 and an 1,800-square-foot condo.
- X.com's cofounders were Musk, Ed Ho (an engineer from Zip2 and SGI), and two Canadian finance contacts, Harris Fricker and Christopher Payne. Personality conflict surfaced fast: Fricker, a Rhodes scholar who wanted to run the company more conventionally, staged a mini-coup just five months in, threatening to walk out with the whole team unless he became CEO. Musk refused to be blackmailed, Fricker left and took most of the engineers with him, and Musk had to rebuild the company almost from scratch with backing from Sequoia's Mike Moritz.
- X.com relaunched with new hires and, on the night before Thanksgiving 1999, went live as one of the world's first online banks with FDIC insurance and mutual funds, plus gimmicks like a $20 signup bonus and $10 referral bonus. More than 200,000 people signed up within its first couple of months.
- Its main rival, Confinity (founded by Max Levchin and Peter Thiel, building the PayPal person-to-person payment product), literally rented office space from X.com down the hall before the two companies became locked in an expensive feature war, each burning cash on signup bonuses in what one engineer called "the internet version of making it rain at a strip club."
- The companies merged in March 2000, with X.com's stronger cash position giving Musk control as largest shareholder of the combined entity. The merger did not resolve the culture clash: Musk pushed for the X.com brand and Microsoft-based infrastructure, while Levchin's team favored the PayPal name and Linux, a fight serious enough that Thiel resigned two months after the merger and Levchin nearly walked too.
- As technical problems mounted (the site crashed roughly once a week, fraud losses grew, CFO Roelof Botha felt Musk wasn't giving the board an accurate picture), a group of employees met at a Palo Alto bar called Fanny & Alexander and plotted to install Thiel as CEO. They delivered letters of no confidence to the board the same night in September 2000 that Musk and his wife Justine boarded a flight to combine a fundraising trip with a long-delayed honeymoon in Sydney. Musk was ousted before the plane landed.
- Musk flew straight back and briefly tried to reverse the decision, but relented once it was clear the board had moved on; several loyal employees, including engineer Jeremy Stoppelman (later CEO of Yelp), were furious on his behalf, but Musk himself stayed on as an advisor, kept investing, and became PayPal's largest shareholder rather than turning vindictive.
- In June 2001 Thiel renamed the company PayPal. When eBay came courting, Musk and Moritz pushed the board to reject early offers and hold out; eBay eventually paid $1.5 billion in July 2002, netting Musk about $250 million ($180 million after taxes) as PayPal's biggest shareholder, even though he hadn't been CEO for nearly two years.
- Musk's reputation took a real hit afterward: Eric Jackson's 2004 book The PayPal Wars painted him as an egomaniacal, wrong-headed leader next to a heroic Thiel and Levchin, prompting Musk to fire off a combative 2,200-word rebuttal email to the gossip blog Valleywag in 2007 defending his cofounder status. Most people Vance interviewed sided with Musk on the credit question but agreed with the criticism that Musk had mishandled the branding, infrastructure, and fraud fights.
- Musk married Justine Wilson in January 2000; the coup derailed their honeymoon further, and when they finally traveled to Brazil and a South African game reserve that December, Musk contracted a severe, near-fatal case of falciparum malaria that put him in intensive care for ten days and cost him six months of recovery and forty-five pounds.
