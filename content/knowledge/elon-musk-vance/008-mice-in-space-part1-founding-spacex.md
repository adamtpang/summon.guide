# Mice in Space, Part 1: Founding SpaceX

> **Key principle:** SpaceX did not start as a rocket company; it started as a publicity stunt (mice, then a Mars greenhouse) meant to reignite public interest in space. It only became a real company once Musk, rebuffed by Russian rocket sellers in February 2002, worked out on a spreadsheet during the flight home that he could build a cheaper rocket himself, and then found in Tom Mueller a credentialed engineer who believed it was possible.

*Synthesized from Chapter 6 of Elon Musk: Tesla, SpaceX, and the Quest for a Fantastic Future by Ashlee Vance.*

## Key lessons

- Musk turned 30 in June 2001 and, freshly pushed out of the PayPal CEO role, moved to Los Angeles with Justine partly for its glitz but explicitly for its access to the aerospace industry; he began reading old Soviet rocket manuals in cabanas in Las Vegas, alarming PayPal colleagues like Kevin Hartz.
- His entry point into the space world was the nonprofit Mars Society: he showed up uninvited to a $500-a-plate fundraiser with a $5,000 check, joined the board, donated $100,000 more, and got seated at dinner next to founder Robert Zubrin, director James Cameron, and NASA scientist Carol Stoker.
- The original concept was absurdly literal: send mice to Mars and back, breeding along the way (roughly costed at $15 million by Musk's back-of-envelope math); friend George Zachary's first reaction was to ask "do the mice come back?" and Jeff Skoll bought Musk a giant wheel of cheese as a joke gift for the trip.
- The idea evolved through hotel-conference-room "salons" (Renaissance LAX, Sheraton Palo Alto) into "Mars Oasis": launch a robotic greenhouse to grow a plant in Martian regolith, with a window and video feed so the public and school kids could watch it grow, budgeted around $20-30 million even though space veteran Dave Bearden thought $200 million was closer to realistic.
- Musk resigned from the Mars Society board and founded his own outfit, the Life to Mars Foundation, pulling in credentialed help including Mike Griffin (aerospace/electrical/civil engineering degrees, ex-CIA In-Q-Tel, ex-NASA/JPL, later NASA administrator in 2005) as "space thinker in chief."
- The pivot to building a rocket happened on a trip to Russia (with Jim Cantrell and college friend Adeo Ressi) to buy refurbished ICBMs; after three meetings over four months, a Russian negotiator quoted $8 million per missile, rejected Musk's counteroffer of $8 million for two with a dismissive "Young boy. No," and Musk stormed out near the end of February 2002.
- On the flight home Musk built a spreadsheet estimating he could build a rocket from raw materials cheaper than buying one from the Russians, having spent months self-studying texts like Rocket Propulsion Elements and Fundamentals of Astrodynamics; Griffin and Cantrell were initially dismissive but were won over by the level of detail.
- A personal tragedy intersected with the company's founding: Musk and Justine's son Nevada Alexander Musk died of SIDS-related complications at ten weeks old in mid-2002, the same period as the eBay-PayPal deal ($1.5 billion, netting Musk over $100 million); Musk's refusal to grieve openly ("wallowing in sadness does no good for anyone") became a lasting point of friction with Justine.
- SpaceX was founded June 2002 in a 75,000-square-foot former shipping warehouse at 1310 East Grand Avenue in El Segundo; Musk personally unloaded the first delivery trucks and set the now-signature aesthetic (glossy epoxy floors, white paint, engineers and machinists sharing floor space rather than separated by miles as at traditional aerospace firms).
- Key early hires: Tom Mueller (ex-TRW propulsion engineer and amateur-rocketry legend, recruited after Musk cross-examined him at a Huntington Beach workshop in January 2002) to build the Merlin and Kestrel engines; Chris Thompson (ex-Marine, ex-Boeing) as VP of operations; Tim Buzza (ex-Boeing rocket tester); Hans Koenigsmann for avionics; and Gwynne Shotwell, hired as SpaceX's first salesperson and later president.
- Mary Beth Brown ("MB") joined as Musk's loyal, gatekeeping assistant, described as a real-life Pepper Potts to his Tony Stark: she managed his schedule, meals, and moods, sent out "Kook of the Week" fan-mail compilations for laughs, and controlled access to Musk with a system of nods (go in) or head-shakes (come back later, bad day).
- Musk recruited engineering talent directly from top aerospace programs, personally cold-calling students in their dorm rooms (Stanford's Michael Colonno assumed it was a prank call); by the end of the first year, one or two new hires joined almost weekly, including employee No. 23, Kevin Brogan, an ex-TRW engineer who compared TRW's culture to "a country club" where "nobody did anything."
- The Falcon 1 name (a nod to Star Wars) was announced with an openly optimistic 15-month timeline (first launch targeted November 2003) at a promised $6.9 million for a 1,400-pound payload versus the industry's roughly $30 million for 550 pounds; Musk decided early to build engines, tanks, and avionics in-house rather than contract them out, judging existing aerospace suppliers too slow and expensive.
- The chapter's earliest hardware milestone was the gas generator test in Mojave: Mueller, Buzza, and junior engineers drove a homemade gas generator out to a borrowed XCOR test stand, ran a 90-second ignition that blackened the sky with smoke over the airport tower and drew a scolding from the airport manager, then talked their way into running more tests the same day and had the unit tuned within two weeks.
