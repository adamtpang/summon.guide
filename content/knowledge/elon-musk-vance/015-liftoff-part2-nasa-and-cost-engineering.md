# Liftoff, Part 2: NASA and Cost Engineering

> **Key principle:** SpaceX's cheap, in-house avionics and manufacturing let it out-execute NASA and legacy contractors on cost and speed, but that same speed collided constantly with a regulatory and political system built to reward caution and protect incumbents like ULA.

*Synthesized from Chapter 9 of Elon Musk: Tesla, SpaceX, and the Quest for a Fantastic Future by Ashlee Vance.*

## Key lessons

- Kevin Watson, hired from JPL in 2008, promised Musk in his job interview he could deliver a rocket avionics system for around $10,000 versus the $10 million-plus such systems normally cost ("it would cost you more than ten thousand dollars just for the food at a meeting to discuss the cost of the avionics," Watson said of traditional aerospace). His first build, a communications box nicknamed CUCU ("cuckoo") for talking to the ISS, passed NASA's protocol tests on the first try, despite NASA staff privately dismissing the SpaceX team as "the guys in the garage." The full Dragon/Falcon 9 avionics platform ultimately landed just over the $10,000 target.
- Musk personally signed off on every SpaceX expenditure above $10,000 and ran fast weekly decision meetings that let "the entire ship turn ninety degrees instantly," in Watson's words — a speed he contrasted with Lockheed Martin. SpaceX built a test bed on the third floor of Hawthorne that replicates a rocket's full electronics end to end for flight simulations; in one case engineers caught a software error, patched it, tested it, and uploaded the fix to a Falcon 9 already on the pad in under thirty minutes, versus NASA's shuttle-era norm of a three-week delay to fix any in-flight problem.
- In a May 2010 company-wide email titled "Acronyms Seriously Suck," Musk banned unapproved made-up acronyms (calling out clunky examples like "HTS"/"VTS" test-stand designations), requiring any new acronym to be personally approved by him. Employees nicknamed the policy the ASS Rule.
- SpaceX repeatedly clashed with the FAA and other regulators over paperwork requirements — a week-long review process just to change a launch procedure like swapping a filter. Musk once berated an FAA official for roughly ten minutes on a conference call, and separately escalated a complaint about an FAA subordinate straight to that person's boss. Musk's stated view: regulators face asymmetric incentives (career risk if a rule change goes wrong, no reward if it goes right), which structurally biases them against changing anything.
- SpaceX hired former astronaut Ken Bowersox in mid-2009 as VP of astronaut safety and mission assurance to bring more process discipline. After a flawed part reached the test stand undetected (one engineer likened it to "a coffee cup not having a bottom"), Bowersox pushed to investigate the root cause; Musk instead moved to fix what he believed was the direct cause and let Bowersox go after about two years. Unnamed government officials told Vance that Musk's blunt treatment of high-ranking military and agency officials risked becoming SpaceX's biggest liability with the Pentagon and NASA.
- Gwynne Shotwell, SpaceX's president, grew up outside Chicago, studied mechanical engineering and applied math at Northwestern, and got written up repeatedly for breaking union rules during a Chrysler management-training rotation before moving to the Aerospace Corporation and then the startup Microcosm. She joined SpaceX in 2002 as employee No. 7 after telling Musk directly that the company needed a real business-development hire, and went on to negotiate the $278 million NASA contract in August 2006 that helped keep SpaceX alive; she became president and COO at the end of 2008.
- At a March 2014 congressional hearing, Musk presented figures showing ULA charging about $380 million per flight versus SpaceX's roughly $90 million (higher than SpaceX's standard $60 million due to added government requirements), while ULA CEO Michael Gass had no counter-figure and admitted ULA had stockpiled Russian-made engines. SpaceX later sued the U.S. Air Force over launch-contract bidding via its "freedomtolaunch.com" campaign. Separately, NASA's commercial crew competition awarded SpaceX $2.6 billion and Boeing $4.2 billion to ferry astronauts to the ISS by 2017, with Musk saying he didn't mind Boeing getting twice the money for "worse technology" since two competing providers was better for the industry.
- The cheap Falcon 1 ($6-12 million per flight) never found a sustainable market despite interest sparked by Google's 2007 Lunar X Prize, and flew its last mission in July 2009 (a Malaysian government satellite). Falcon 9 first reached orbit in June 2010, and on May 22, 2012, a Falcon 9/Dragon mission became the first commercial spacecraft to dock with the ISS — astronaut Don Pettit caught it with a robotic arm after engineers fixed a last-minute laser-sensor glitch (caused by an unexpected glint) with an emergency software patch during the approach.
- In May 2014, Musk unveiled the Dragon V2 capsule, powered by the SuperDraco engine — the first rocket engine ever fully 3D-printed as a single piece of Inconel alloy — designed to let Dragon land propulsively anywhere on Earth instead of splashing down and being discarded. In a June 2013 company-wide email ("Going Public"), Musk explained he was delaying any SpaceX IPO until the Mars transport system was secure, arguing that public-market volatility and short-term pressure would distract from that mission.
