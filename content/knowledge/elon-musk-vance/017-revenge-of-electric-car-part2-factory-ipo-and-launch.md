# The Revenge of the Electric Car, Part 2: Factory, IPO, and Launch

> **Key principle:** Getting the Model S financed and designed (Part 1) was only half the battle. Tesla still had to acquire a real factory, survive a skeptical IPO, ship a genuinely late and imperfect product, and then claw back from a 2013 near-bankruptcy caused by reservations not converting to sales, a crisis serious enough that Musk struck a handshake deal to sell Tesla to Google before a two-week sales blitz made the deal unnecessary.

*Synthesized from Chapter 10 of Elon Musk: Tesla, SpaceX, and the Quest for a Fantastic Future by Ashlee Vance.*

## Key lessons

- In May 2010, Tesla acquired the former NUMMI plant in Fremont, California, a 5.3-million-square-foot factory once jointly run by GM and Toyota (built 1984, produced cars like the Chevy Nova and Toyota Corolla) that both companies abandoned amid GM's bankruptcy and Toyota's 2010 pullout, leaving 5,000 people jobless. Tesla paid $42 million for a plant once worth $1 billion, while Toyota separately invested $50 million for a 2.5 percent Tesla stake, effectively getting the factory and its stamping machines for free.
- Tesla filed for its IPO shortly after the factory deal closed and went public on June 29, 2010, raising $226 million with shares jumping 41 percent on day one, the first American automaker IPO since Ford in 1956. The press was skeptical: Jalopnik called Tesla "a money pit" citing $290 million in cumulative losses against $147.6 million in revenue, and Nissan's Carlos Ghosn dismissed Tesla as a "pipsqueak" while touting Nissan's own plan for 500,000 electric cars by 2012.
- Post-IPO, Tesla moved fast on engineering: alpha Model S prototypes were built in the Palo Alto office basement using newly stamped Fremont body parts (the first was finished around 2 a.m. and driven without glass, interior, or a hood); Musk personally test-drove and critiqued nearly every iteration, and engineers learned to never tell him something couldn't be done without a fully costed alternative plan and resumes in hand.
- Musk's design process extended to obsessive detail (rejecting a "fish-lipped" sun visor seam, worrying that independently adjustable Model X middle-row seats would look "hodgy podgy") and to the Model X's signature falcon-wing doors, which engineers initially resisted as impractical until Musk demoed them opening cleanly at his own house.
- The Model S launch event took place June 22, 2012, at the repainted Fremont factory (white floors and machines, red robots), roughly 18 months to two-plus years behind various promised dates; many of the factory workers handing over the first cars were former NUMMI autoworkers rehired by Tesla, and several cried during the ceremony.
- Despite the triumphant launch, Tesla could only build about 10 sedans a week initially against thousands of backorders, and became the most-shorted stock among the 100 largest NASDAQ companies; Mitt Romney publicly called Tesla "a loser" in an October 2012 debate. Musk responded by unveiling a secret Supercharger network (six stations across California, Nevada, and Arizona in September 2012) offering free fast charging.
- Quality and channel problems piled up: early cars shipped without parking sensors or radar cruise control, suppliers treated Tesla's low volumes as not worth prioritizing (leading to a "third-rate" sun visor supplier), and George Blankenship (ex-Apple, architect of the Apple Store playbook) built out Tesla's retail stores but could not convert the roughly 10,000-plus $5,000 reservations into actual sales fast enough.
- By February 2013 Tesla was in a genuine crisis: reservations weren't converting, the factory risked sitting idle, and the company had only a couple weeks of cash left. Musk pulled staff from every department onto phone sales ("your new job is delivering cars"), personally guaranteed Model S resale values, replaced underperforming leaders, and in early April struck a handshake deal with Larry Page for Google to acquire Tesla (Musk wanted 8 years of control or until a mass-market EV shipped, plus $5 billion for factory expansion; Google's price was estimated around $6 billion).
- The Google deal became moot when the ad hoc sales team closed a huge volume of orders in about two weeks; Tesla posted its first-ever quarterly profit as a public company on May 8, 2013 ($11 million on $562 million in sales, 4,900 Model S deliveries), sending shares from about $30 to $130 by July, and Tesla repaid its $465 million DOE loan early with interest shortly after.
- The following "Summer of Musk" cemented Tesla as a lifestyle brand rather than just a car company: a June 2013 battery-swap demo (90-second swaps at Supercharger stations), a combative public rebuttal to a critical New York Times review, added titanium/aluminum underbody shielding after Model S fires, an October 2014 dual-motor "Ludicrous"-style Model S reveal (0-60 in 3.2 seconds) with early Autopilot software, and a public Model E/Model Y naming spat with Ford's Alan Mulally. Tesla's over-the-air software updates and no-model-year, no-dealership sales model became a durable structural advantage over rivals like Fisker Automotive (bankrupt, sold to a Chinese firm in 2014) and Better Place (nearly $1 billion raised, bankrupt in 2013).
