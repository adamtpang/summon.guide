# Liftoff, Part 1: Building Falcon 9 and Dragon

> **Key principle:** By the mid-2010s SpaceX had gone from a startup flying a small rocket off a Pacific atoll to an aerospace power that builds 80-90% of its own hardware in-house, prices launches at a fraction of its competitors, and staffs itself with engineers hand-selected and personally interviewed by Musk.

*Synthesized from Chapter 9 of Elon Musk: Tesla, SpaceX, and the Quest for a Fantastic Future by Ashlee Vance.*

## Key lessons

- The Falcon 9 (224.4 feet tall, nine engines in an "octaweb" pattern at the base) became SpaceX's workhorse, launching roughly once a month from sites like Vandenberg Air Force Base. The launch sequence hands control to automated systems ten minutes before liftoff; humans step back and let the computers make the final go/no-go call.
- SpaceX undercut Boeing, Lockheed Martin, and Orbital Sciences on price by a wide margin, charging around $60 million per Falcon 9 launch against a global satellite and launch market that had grown from about $60 billion to more than $200 billion a year. The company had more than fifty flights on its public manifest worth over $5 billion, and had been profitable since its 2008 near-death experience, with an estimated valuation of $12 billion.
- SpaceX's hiring bar was built around finding "type A" builders, not just top-school grads. Dolly Singh, former head of talent acquisition, cold-called researchers from academic papers and had recruiters hand out blank envelopes at trade shows inviting select candidates to secret interviews. Software candidates faced problems requiring 500+ lines of code (versus the usual couple dozen), and every finalist wrote an essay to Musk on why they wanted the job.
- Musk personally interviewed almost all of SpaceX's first thousand hires, including janitors, and kept interviewing engineers as the company grew. His standard interview riddle: walk one mile south, one mile west, one mile north, and end up back where you started — where are you? (Most guess the North Pole; fewer find the near-South-Pole answer.)
- The Hawthorne, California headquarters ("1 Rocket Road") is an all-white building with a 550,000-square-foot factory floor. Musk built a three-story glass office structure in the dead center of the factory floor specifically so engineers would have to walk through the manufacturing area to reach their desks and stay connected to what was being built.
- SpaceX's central strategy is building 80-90% of its rockets, engines, and electronics in-house, versus a competitor like United Launch Alliance (ULA) that depends on more than 1,200 suppliers. A homemade radio cost SpaceX about $5,000 versus $50,000-$100,000 for industrial aerospace-grade equipment. SpaceX also developed its own large-scale friction stir welding process for rocket body panels (later transferred to Tesla for car manufacturing), a technique aggressive enough that Blue Origin poached SpaceX's lead welding expert, Ray Miryekta, souring Musk's relationship with Jeff Bezos.
- Musk is chronically, admittedly overoptimistic about schedules — engineer Kevin Brogan described him picking "the most aggressive time schedule imaginable" and then accelerating it further. Musk's management technique was to ask an engineer "I need the impossible done by Friday at two P.M. Can you do it?" rather than issuing a flat order, so the engineer felt they had personally signed up for the deadline. President Gwynne Shotwell was frequently left to manage customer expectations when Musk's internal target dates leaked out as promises.
- Steve Davis, SpaceX's twenty-second hire, was given the job of building a $120,000-quoted electromechanical actuator for Falcon 1's steering gimbal on a $5,000 budget ("that part is no more complicated than a garage door opener"). After nine months of work and a detailed cost-justification email, Musk's entire reply was "Ok." The finished actuator cost $3,900 and flew successfully.
- The Dragon capsule took about four years to design, starting with Musk and a handful of engineers under thirty and peaking at around a hundred people. It cost roughly $300 million total — 10 to 30 times cheaper than comparable capsule programs elsewhere in the industry — partly because SpaceX renegotiated and improved the recipe for its PICA heat shield material through a NASA deal, and built the capsule with steeper wall angles than Apollo to fit gear and astronauts. Kevin Watson, a 24-year JPL veteran hired in 2008, was tasked by Musk with getting a rocket's entire computing/avionics system down to $10,000, versus the $10 million-plus such systems traditionally cost in aerospace.
