# Mice in Space, Part 2: Falcon 1 and the Kwajalein Launch Attempts

> **Key principle:** Vandenberg Air Force Base froze SpaceX out with launch delays, forcing the company to relocate to Kwajalein Atoll in the Marshall Islands; there, a tiny, exhausted team endured two years and three failed launches (valve failure, more valve/wind issues, and a snapped fuel fitting from salt corrosion) before a fourth attempt reached space but tumbled apart from fuel slosh, and only on the following attempt, years behind schedule, did Falcon 1 finally succeed.

*Synthesized from Chapter 6 of Elon Musk: Tesla, SpaceX, and the Quest for a Fantastic Future by Ashlee Vance.*

## Key lessons

- SpaceX found a three-hundred-acre test site in McGregor, Texas (previously used by the Navy and by Andrew Beal's failed rocket venture, which left behind a three-story concrete tripod) and young engineer Jeremy Hollman, an Iowa State/USC graduate disillusioned by Boeing's post-merger cost-cutting culture, became Mueller's second-in-command there.
- The "Texas Cattle Haul" became SpaceX lore: engineers worked ten days straight in Texas, flew home on Musk's six-seat jet (seven "if someone sat in the toilet"), and drove the first completed Merlin engine 4,000 pounds across Interstate 10 from Los Angeles in a U-Haul behind a Hummer H2.
- Engine testing in Texas was a brutal iterative slog: the Merlin needed to burn 180 seconds to reach orbit but initially conked out after half a second; failures included excessive vibration, cracking that forced a switch from aluminum to Inconel manifolds, a fuel valve that caused a full engine explosion, and a test stand burning down entirely.
- Musk's management style under pressure showed two faces in this stretch: he stayed calm and told an exhausted, cursing Mueller "Just calm down, we'll do it again tomorrow" after two blown engines in one day, but grilled Hollman hard after an early failure for not having an immediate answer on repair time ("Why don't you have an answer?"), and personally worked through the night applying epoxy to cracked $75,000 cooling chambers in his dress clothes.
- SpaceX operated with an intense, informal culture: nightly LAN parties of Quake III and Counter-Strike where Musk (playing as "Random9") reportedly beat his own employees; an off-the-books crew ran fiber-optic cable across utility poles at 2 a.m. to skip permitting; and Musk paid for Hollman's LASIK surgery unprompted after overhearing him complain about broken glasses.
- Musk could be simultaneously generous and harsh: he refused a $200,000 request for equipment while approving similar spending on cosmetic factory flooring, publicly took sole credit for designing the Falcon rocket (angering the engineering team), and, according to one engineer, "the kiss of death was proving Elon wrong about something," with some staff quietly forced out after doing so.
- SpaceX announced the five-engine Falcon 5 (capable of reaching the ISS) before Falcon 1 had even flown once, and staged a publicity event in December 2003 hauling a seven-story Falcon 1 mock-up to sit outside FAA headquarters in Washington, D.C. — seen by engineers like Hollman as a distracting "boondoggle" pulled from real work.
- After Vandenberg Air Force Base (crowded out by Lockheed and Boeing's priority spy-satellite launches) told SpaceX to expect months of delay, Gwynne Shotwell and Hans Koenigsmann picked Kwajalein Atoll off a wall map for its equatorial location and prior use as a U.S. Army missile test site; SpaceX shipped its operation there in June 2005, building a launchpad from scratch on the tiny island of Omelek.
- Life on Kwaj combined grueling manual labor (moving the rocket on wooden planks "like the ancient Egyptians" because Musk wouldn't fund paving a path) with genuine camaraderie: engineers cooked steak dinners, fished off the docks, and slept in converted offices, described by one tech as feeling "like Gilligan's Island except with rockets."
- The first launch attempt (November 26, 2005) scrubbed when a stuck liquid-oxygen valve boiled off fuel at 500 gallons/hour; a second attempt in December failed from high winds and faulty valves; before a third try, the team discovered failing capacitors and flew a technician to Minnesota and back to California and Kwaj in under 80 hours to source and test replacements.
- The first actual launch, March 24, 2006, ended about 25 seconds in when a fire broke out above the Merlin engine and the rocket tumbled back onto the launch site, crashing into SpaceX's own machine shop roof; Musk and SpaceX initially blamed an uncorroded aluminum b-nut fitting on technician Hollman, who confronted Musk directly (with Mary Beth Brown trying to intercept him) before analysis showed the fitting had actually cracked from months of salt corrosion — "dumb luck," per Mueller, not human error.
- Musk publicly compared the failure to other rockets' rocky starts (citing historical success rates like 5 of 9 for Pegasus, 9 of 21 for Soyuz) and wrote that "SpaceX is in this for the long haul and, come hell or high water, we are going to make this work"; Defense Department consultant Pete Worden, who had warned Musk before the failure and been rebuffed ("What do you know? You're just an astronomer"), was later asked by Musk to lead the government's crash investigation.
- The second launch attempt, March 21, 2007, flew for over five minutes, successfully separating its first stage and firing the Kestrel second-stage engine, before unburned propellant sloshing in the tank exposed the engine to air, caused it to flame out, and the rocket broke apart — a failure the team diagnosed quickly, unlike the mystery of the first crash.
- By this point SpaceX was roughly four years behind Musk's original 2003 timeline and, according to people inside and outside the company, financially positioned for at most one or two more launch attempts; Musk kept this pressure largely hidden from staff, and in the middle of the ongoing setbacks reportedly mused to Worden about engineering projects to unify the Kwajalein islands and, separately, about settling Mars.
