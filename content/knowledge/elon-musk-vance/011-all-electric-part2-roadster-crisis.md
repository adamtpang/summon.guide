# All Electric, Part 2: The Roadster's Manufacturing Crisis and Eberhard's Ouster

> **Key principle:** Building two beautiful hand-assembled prototypes turned out to be a completely different problem from manufacturing thousands of reliable cars at a sane cost, and the gap between those two things cost Tesla its founding CEO, most of its original engineering team's morale, and very nearly the company itself.

*Synthesized from Chapter 7 of Elon Musk: Tesla, SpaceX, and the Quest for a Fantastic Future by Ashlee Vance.*

## Key lessons

- Tesla exploited advantages unavailable to a startup a generation earlier: it used a third party's collision-testing facility instead of building a crash-test fleet, borrowed big automakers' cobblestone durability tracks, and tuned cars on an Arctic Circle ice track in Sweden by rewriting code and retesting on the spot rather than trekking home for weeks of meetings the way Tarpenning says BMW would. When Tesla needed a cold-weather test chamber, engineers rented a refrigerated ice cream delivery truck instead of paying for one.
- Detroit's bureaucratic culture stood in sharp contrast: a landlord asked the still-private, cash-flush Tesla for seven years of audited financials and two years of advance rent before it could even lease a small Detroit office, prompting Tarpenning's complaint that "in Silicon Valley, you say you're backed by a venture capitalist, and that's the end of the negotiation."
- The single biggest technical misstep was the two-speed transmission, meant to take the Roadster from zero to 60 in under four seconds in first gear and up to 130 mph in second. Contractor Xtrac's first unit lasted forty seconds; later replacements from other firms broke after roughly 150 miles, with a mean time between failures near 2,000 miles, and a Detroit root-cause team eventually found fourteen separate ways the design could fail. By January 1, 2008, Tesla had to restart the transmission design from scratch a third time, and the two-speed approach was ultimately abandoned for a redesigned single-speed motor and inverter.
- Overseas manufacturing compounded the mess: a promised "state-of-the-art" battery factory in Thailand turned out to be a bare concrete slab under a roof, meant for stove and tire manufacturing, unsuited to sensitive battery electronics in salty, humid heat. Tesla's partner spent about $75,000 retrofitting it, but the supply chain, cells from China, packs assembled in Thailand, body panels from France, motors from Taiwan, final assembly by Lotus in England, shipping around Cape Horn to Los Angeles, meant Tesla paid for most of the car six to nine months before it could book any revenue.
- New finance director Ryan Popple, a former Army officer with a Harvard MBA hired to prep Tesla for an IPO, asked the operations head how the car would actually get built and was told, roughly, that production would be declared and "a miracle is going to happen," a telling snapshot of how undisciplined the manufacturing plan really was.
- Investor Valor Equity sent operations expert Tim Watkins (known for a black leather jacket, a scrunchie, and a fanny pack holding his passport and survival gear) to audit Tesla's costs. He found the company's own bill-of-materials software was widely distrusted and inconsistently used, and that people were estimating bulk-purchase discounts rather than negotiating real prices; one internal estimate claimed each Roadster would cost $68,000 to build against an $85,000 price, but the real figure Watkins reported to Musk in mid-2007 was that full-scale production could run as high as $170,000-$200,000 per car, and, as Musk put it, "about a third of the cars didn't flat-out fucking work" anyway.
- Eberhard tried to rally the team by echoing a speech from VC John Doerr, projecting a photo of his young daughter and telling engineers Tesla existed so she would grow up thinking of cars the way people now think of cordless phones. It boosted morale briefly, but did not resolve the underlying view among staff, voiced by engineer David Lyons, that "the person who starts a company is not necessarily the right person to lead it in the long term."
- Musk, blaming Eberhard for the cost blowup and for not disclosing its severity to the board, called Eberhard by phone on his way to a Motor Press Guild talk to tell him he was being replaced. Tesla's board demoted Eberhard to "president of technology" in August 2007, an arrangement Straubel says only made things worse ("Martin was so bitter and disruptive... running around the office and sowing discontent"); by December 2007 Eberhard left the company entirely, disputing Tesla's characterization that he'd been offered an advisory role, and the two men's public feud continued for years afterward.
- Interim CEO Michael Marks, former head of Flextronics, imposed a disciplined "Marks List" (a 10-point, 100-day plan covering battery-pack faults, body-panel gap tolerances, and reservation targets) and stabilized operations, but his instinct, given the company's dire state, was to package Tesla for sale to a larger automaker. Musk refused to sell or cede control, and Marks was replaced as CEO by Ze'ev Drori on December 3, 2007; Drori functioned more as an executor of Musk's direction than an independent leader.
- Musk then personally drove an intense cost-down campaign, mandating weekly 7 a.m. Thursday bill-of-materials reviews, pushing the motor's cost target from $6,500 down to $3,800 within months, firing people who missed targets or made grammatical errors in emails, and telling employees pushing back on brutal hours, "I would tell those people they will get to see their families a lot when we go bankrupt." He personally flew manufacturing tooling from England to a body-panel factory in France to keep production on schedule.
- The human cost was heavy: Ian Wright left to build electric delivery trucks, Gene Berdichevsky left for Stanford and a battery startup, and Tarpenning drifted away after Eberhard's departure, while Lyons stayed on through Musk's more explosive tirades (including, he recalled, watching an exhausted Musk spit out cold coffee mid-meeting and immediately demand harder work) and likened the environment to "being Kurtz in Apocalypse Now." By early 2008 the Roadster had cost roughly $140 million to develop against an original $25 million estimate, and Tesla was heading into the 2008 financial crisis needing tens of millions more just as investors' own confidence in funding anything was collapsing.
