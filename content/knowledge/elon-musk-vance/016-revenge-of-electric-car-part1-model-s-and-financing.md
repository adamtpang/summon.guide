# The Revenge of the Electric Car, Part 1: The Model S and Its Financing

> **Key principle:** Between 2008 and early 2010, Tesla went from a near-bankrupt one-product company hemorrhaging money on Roadster recalls and a founder lawsuit to a company with a validated design partner (Daimler), a chief designer who could execute Musk's vision (Franz von Holzhausen), and a $465 million federal loan, all while insisting on a ground-up, no-compromise electric sedan rather than a cheaper adapted vehicle.

*Synthesized from Chapter 10 of Elon Musk: Tesla, SpaceX, and the Quest for a Fantastic Future by Ashlee Vance.*

## Key lessons

- By the time the Model S shipped in mid-2012, it wasn't just a good electric car, it beat gas luxury sedans outright: over 300 miles of range, 0-60 in 4.2 seconds, a 17-inch touchscreen controlling nearly every function, roughly a dozen moving parts versus hundreds/thousands in a combustion car, and about 60 percent energy efficiency versus 10-20 percent for gas engines; it won Motor Trend's first unanimous Car of the Year vote (November 2012) and a 99/100 rating from Consumer Reports.
- Before any of that, Tesla nearly didn't survive the Roadster years: a 2009 e-mail raised the Roadster's price from about $92,000 to $109,000 (retroactively hitting roughly 400 already-ordered customers), two voluntary safety recalls hit 345 and then 439 cars for a loose chassis bolt and a chafing power cable, and cofounder Martin Eberhard sued Musk in June 2009 over libel, slander, and breach of contract before the two settled.
- The Model S (code-named WhiteStar) was deliberately not a retrofit: Tesla put the 1,300-pound battery pack flat along the car's base rather than cramming it in like the Roadster had to fit the Lotus Elise chassis, giving the car a low center of gravity and a low polar moment of inertia for handling.
- Tesla's first designer, Henrik Fisker (hired 2007, known for Aston Martin work), delivered what staff called "a giant egg" and blamed Tesla's constraints; Musk later discovered Fisker had pitched his own car company to investors (including Kleiner Perkins, which passed on Tesla to back Fisker) and sued him in 2008 for allegedly using Tesla's $875,000 design payment to launch Fisker Automotive. Fisker ultimately won the arbitration and Tesla had to cover his legal fees.
- Franz von Holzhausen, hired away from Mazda's design directorship in August 2008, redesigned essentially the whole visible car in about three months, working out of a tent inside the SpaceX factory in Hawthorne; he and Musk talked daily and settled early on Musk's must-haves, including seven-passenger seating (driven by Musk's five kids) and a vertically-oriented, laptop-sized touchscreen chosen by literally holding laptops up in a gutted prototype.
- Musk overrode his own engineers repeatedly on things they called impossible: an all-aluminum body (nearly unused in North American manufacturing at the time), a 17-inch capacitive touchscreen (no supplier had built one for a car; Tesla had to convince Asian laptop-screen makers to test for automotive temperature and vibration loads), and the pop-out door handles (one prototype secretly used pivoting handles instead, which Musk rejected outright).
- Tesla built early proof-of-concept vehicles by gutting purchased Mercedes CLS sedans (chosen after test-driving a CLS and an E-Class at a dealership), fitting a flattened battery pack and electronics, and driving the disguised prototypes on public roads; these cars were used to court investors, including Daimler.
- The March 2009 Model S unveiling at SpaceX was largely theater: the display car still used a Mercedes CLS structure underneath, with body panels and the hood held on by magnets that could slide off if pushed, though the press and public had no idea.
- Financing came together through a Washington-savvy hire, Diarmuid O'Connell (ex-management consultant and State Department chief of staff), who filed for a Department of Energy stimulus loan while Tesla courted automaker partners; Daimler tested Tesla's speed by asking for an electric Mercedes A-Class concept, got two prototypes built before the promised visit, ordered 4,000 battery packs for German test vehicles, then in May 2009 invested $50 million for a 10 percent stake in Tesla alongside a battery-pack supply deal for 1,000 Smart cars. Toyota placed a similar order.
- The Daimler deal (called "a seminal moment" by O'Connell, who credited it with convincing "the guys over at the DOE... it's Mercedes freaking Benz") helped Tesla secure a $465 million Department of Energy loan agreement in January 2010, still only a fraction of the $1 billion-plus typically needed to bring a new car to market.
