# All Electric, Part 1: Founding Tesla and the Roadster Unveiling

> **Key principle:** Before Tesla was a company it was three converging bets on the same insight, that 18650 lithium ion cells from laptops had crossed a threshold that made a genuinely fast, long-range electric car possible, and Musk's willingness to write large checks turned separate science projects into one venture.

*Synthesized from Chapter 7 of Elon Musk: Tesla, SpaceX, and the Quest for a Fantastic Future by Ashlee Vance.*

## Key lessons

- JB Straubel arrived at the idea through a lifetime of hands-on tinkering, a home chemistry lab that scarred his cheek, rebuilding a dump-salvaged golf cart's motor at thirteen, and a self-designed Stanford major in "energy systems and engineering" after finding pure physics too theoretical. He converted a $1,600 "piece of shit Porsche" into a record-setting EV that ran a quarter mile in 17.28 seconds, which taught him the electronics were ready but the batteries were the bottleneck.
- The battery insight itself came from a late-night crash pad session: Stanford solar-car teammates, freshly finished with a 2,300-mile race, camped on Straubel's floor and did back-of-envelope math showing that stringing together ten thousand laptop-style 18650 cells could push a car nearly a thousand miles. Straubel spent months afterward trying to sell that idea to investors and getting universally rejected.
- Straubel met Musk in fall 2003 at a lunch that was actually supposed to pitch Musk on an electric plane; when that failed to land, Straubel mentioned his electric car side project instead and Musk, who had already been mulling EVs using ultracapacitors, offered $10,000 of the $100,000 Straubel was raising on the spot.
- Straubel then connected Musk to AC Propulsion's tzero, a kit car with a fiberglass body that hit zero to 60 in 4.9 seconds; Musk fell for it and tried for months to fund AC Propulsion into a real commercial venture, but the company's founders (whom Straubel liked but called "hopeless at business") kept refusing and pushing an uninspiring alternative called the eBox instead.
- In parallel, Martin Eberhard and Marc Tarpenning, who had sold their e-reader company NuvoMedia (the Rocket eBook) to Gemstar for $187 million in 2000, were independently modeling an electric car on a spreadsheet. Eberhard had personally put $500,000 into AC Propulsion to build himself a lithium-ion version of one of their cars, and the two men's financial research found EV1 lessors averaged $200,000 in income, pointing them toward a high-end sports car rather than an SUV or delivery truck.
- Eberhard and Tarpenning incorporated Tesla Motors on July 1, 2003 (the name chosen partly because it sounded cool), rented a three-desk office in Menlo Park, and were joined by engineer Ian Wright as employee three. Their plan borrowed AC Propulsion's tzero powertrain concept and used a Lotus Elise chassis as the body, reasoning that big automakers already outsource nearly everything except final assembly, marketing, and combustion R&D.
- Eberhard and Wright pitched Musk directly after hearing through AC Propulsion's Tom Gage that Musk wanted to fund something in the space; after a weekend of Tarpenning fielding Musk's financial-model questions by phone, Musk said "Okay, I'm in" and put in $6.5 million, becoming Tesla's largest shareholder and chairman. Straubel joined shortly after, hired in May 2004 at $95,000 a year once he realized the Menlo Park team was building the same battery pack he'd been designing down the street.
- Early engineering moved fast: the team stretched a yellow Lotus Elise chassis five inches, craned in a battery pack built from bricks of supergrued-together cells, and had a drivable prototype by January 27, 2005, just over four months after starting, built by eighteen people. On July 4, 2005, they deliberately set off a bundle of twenty batteries at Eberhard's house to test fire risk; it "went up like a cluster of bottle rockets," which sobered the team into forming a six-person task force and running blast tests behind a fire department substation, ultimately producing arrangement techniques that became a real Tesla technical advantage.
- Musk pushed for a from-scratch body rather than a lightly modified Elise (carbon fiber instead of fiberglass, taller doors, more room), which led to a quarter-scale model in January 2005, a full-scale model in April, an "aero buck" tested at Burning Man, and a black pencils-down prototype (EP1) by May 2006 with the company at a hundred employees; a further $12 million from Musk and a $40 million round including Draper Fisher Jurvetson, J.P. Morgan, Larry Page, and Sergey Brin followed.
- Tesla went public with the Roadster at a July 2006 Santa Clara event, unveiling a red prototype (EP2) alongside the black one, quoting a $90,000 price and 250-mile range, with Musk declaring "until today, all electric cars have sucked." Arnold Schwarzenegger and Michael Eisner attended; only Straubel and a couple of trusted engineers could actually drive the fragile cars, swapped every five minutes to avoid overheating. A New York Times mini-profile followed that infuriated Musk by barely mentioning him, and the next month's Pebble Beach Concours d'Elegance saw people writing $100,000 pre-order checks on the spot, including entrepreneurs who joined a $100,000 "club" membership just to guarantee a car.
