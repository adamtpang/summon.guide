# Appendix 3: The Going Public Memo

> **Key principle:** Musk treats "should SpaceX go public" as a first-principles question subordinate to the company's actual goal, Mars, and concludes that public-company incentives (quarterly judgment, insider-trading restrictions, class-action exposure) would divert energy from execution, so he proposes a private structure that pays employees like public stock would without inviting the scrutiny.

*Synthesized from Appendix 3 of Elon Musk: Tesla, SpaceX, and the Quest for a Fantastic Future by Ashlee Vance.*

## Key lessons

- The appendix reproduces Musk's June 7, 2013 email to All@spacex.com titled "Going Public," written after some employees had reportedly started assuming an IPO was desirable; Musk opens by stating his central test explicitly: if being public reduces the odds of establishing life on Mars, SpaceX should not go public until Mars is secure.
- He draws a sharp distinction from his other two companies: Tesla and SolarCity went public because they had no real choice, their private capital structures had become unwieldy and they needed large equity raises, and in SolarCity's case its lenders required the added public-company scrutiny (Sarbanes-Oxley reporting) as a condition of the debt needed to fund solar leases.
- He directly rebuts the employee assumption that public stock lets savvy insiders time their exits for maximum profit, arguing that selling on inside knowledge is illegal, that sale windows are narrow and rare, and that he can point to a real case: a Tesla employee and an investor both went through a grand jury investigation over stock sales despite acting properly by the letter and spirit of the law.
- He warns that public status invites class-action lawsuits almost automatically, citing Tesla's own live lawsuit at the time, triggered by a stock-price drop from the prior year even though the current share price was strong, as proof that legal exposure doesn't require the company to actually be underperforming.
- He undercuts the assumption that SpaceX's public debut would ride the same wave as Tesla's and SolarCity's strong first-quarter results, revealing that SpaceX had in fact just had "an awful first quarter" financially, and argues that as a public company it would have been punished hard for that, plus for any rocket or spacecraft anomaly (citing the flight 4 engine failure and flight 5 Dragon prevalve issue) and for schedule slips on its primary revenue driver, the delayed V1.1 vehicle.
- He backs the danger of quarterly punishment with a concrete precedent: Tesla's Q4 vehicle production had been only three weeks behind schedule, and the market reaction was, in his word, brutal, which he uses to argue SpaceX's much longer and riskier development timelines would fare far worse under the same scrutiny.
- His proposed alternative is to structure private financing rounds to approximate the financial upside of being public, pegging the share price to what SpaceX would roughly be worth if traded publicly (excluding market over- or under-reaction), while aiming for a steady upward trend that never dips below the previous round's price, so investors and employees get the same effective payoff as a public shareholder without the "manic-depressive" stock swings or the reporting burden.
- He grounds the pitch in hard numbers: annual expenses of roughly $800-900 million, about $60 million in revenue per Falcon 9 flight (double for a Falcon Heavy or Dragon flight), a need for roughly twelve flights a year (four of them Dragon or Heavy) just to hit 10% profitability, and a personal projection that the long-term share price could exceed $100 if SpaceX executes well on Falcon 9 and Dragon and sustains an average launch cadence of at least once a month.
