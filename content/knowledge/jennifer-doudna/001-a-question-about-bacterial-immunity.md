## Key lessons

- She opens the lecture with origins: roughly two decades of microbiology suggesting bacteria have an adaptive immune system, CRISPR, that records pieces of viral DNA in the genome and uses them to recognize later infections.
- She frames the 2011 collaboration with Emmanuelle Charpentier and her student Krzysztof Chylinski around one open question in the field: what is the function of Cas9, a protein implicated in protecting Streptococcus pyogenes from viruses.
- She credits Martin Jinek, then a postdoc in her lab, and Chylinski with the biochemical experiments showing that Cas9 is guided by two RNAs (CRISPR RNA and tracrRNA) to a matching DNA sequence, which it then cuts.
- She describes the turn from understanding to engineering: once the natural dual RNA system was understood, the two RNAs could be fused into a single guide RNA that programs Cas9 to cut a chosen sequence. She calls the day of Jinek's test-tube result the "pure joy of discovery".
- She explains why a programmable cut matters outside bacteria: plant, animal and human cells repair double-strand breaks, and that repair is the opening to change a genome at a precise place. She notes the repair research had been done by others over the previous decades.
- She separates somatic editing, which affects one individual, from germline editing, which can be inherited, and says heritable editing in humans raises large ethical and societal issues and that responsible use has become an active part of her own work.
- She points to somatic uses as the near-term opportunity, citing a patient treated for sickle cell disease, and to a joint National Academies and Royal Society report that sets criteria for any future human germline use.
- She names lab members throughout (Blake Wiedenheft, Rachel Haurwitz, Fuguo Jiang, Alexandra East-Seletsky, Mitch O'Connell, Janice Chen) and collaborator Jillian Banfield, and describes later findings such as Cas13 and Cas12 detection chemistry used for HPV and SARS-CoV-2 testing.
- She closes by saying delivery and control are the key open problems for genome editing, and that fundamental research will continue to move the field forward as it has before.
