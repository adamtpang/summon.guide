## Key lessons

- The interview took place the day after the US Patent Office ruled against the University of California in its long dispute with the Broad Institute of MIT and Harvard over CRISPR's key uses. The interviewer describes the Broad as the winner of those commercial rights.
- Her response is restrained: she says the decision does not make sense to her and will be appealed, points to the university's issued and pending US patents and its European patents, and says she is carrying on with her research.
- She declines to speculate about other parties' motives in the dispute, and says that within the scientific community she does not think there is much question about what happened.
- She says her best days are still spent looking at data over a student's shoulder, but that she has come to believe CRISPR's impact in the next decade depends on putting real capital behind the right teams. This is her stated reason for advising an investment firm.
- She sees promise in applying machine learning to the large data sets CRISPR experiments generate, in order to understand how sets of genes function together.
- She is disappointed but not shocked that very little venture money goes to women-led startups, says women are often less connected in biotech and venture circles, and describes an institute program to give women founders connections and training in pitching and leading teams. Former students Rachel Haurwitz and Janice Chen are mentioned as founders.
- She expects agriculture and climate uses, such as drought-tolerant rice and soil microbes that capture carbon, may end up with broader everyday impact than medical uses.
- On heritable editing she says her view has been consistent: not clinically now, because the technology is not vetted in embryos, no clear medical need has been shown, and society has not had the chance to weigh it.
- She does not rule out future protective uses, saying that if editing could one day reduce susceptibility to diseases like Alzheimer's or cardiovascular disease, that would have to be considered. She distinguishes this from enhancement.
