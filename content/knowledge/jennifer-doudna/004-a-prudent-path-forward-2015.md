## Key lessons

- The article reports on a January 2015 meeting in Napa, California, sponsored by the Innovative Genomics Initiative, where scientists, legal scholars and bioethicists discussed the implications of genome engineering. Doudna is a co-author alongside David Baltimore and Paul Berg, among others.
- The authors say CRISPR-Cas9 is simple and inexpensive enough that any researcher with molecular biology training can modify genomes, and that the same ease applies to germline changes, which had already been shown in mice and monkeys.
- They present the core worry as heritable change made with incomplete knowledge of human genetics, gene and environment interactions, and disease pathways, so even correcting a known disease mutation could have unintended consequences.
- They acknowledge public unease about a slide from curing disease toward less compelling or troubling uses, and say regulatory review focused on safety and efficacy does not cover the wider value judgments involved.
- Their first recommendation is to strongly discourage any clinical attempt at human germline modification, including in countries with lax rules, while societal, environmental and ethical implications are discussed. The text does not use the word moratorium.
- They also recommend forums where scientists and bioethicists can inform the public, transparent research on the efficacy and specificity of CRISPR-Cas9 in relevant model systems, and a globally representative meeting to consider policy.
- They say off-target changes and unintended on-target effects must be thoroughly studied, with standardized benchmarks, before any human germline attempt is sanctioned for clinical testing, "if ever".
- They draw on the recombinant DNA debates of the 1970s for the closing lesson: public trust in science depends on continuing transparency and open discussion.
- The acknowledgments disclose financial interests, including that Doudna co-founded Caribou Biosciences and Editas Medicine and is an inventor on University of California patent filings on CRISPR-Cas9 genome engineering.
