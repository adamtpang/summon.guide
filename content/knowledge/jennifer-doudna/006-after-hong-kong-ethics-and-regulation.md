## Key lessons

- She says she felt stunned and sickened on hearing He Jiankui present the editing of twin girls' embryos. She had thought someone might cross that line, but not so soon, and not disclosed only after the births.
- She lists the continuing fallout as the health and future of the children, the fate of the scientist, and public perception of CRISPR, and says she is encouraged by the broad global rejection of what was done.
- She argues the main obstacle to embryo editing is ethical more than scientific: what consent means for future generations, where treatment ends and enhancement begins, and whether removing a condition stigmatizes people who live with it.
- She says the 2015 Science perspective was in effect a moratorium without the word, that it was ignored, and that she now thinks moratoria are not strong enough. She wants carefully made regulation that does not choke the technology.
- Asked whether colleagues should have raised alarms earlier, she says confidentiality norms put them in a hard position, and proposes an anonymous whistleblower line to a body such as the WHO and a statement that nondisclosure agreements do not bind in cases of severe ethical concern.
- She concedes the episode dented public confidence that scientists can deploy CRISPR safely, and says self-governance "failed" in this case while most researchers act ethically.
- She welcomes WHO steps in 2019 (a request to halt clinical germline editing and a planned registry) because they make it hard for anyone to claim ignorance of the guidelines, and calls for an enforceable framework.
- She says individual scientists should make time for conversations with the public despite busy schedules, and treats ethical concerns as among the most important considerations for every researcher.
- She ends by noting that taxpayer and nonprofit funded curiosity-driven research produced CRISPR-Cas9 and the companies that followed, and that responsible use protects the technology's long-term viability.
