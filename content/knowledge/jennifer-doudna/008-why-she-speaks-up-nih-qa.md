## Key lessons

- She describes CRISPR as a way to change DNA precisely, from a single letter to whole genes, and stresses that it evolved long ago as a bacterial defense against viruses.
- She says the question for therapies is shifting from whether CRISPR can work to how to make it affordable and accessible, in the United States and globally.
- Her example is sickle cell disease: she cites a patient, Victoria Gray, as effectively cured, but notes the treatment currently requires editing cells outside the body and a bone marrow transplant, which is expensive and demanding.
- She explains that it was clear early on that CRISPR worked in many cell types including embryos, and that edits to embryos, sperm or eggs differ from treating a patient's own cells because they can be inherited.
- She says she realized quickly that, as someone involved in the original work, she had to explain the technology to people who are not scientists and help the international scientific community reach shared criteria for that special use.
- She says she has been pleased with the direction of the previous five years, describing a shared sense among scientists that the tool calls for restraint and stewardship.
- She reports that over 26 years of running an academic lab, the more diverse her group has been, the better its science, and hopes the prize signals to women and girls that they are welcome in STEM fields.
- Her advice to young people is to go ahead and not let anyone say they cannot do it. She says the successful scientists she knows "trusted their inner voice".
