## Key lessons

- She writes that growing up in Hilo, Hawaii she felt like an outsider at school, and that this pushed her toward books, toward exploring the island's natural diversity, and later toward taking risks as a scientist.
- She recalls reading The Double Helix in sixth grade, a book her father left for her, and being struck by collaborative puzzle solving and by Rosalind Franklin's role, which showed her that a woman could be a great scientist.
- She reports that a high school guidance counselor discouraged her from chemistry and from science altogether, and that she went ahead anyway, studying chemistry at Pomona College from 1981.
- She describes early lab summers (a papaya fungus project with Don Hemmes in Hilo, then bacterial signaling with Sharon Panasenko) as her first experience of the thrill of discovery.
- She calls choosing RNA with Jack Szostak at Harvard a risky move at a time when attention and funding were going to the Human Genome Project. Their 1989 Nature paper showed an RNA could help copy RNA.
- She recounts moving to Tom Cech's lab in 1991 to solve RNA structures, struggling with crystals that X-rays destroyed, and learning a cryo-cooling method from Thomas and Joan Steitz. With Jamie Cate she published the first detailed structure of a large ribozyme domain in Science in 1996.
- She says CRISPR reached her through a call from Berkeley microbiologist Jillian Banfield, who found her lab online because of its RNA interference work. They talked over tea about whether CRISPR was a bacterial version of RNA interference, at a time when no one had run the experiments.
- She lays out the steps that followed: a first CRISPR conference in Berkeley in 2008, lab work on Cas1 and Cas6f, meeting Charpentier in Puerto Rico in 2011, and the 2012 Science paper on a programmable RNA-guided DNA-cutting enzyme.
- She describes founding the Innovative Genomics Institute in 2014 and co-founding several companies to move CRISPR toward the clinic, and says the technology's dual-use potential led her to start public discussion of ethics, including a 2015 Napa meeting modeled on Asilomar.
