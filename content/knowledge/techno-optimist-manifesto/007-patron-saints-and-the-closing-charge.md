# Patron Saints and the Closing Charge

> **Key principle:** Rather than end on argument, the manifesto ends on lineage and slogan. It lists dozens of named intellectual influences, spanning classical and modern economists, technologists, and cultural writers, an unusually explicit move for this kind of document, then closes with a short, quotable statement of the choice it believes is in front of the reader, and a standard disclaimer that the views expressed are personal, not an official position of Andreessen's firm.

*Synthesized from the closing "patron saints" list and final call to action of "The Techno-Optimist Manifesto" by Marc Andreessen, published at a16z.com, October 2023.*

## Key lessons

- The economists and thinkers named span multiple, sometimes rival traditions rather than a single school: classical figures like Adam Smith and David Ricardo, market-liberal economists Milton Friedman, Friedrich Hayek, and Ludwig von Mises, growth theorists Paul Romer and William Nordhaus, and contemporary abundance-and-progress writers such as Thomas Sowell, Marian Tupy, Julian Simon, and Joel Mokyr.
- Technologists and futurists named include Ray Kurzweil, Doug Engelbart, Richard Feynman, John von Neumann, Stephen Wolfram, Kevin Kelly, and George Gilder, a group spanning computing pioneers, physicists, and technology writers, chosen to root the manifesto's optimism in people who actually built or theorized about transformative technology rather than only economists who theorized about markets.
- Cultural and historical figures named include Friedrich Nietzsche, Buckminster Fuller, Andy Warhol, Ada Lovelace, and Bertrand Russell, alongside less widely known writers on markets, individualism, and progress such as Isabel Paterson, Rose Wilder Lane, Virginia Postrel, Deirdre McCloskey, and Matt Ridley, an eclectic list that mixes canonical names with figures from a specific libertarian and pro-market intellectual tradition.
- The list's sheer range, philosophers, economists, physicists, novelists, is itself a claim: by naming this many disparate influences together, the manifesto argues techno-optimism isn't a narrow or new ideology invented for the occasion, but a synthesis of a long-running current across multiple disciplines and centuries.
- The closing lines set up a direct binary choice for the reader between two imagined futures, one defined by fear, guilt, and resentment, the other by ambition, abundance, and adventure, presenting techno-optimism as the deliberate choice of the second over the first rather than a neutral forecast of which one will happen.
- He invokes physicist and philosopher David Deutsch's argument that an open future creates a duty toward optimism, using it as the manifesto's final piece of intellectual support immediately before the closing slogan, "it's time to be a Techno-Optimist, it's time to build," which directly echoes the title of Andreessen's earlier essay "It's Time to Build."
- The manifesto ends with a standard disclaimer noting the views are Andreessen's own and other named a16z contributors', not an official position of the firm, alongside typical investment disclaimers, a housekeeping note that nonetheless matters for accurately citing the piece as a personal statement rather than a corporate one.
