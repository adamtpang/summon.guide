# The Pandemic Proved We Stopped Building

> **Key principle:** Written in the early weeks of the pandemic, the essay opens with a blunt catalogue of what the United States and its peers didn't have on hand, and asks why not, given none of it required new science. Andreessen's answer is that the shortage wasn't of money, capitalism, or technical skill, it was of will, a society had simply chosen, over decades, not to build and maintain the capacity these emergencies required.

*Synthesized from the opening section of "It's Time to Build" by Marc Andreessen, published at a16z.com, April 2020.*

## Key lessons

- The essay opens with an inventory of what was missing in the pandemic's first weeks: enough coronavirus tests, cotton swabs and reagents to run them, ventilators, negative-pressure isolation rooms, ICU beds, surgical masks, eye shields, and medical gowns, severe enough that New York City reportedly asked for rain ponchos as improvised protective equipment.
- Beyond physical supplies, he notes the absence of ready therapies or vaccines despite years of prior warnings from public health experts about the risk of a coronavirus jumping from an animal reservoir into the human population, the warning existed, the built response to it did not.
- He points to a specific, almost absurd gap: the federal government collects taxes from every working American every year but had built no corresponding system to rapidly get emergency money to laid-off workers and small businesses in a crisis, the state's revenue-collection machinery was mature while its distribution machinery was not.
- His central move is to reject the idea that any of this reflects a lack of underlying capability: none of these items, tests, swabs, ventilators, masks, or a payment system, required breakthrough science to produce, they required someone to have built the manufacturing capacity or the software system in advance, and nobody had.
- He is explicit that the failure is not partisan and not uniquely American: essentially every Western country, regardless of which party held power, stumbled on broadly the same shortages, which he treats as evidence the real cause sits one level up, in a shared civilizational complacency rather than in any single country's politics.
- The rhetorical function of this opening is to convert a specific, temporary emergency into a permanent diagnostic: if the crisis revealed the problem, the problem itself, an unwillingness to build capacity before it's desperately needed, long predates and will long outlast the crisis that exposed it.
- This section sets up the essay's actual thesis, which is not really about pandemic preparedness specifically, it uses the pandemic as the most vivid, hardest-to-dismiss recent evidence for a broader claim about housing, education, manufacturing, and transportation that the rest of the essay goes on to make.
