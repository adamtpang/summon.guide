# A Build Agenda for Left and Right

> **Key principle:** Having named will, not money or capability, as the real obstacle, Andreessen turns the essay into a direct address to both major political camps, arguing each has a version of the problem specific to its own instincts, and that a genuine build agenda requires each side to fix its own failure mode rather than waiting for the other to change first.

*Synthesized from the political-prescription section of "It's Time to Build" by Marc Andreessen, published at a16z.com, April 2020.*

## Key lessons

- To the right: he argues conservatives are instinctively pro-production but have let that instinct get captured by forces that actually oppose real market competition, crony capitalism, regulatory capture that protects incumbents, oligopolies that no longer need to innovate, offshoring that trades domestic capacity for short-term cost savings, and stock buybacks favored over investment in new products and factories, and he calls on the right to aggressively back new industries, new factories, and technological leaps instead of defending whichever large company already has political access.
- To the left: he challenges progressives who favor public-sector solutions to demonstrate results rather than good intentions, explicitly invoking Milton Friedman's framing that judging policy by intentions rather than outcomes is a category error, and asks the left to stop merely defending existing public institutions and instead commit to building public hospitals, public schools, public transit, and public housing that are actually better than what exists today.
- He gives concrete examples of what a serious left-coded build agenda would look like in practice: modernizing the VA hospital system rather than only debating its funding, scaling a prestigious university like Harvard to serve 100,000 or even a million students a year instead of guarding scarcity as a form of prestige, and building large numbers of new nuclear reactors to replace carbon-based electricity generation.
- The nuclear example is deliberately chosen to cut across the usual political alignment: it is a large, public-good, infrastructure-scale build that should appeal to a left concerned with climate and a right concerned with energy independence and industrial capacity, and Andreessen uses it to argue the "build more" agenda is not itself a partisan position even though the obstacles to it often are.
- The section's rhetorical structure, one paragraph indicting the right's failure mode, one paragraph indicting the left's, is a deliberate refusal to let the essay be read as a partisan document, the earlier claim that every Western country and every party failed on pandemic preparedness only holds together if he applies the same standard to both American political coalitions here.
- He is not asking either side to abandon its values, he is asking the right to apply its stated belief in competition against its own crony beneficiaries, and asking the left to apply its stated belief in public solutions by actually delivering superior public outcomes rather than only protecting existing public budgets and staff.
- This section is the essay's most concrete policy content, everywhere else the argument stays at the level of will and culture, here it names specific institutions, the VA, Harvard, the nuclear fleet, that a reader could actually watch for progress or failure on.
