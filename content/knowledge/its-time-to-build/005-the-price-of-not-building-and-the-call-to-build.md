# The Price of Not Building and the Call to Build

> **Key principle:** The essay's final movement ties the whole argument to something every reader feels directly: the price of anything produced at scale keeps falling while the price of anything artificially undersupplied keeps climbing, and the difference between those two price curves is, in his telling, the difference between an accessible American dream and an inaccessible one. He closes not with a policy list but with a question meant to be asked of everyone in a position of leadership or talent: what are you building?

*Synthesized from the closing economic argument and call-to-action of "It's Time to Build" by Marc Andreessen, published at a16z.com, April 2020.*

## Key lessons

- He draws a direct price contrast: products manufactured at real scale, computers and televisions being his examples, get cheaper year after year, while housing, education, and healthcare, the categories he has just spent the essay arguing are deliberately undersupplied, keep getting more expensive, and treats this divergence as the clearest possible evidence that scarcity in the second group is a choice, not a law of economics.
- He ties this price divergence directly to the American dream, arguing that affordable homeownership and the ability to provide for a family depend on breaking that upward price curve, and that breaking it requires building enough supply to bend the curve back down, not further subsidizing demand into an artificially constrained market.
- Building, in his framing, is presented as the actual mechanism for restoring opportunity broadly, not a nice-to-have alongside other policy tools, if the price curves for housing, education, and healthcare are the barrier to opportunity, building more of each is the only lever that addresses the cause rather than the symptom.
- The closing call to action is addressed broadly, to political leaders, CEOs, entrepreneurs, investors, and culture generally, demanding a higher level of ambition and performance from each, rather than being narrowly aimed at any one group such as founders or policymakers alone.
- He proposes a specific, repeatable question as the essay's practical takeaway: everyone should ask others, and ask themselves, "what are you building?", and frames any answer that isn't building something directly, enabling someone else's building, teaching people to build, or materially supporting builders as a sign of unmet potential.
- The essay ends by connecting the present obligation to build to inherited history, roads, railways, farms, factories, computers, and smartphones were all built by people who came before, and honoring that inheritance, in his framing, requires the current generation to keep building rather than only maintaining or debating what already exists.
- He closes by inviting disagreement on the specifics while insisting on the verb: critics of any particular item in his list are explicitly invited to propose their own building agenda, but the essay's final line collapses the entire argument into a single imperative, build, making clear the point of the piece was never the specific examples, it was the instruction.
