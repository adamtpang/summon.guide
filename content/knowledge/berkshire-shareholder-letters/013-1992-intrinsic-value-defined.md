# 1992: Intrinsic Value Defined

> **Key principle:** Buffett rejects the Wall Street habit of treating "value" and "growth" investing as opposing schools, arguing growth is simply one variable, sometimes positive, sometimes negative, inside a single valuation calculation: the discounted value of the cash a business can distribute to owners over its remaining life.

*Synthesized from Berkshire Hathaway's 1992 Chairman's Letter to shareholders, by Warren E. Buffett.*

## Key lessons

- Buffett calls the industry split between "value" and "growth" investing "fuzzy thinking," admitting he engaged in it himself years earlier, and states that growth "is always a component in the calculation of value," a variable whose importance ranges from negligible to enormous and can cut either way.
- He goes further and calls the term "value investing" itself redundant: investing, by definition, is seeking value at least sufficient to justify the price paid; consciously overpaying in hopes of reselling higher is "speculation," which he notes is "neither illegal, immoral nor... financially fattening."
- He debunks the common shorthand for "value" stocks, low price-to-book, low P/E, high dividend yield, as neither necessary nor sufficient: a stock with the opposite characteristics, high P/E, high price-to-book, low yield, can still be a genuine value purchase, and vice versa.
- Growth itself "tells us little about value" on its own: he cites the U.S. airline industry, where decades of growth financed at poor returns destroyed shareholder capital, joking it would have been better for investors "if Orville had failed to get off the ground at Kitty Hawk." Growth only helps an investor when a business can invest incremental capital at returns exceeding a dollar of value per dollar deployed.
- Buffett states the formal definition, condensed from John Burr Williams's The Theory of Investment Value: the value of any stock, bond, or business is the discounted value of the cash inflows and outflows expected to occur over the remaining life of the asset. He notes this formula is identical for stocks and bonds, the key difference being that a bond's future "coupons" are contractually fixed while an equity investor must estimate them, and that management quality can materially change an equity's coupons in a way it rarely can a bond's.
- He describes the best business to own as one that can employ large amounts of incremental capital at very high rates of return over an extended period, and the worst as one that must keep consuming ever more capital at low returns; he notes truly high-return businesses are hard to find precisely because they usually need little capital, meaning their owners benefit most from dividends or buybacks.
- To manage the difficulty of estimating future cash flows, Buffett names Berkshire's two safeguards: sticking to businesses simple and stable enough to understand ("what counts for most people in investing is not how much they know, but rather how realistically they define what they don't know"), and insisting on Ben Graham's "margin of safety" so that the purchase price sits meaningfully below calculated value.
