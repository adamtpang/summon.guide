# 1977-1978: The Owner-Oriented View

> **Key principle:** In his first letters as a public-company chairman, Buffett rejects earnings-per-share as the measure of managerial success and replaces it with return on beginning equity capital, then extends the same discipline to the stock portfolio: a share of Capital Cities or The Washington Post is evaluated exactly as if Berkshire were buying the whole business, at a price, from honest and competent people, for the long run.

*Synthesized from Berkshire Hathaway's 1977 and 1978 Chairman's Letters to shareholders, by Warren E. Buffett.*

## Key lessons

**1977**

- Buffett opens by dismissing rising earnings-per-share as a meaningful scorecard: a business that grows equity 10% and earnings 5% has actually gotten worse, "even a totally dormant savings account" produces rising earnings through compounding. He substitutes return on beginning equity capital (19% in 1977) as the real test.
- Berkshire's insurance operations, built from an $8.6 million purchase of National Indemnity in 1967 with $22 million of premium volume, had grown to $151 million of premium volume by 1977, a nearly 600% increase achieved without issuing a single new share, through new subsidiaries (Cornhusker, Lakeland, Texas United, Kansas Fire and Casualty) and cash purchases (Home and Automobile, Cypress).
- The textile business, by contrast, "again had a very poor year," and Buffett lays out plainly why Berkshire still keeps it: the mills are among the largest employers in New Bedford and Manchester, management (Ken Chace) had been straightforward and energetic, labor had cooperated, and modest profits still seemed achievable, even though the industry could not compete with businesses enjoying "tailwinds rather than headwinds."
- Buffett states the four-part test Berkshire uses to buy marketable equities exactly as it would evaluate an acquisition of an entire business: (1) a business it can understand, (2) with favorable long-term prospects, (3) run by honest and competent people, (4) available at an attractive price. He argues pro-rata slices of outstanding businesses often sell at large discounts to what the same business would cost in a negotiated buyout.
- The Capital Cities Communications stake ($10.9 million invested, with earnings attributable to Berkshire of about $1.3 million but only a $40,000 dividend reflected in reported earnings) is his worked example: buying the properties outright would have cost roughly twice as much as buying the stock, so "we can obtain a better management result through non-control than control."
- A digression on Berkshire's own textile predecessor firms drives the point home: in 1948 Berkshire Fine Spinning and Hathaway Manufacturing together earned almost $18 million (more than Safeway or 3M that year), yet the following decade of $595 million in aggregate sales produced a $10 million aggregate loss, "so much for single year snapshots as adequate portrayals of a business."

**1978**

- The merger with Diversified Retailing Company pushed Berkshire's stake in Blue Chip Stamps to about 58%, forcing full consolidation of Blue Chip's textiles, insurance, candy, newspaper, and trading-stamp businesses onto Berkshire's balance sheet, a presentation Buffett says obscures more than it illuminates and that management "never" uses internally.
- 1978 operating earnings were 19.4% of beginning equity, just short of the 1972 record; Buffett again warns that capital gains, while excluded from single-year performance measurement, are a critical part of the long-term record, and that Berkshire's 25% compounded three-year equity growth was not sustainable as the insurance cycle turned down for 1979.
- He introduces a detailed "Sources of Earnings" table breaking out pre-tax and after-tax contribution by entity (insurance underwriting, net investment income, textiles, See's Candies, Illinois National Bank, Blue Chip parent, and others), a format meant to let shareholders see the business the way management does rather than through consolidated GAAP.
- The textile section states the mechanism plainly: capital-intensive, undifferentiated-product industries earn inadequate returns whenever excess capacity exists, because "prices tend to reflect direct operating costs rather than capital employed" — a condition Buffett expects to persist in textiles indefinitely.
- Insurance underwriting had its best year yet under Phil Liesche (roughly $11 million of underwriting profit on about $90 million of earned premiums), but Buffett flags that margins are cyclical and warns 1979 combined ratios will worsen as claims costs (auto repair, medical care) were rising faster than rates.
- The SAFECO stake becomes his second worked example of buying earning power below its true cost: Berkshire bought "the best company in the business" at under book value, while corporate acquirers routinely pay more than 100 cents on the dollar for mediocre companies; SAFECO's retained earnings, though only partly reflected in Berkshire's reported operating earnings, are "just as real" to shareholders as distributed dividends.
- Buffett closes with a preview of the concept his 1980 letter will formalize: undistributed earnings of partly-owned companies are real economic earnings to Berkshire "regardless of the inclusion or non-inclusion... in our own reported operating earnings," whether Berkshire owns 100%, 50%, 20%, or a much smaller slice.
