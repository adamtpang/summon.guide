# 1989: Mistakes and the Institutional Imperative

> **Key principle:** Marking Berkshire's 25th year under his management, Buffett runs a condensed self-audit of his own worst calls, from buying control of a doomed textile mill to passing on opportunities he understood perfectly well, and uses the review to name the single force he says did the most damage: the institutional imperative, an organization's built-in tendency to resist change, spend available funds, rationalize a leader's wishes, and imitate peers regardless of merit.

*Synthesized from Berkshire Hathaway's 1989 Chairman's Letter to shareholders, by Warren E. Buffett.*

## Key lessons

- Buffett names his first mistake as buying control of Berkshire itself in 1965: he knew the textile business was unpromising but was drawn in because the price looked cheap, an approach he calls the "cigar butt" style of investing, a bargain purchase good for one last free puff but no real smoke.
- He extends the lesson through a second real example, the 1966 purchase of Baltimore department store Hochschild Kohn at a discount to book value with unrecorded real estate and inventory value: he sold it three years later for about what he paid, concluding "time is the friend of the wonderful business, the enemy of the mediocre."
- The condensed rule that follows: "it's far better to buy a wonderful company at a fair price than a fair company at a wonderful price." He credits Charlie Munger with grasping this early and calls himself the slow learner between the two.
- A related lesson on management, illustrated by the same two businesses: able managers ran both the textile mill and Hochschild Kohn, but "good jockeys will do well on good horses, but not on broken-down nags," since a strong reputation for management cannot outrun weak underlying economics.
- His formal naming of "the institutional imperative": the tendency of organizations, regardless of the individual rationality of their leaders, to (1) resist any change in current direction like a body under Newton's First Law, (2) let projects or acquisitions expand to absorb whatever funds are available, (3) have any leader's business craving, however foolish, quickly supported by staff-produced rate-of-return studies, and (4) mindlessly imitate the behavior of peer companies.
- He states he had no exposure to this concept in business school and assumed decent, experienced managers would automatically act rationally; learning otherwise, through his own expensive mistakes, led him to try to structure Berkshire in ways that minimize the imperative's pull, and to favor investing in companies whose managements seem alert to the problem.
- Buffett also flags his quietest category of mistakes: opportunities he fully understood and was offered "on a platter" but declined to act on, which he calls "thumb-sucking," and argues cost Berkshire's shareholders more than any of his visible errors.
- On leverage, he defends Berkshire's consistently conservative financing as correct even in hindsight, despite acknowledging that higher leverage would likely have produced better returns on equity than the 23.8% actually averaged: even at 99-to-1 odds of a good outcome, a small chance of distress or disgrace is not worth trading for a larger chance of extra return.
