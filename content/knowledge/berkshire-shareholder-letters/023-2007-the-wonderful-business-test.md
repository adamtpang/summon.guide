# The Great, the Good, and the Gruesome

> **Key principle:** Buffett sorts businesses into three "savings accounts": the great one pays a rising rate of interest on capital that barely needs to grow, the good one pays an attractive rate on capital that does grow, and the gruesome one pays a poor rate while forever demanding more money just to keep running.

*Synthesized from Berkshire Hathaway's 2007 Chairman's Letter to shareholders, by Warren E. Buffett.*

## Key lessons

- Buffett lays out four criteria he and Charlie Munger use to screen businesses: one they understand, with favorable long-term economics, able and trustworthy management, and a sensible price. A truly great business also needs an enduring "moat," something like being the low-cost producer (his examples: GEICO, Costco) or owning a powerful global brand (Coca-Cola, Gillette, American Express).
- He explicitly excludes businesses that depend on a single brilliant manager from ever counting as "great," using a premier brain surgeon's medical practice as the example: the moat disappears when the surgeon leaves, whereas the Mayo Clinic's moat endures no matter who runs it.
- See's Candy is his prototype "dream business": bought in 1972 for $25 million against $30 million in sales and under $5 million in pre-tax earnings, needing only $8 million of invested capital, for a roughly 60% pre-tax return on capital. By 2007, sales had grown to $383 million and pre-tax profits to $82 million, yet the business only ever needed $40 million in capital, meaning Berkshire had to reinvest just $32 million since 1972 while collecting $1.35 billion in cumulative pre-tax earnings.
- FlightSafety is his example of a "good" but not great business: bought in 1996 with $111 million in pre-tax earnings against $570 million in fixed assets, it required $1.635 billion in subsequent capital spending (mostly flight simulators) to grow earnings by only $159 million, a decent but unspectacular return on that incremental investment.
- Airlines are his "gruesome" archetype: capital-hungry growth that has never produced a durable competitive advantage since Kitty Hawk. He recounts his own 1989 mistake buying U.S. Air preferred stock, which stopped paying dividends before he got lucky selling out in 1998 for a gain, only for the airline to go bankrupt twice in the following decade.
- He separately confesses his worst deal: the 1993 purchase of shoe company Dexter for $433 million in Berkshire stock. Because that stock has since appreciated enormously, the true cost to shareholders was not $400 million but roughly $3.5 billion in given-away value, since Dexter's "durable" advantage evaporated within a few years.
- On the emerging credit crisis, Buffett notes most of Berkshire's operating businesses did well in 2007, but the exceptions were tied to housing: brick, carpet, and real estate brokerage operations. He quotes Wells Fargo CEO John Stumpf's line that the industry "invented new ways to lose money when the old ways seemed to work just fine," and describes lenders shoveling out money on the assumption that house price appreciation would cure all problems, adding that "you only learn who has been swimming naked when the tide goes out."
- He flags that Berkshire's own housing-exposed manufacturing units (Shaw, Acme Brick, Johns Manville, MiTek) saw pre-tax earnings fall 27%, 41%, 38%, and 9% respectively in 2007, while noting Clayton Homes' $11 billion manufactured-housing loan portfolio was still performing at normal delinquency and foreclosure rates despite the broader real-estate finance turmoil.
- He warns plainly that insurance industry profit margins, including Berkshire's, will fall significantly in 2008 as prices soften even absent major catastrophes, and separately criticizes the widespread practice of assuming unrealistic 8% pension-fund investment returns, math he walks through using bond/equity mix data to show as improbable.
