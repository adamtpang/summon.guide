# Buybacks and Operating Earnings

> **Key principle:** With a new GAAP rule now forcing swings in unrealized stock gains into reported net income, Buffett tells shareholders to ignore the resulting bottom-line noise and watch operating earnings instead, while framing buybacks, not elephant hunting, as the default use of Berkshire's excess cash in a market where big acquisitions are priced too high.

*Synthesized from Berkshire Hathaway's 2018 and 2019 Chairman's Letters to shareholders, by Warren E. Buffett.*

## Key lessons

- The new GAAP rule (requiring unrealized gains and losses on Berkshire's equity portfolio to run through quarterly earnings) produced exactly the "wild and capricious" swings Buffett and Munger predicted: 2018 GAAP earnings of $4.0 billion swung between a $1.1 billion first-quarter loss and a $25.4 billion fourth-quarter loss against $12 billion and $18.5 billion profits in the second and third quarters, even as operating earnings stayed steady and beat 2016's prior high by 41%.
- The contrast sharpened in 2019: reported GAAP earnings jumped to $81.4 billion, a "crazy 1,900% increase," almost entirely because net unrealized gains on the stock portfolio rose $53.7 billion in a strong market, while operating earnings themselves were "little changed" at $24 billion. Buffett states plainly that this whipsaw between 2018 and 2019 is the argument for his rule: ignore quarterly and annual investment gains or losses, realized or not, and focus on operating earnings.
- Starting with the 2018 letter, Buffett formally retires per-share book value as Berkshire's headline performance metric after using it for nearly three decades, on the grounds that it understates the growing gap between book and intrinsic value: operating subsidiaries are carried at depreciated historical cost far below current worth, equity holdings are marked to market, and buybacks themselves push per-share intrinsic value up while pushing book value down. Going forward he says Berkshire will emphasize market price as the better long-run performance gauge.
- On buybacks, the 2019 letter states Berkshire's rule in one sentence: it will repurchase stock only when Buffett and Munger believe shares sell for less than they are worth, and only when the company is left with ample cash afterward. Because intrinsic-value estimates are imprecise, he says there is no urgency to buy "an estimated $1 of value for a very real 95 cents."
- Berkshire spent about $5 billion buying back roughly 1% of the company's own shares in 2019, after having no significant buyback activity in prior years described in these letters; Buffett frames the long-run goal as wanting Berkshire's share count to trend down over time, with the pace becoming more aggressive if the price-to-value discount widens, but he explicitly rules out ever propping up the stock price at any level.
- Both letters describe the same disappointing acquisition environment as the reason buybacks and marketable securities took priority over whole-company purchases: in 2018 Buffett calls prospects for deploying Berkshire's roughly $112 billion cash pile into permanent acquisitions "not good" because "prices are sky-high," even as he says the prospect of an "elephant-sized acquisition" still makes his and Munger's hearts beat faster. That backdrop drove Berkshire to buy about $43 billion of marketable equities in 2018 against only $19 billion sold.
- The 2019 letter's "Power of Retained Earnings" section (built around Edgar Lawrence Smith's 1924 book and a Keynes review of it) argues that Berkshire's 5%-to-10% non-controlling stakes generate real value shareholders never see in reported dividends: across the ten largest holdings (American Express, Apple, Bank of America, Bank of New York Mellon, Coca-Cola, Delta, JPMorgan Chase, Moody's, U.S. Bancorp, Wells Fargo), Berkshire's share of 2019 dividends was $3.8 billion versus $8.3 billion of retained earnings working inside those companies, some of which those companies use for their own buybacks.
- Buffett illustrates the buyback-driven-ownership effect with American Express: Berkshire's share count in Amex was unchanged for eight years through 2018, yet its ownership stake rose from 12.6% to 17.9% purely because Amex itself kept repurchasing shares, and Berkshire's $1.2 billion share of Amex's 2018 earnings equaled about 96% of what Berkshire originally paid for the whole stake.
- Insurance float, the funding base behind Berkshire's investment activity, grew from $122.7 billion at the end of 2018 to $129.4 billion at the end of 2019; both letters restate the long-run float table (from $39 million in 1970) and note an underwriting profit in 16 of the prior 17 years, the exception being a $3.2 billion pre-tax underwriting loss in 2017, with a $2 billion pre-tax underwriting gain recorded in 2018 and $400 million in 2019.
- Both letters carry near-identical management notes: Ajit Jain (insurance) and Greg Abel (all other operations) took charge of operations in early 2018, and Buffett credits them with making Berkshire "far better managed" than under his sole supervision; the 2019 letter also flags Berkshire Hathaway Energy's 91%-owned stake retaining $28 billion of earnings since 2000 rather than paying dividends, in service of funding wind-power self-sufficiency in Iowa.
