# 2000-2001: The Bubble Bursts, and September 11th

> **Key principle:** In 2000, Buffett explains investment value with a 2,600-year-old formula from Aesop — a bird in the hand is worth two in the bush — and uses it to define speculation as betting on what the next buyer will pay rather than what an asset will produce. In 2001, he applies a harder self-criticism to Berkshire itself: he had recognized the growing danger of catastrophic terrorism risk before September 11th, but "didn't convert thought into action."

*Synthesized from Berkshire Hathaway's 2000 and 2001 Chairman's Letters to shareholders, by Warren E. Buffett.*

## Key lessons

### 2000: the bubble validated

- **Aesop's formula for all valuation.** Buffett states that every asset's value reduces to answering three questions: how certain are you there are birds in the bush, when will they emerge, and what's the risk-free rate to discount them by. He argues this formula, unchanged since roughly 600 B.C., was never altered by the steam engine, electricity, the automobile, or the internet — "just insert the correct numbers."
- **Investment versus speculation, defined.** When a business's future cash flows can't be estimated with any useful range of confidence — as with many "new businesses and rapidly changing industries" of the era — Buffett says any capital committed to it "must be labeled speculative." Speculation itself isn't immoral, he adds, but it's a game he and Munger decline to play because "we bring nothing to the party."
- **The Cinderella metaphor.** Describing the market mania of the prior year, Buffett writes that speculators know overstaying the party will "bring on pumpkins and mice," but keep dancing anyway, planning to leave "just seconds before midnight" — a problem, he notes, because "the clocks have no hands." A December 1999 Paine Webber-Gallup survey in which investors expected 19% annual returns for the coming decade is cited as concrete evidence of how detached expectations had become.
- **Bubble companies versus wealth creation.** Buffett distinguishes real value creation (which he says did occur at many young businesses) from a "bubble market" that enabled "bubble companies" designed to extract money from investors via IPOs rather than to earn it — describing the underlying business model, at bottom, as "the old-fashioned chain letter."
- **Discipline paid off in acquisitions.** As the junk-bond market tightened and leveraged buyout financing dried up in 2000, Berkshire — which evaluates purchases on an all-equity basis — became comparatively more competitive, closing roughly $8 billion in acquisitions (CORT, U.S. Liability, Ben Bridge Jeweler, Justin Industries, Shaw Industries, Benjamin Moore, and Johns Manville) with 97% paid in cash and almost no increase in shares outstanding.

### 2001: the Noah rule

- **The mistake, named directly.** Buffett writes that Berkshire's General Re unit had, for years, priced property and casualty coverage by looking at past experience — windstorm, fire, earthquake — while ignoring the exposure to large-scale terrorism, "a relevant underwriting factor" that the industry, including Berkshire, had simply not charged for. September 11th delivered the insurance industry's largest loss in history, and pushed Berkshire's cost of float that year to 12.8%, its worst since 1984.
- **The Noah rule.** Buffett's own words: "I violated the Noah rule: Predicting rain doesn't count; building arks does." He states he had recognized the danger of underpriced terrorism risk before the attacks but failed to act on that recognition, leaving Berkshire, and General Re in particular, exposed to a "dangerous level of risk."
- **Three underwriting principles, one violated.** Buffett restates Berkshire's underwriting discipline as three rules: accept only risks you can properly evaluate and price for profit; avoid any aggregation of losses from a single event that could threaten solvency; and avoid moral risk by not doing business with bad actors regardless of price. He states plainly that General Re broke the first two rules in the run-up to September 11th, while Ajit Jain's reinsurance operation, by contrast, had never broken any of them in the fifteen years Buffett had reviewed its policies.
- **Berkshire's tolerance for large, priced losses.** Buffett distinguishes between losses Berkshire accepts knowingly — it is willing to lose $2 billion to $2.5 billion in a single event, as it did on September 11th, if properly compensated for the risk — and the September 11th loss specifically, which it was not compensated for, since the exposure had gone unpriced.
- **Post-9/11 underwriting response.** Ajit Jain's unit moved quickly afterward to write large, deliberately non-correlated terrorism-inclusive policies (on international airlines, a North Sea oil platform, the Sears Tower, and major sporting events), while explicitly avoiding writing many separate policies across a single metropolis that could aggregate into an uncapped loss from one event.
