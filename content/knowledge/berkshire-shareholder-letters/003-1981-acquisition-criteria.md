# 1981: Toads, Princesses, and the Discipline of Not Overpaying

> **Key principle:** Most premium-priced corporate takeovers are driven by the acquiring management's "animal spirits," its desire to rank higher on size-based league tables, or its unfounded confidence that a managerial "kiss" will turn a mediocre business into a prince; Berkshire instead measures every acquisition, controlling or fractional, against the plain test of real economic benefit per dollar paid.

*Synthesized from Berkshire Hathaway's 1981 Chairman's Letter to shareholders, by Warren E. Buffett.*

## Key lessons

- Operating earnings fell to 15.2% of beginning equity capital (from 17.8% in 1980), and Buffett extends the "non-controlled ownership earnings" concept introduced the year before: Berkshire's share of undistributed earnings from GEICO, General Foods, R.J. Reynolds, and The Washington Post alone was projected to exceed $35 million in 1982, none of it appearing in reported operating earnings.
- In the letter's central essay, "General Acquisition Behavior," Buffett names three usually-unspoken motives behind high-premium corporate takeovers: leaders' "animal spirits" and love of activity; the tendency of organizations to measure themselves (and pay managers) by size rather than profitability, noting most Fortune 500 executives know their rank by sales but not by profitability; and a fairy-tale belief that a management's "kiss" will transform a mediocre "toad" company into a profitable "prince."
- His logical challenge to acquirers: if a takeover requires paying 2X per share for a business investors could otherwise buy on the open market at X, the acquiring shareholders are implicitly betting the acquirer's management skill is worth the difference — "investors can always buy toads at the going price for toads," and Buffett says he has "observed many kisses but very few miracles."
- Buffett identifies two categories of acquirers with genuinely strong records: those who buy businesses naturally suited to inflation (able to raise prices without losing volume, and able to grow revenue without much new capital), and rare "managerial superstars" who can spot real value hidden in a distressed business, naming Tom Murphy of Capital Cities Communications, Henry Singleton of Teledyne, Ben Heineman of Northwest Industries, and Erwin Zaban of National Service Industries as examples.
- He is candid that Berkshire itself does not qualify as a Category 2 (turnaround-spotting) acquirer, and that its pursuit of Category 1 (inflation-favored) businesses had been "sporadic and inadequate" despite understanding the theory — invoking "the Noah principle: predicting rain doesn't count, building arks does." A major 1981 acquisition target fell through only because the final price would have left Berkshire's owners "worse off than before the purchase," which Buffett treats as a reprieve rather than a loss: "the empire would have been larger, but the citizenry would have been poorer."
- Berkshire's stated preference, going forward, is to keep finding value through open-market purchases of fractional stakes in well-run, understandable, honestly-managed businesses, because full-company purchases at sensible prices are rare, while fractional stakes at good prices are more often available; Buffett frames the test simply as whether undistributed earnings from these stakes eventually produce full value for shareholders.
- The "Equity Value-Added" section extends the inflation argument from earlier letters: he questions whether equity capital reliably earns a premium return over passive fixed-income investment once inflation and taxes are accounted for, warning that a stable price level is "capable of maintenance, but not of restoration" once lost, and that Berkshire's efforts will do "a much better job of filling your wallet than of filling your stomach."
- Book value per share had grown from $19.46 to $526.02 over Buffett's seventeen-year tenure (21.1% compounded annually), but he flags that over half of 1981's $124 million net-worth gain came from the market price movement of a single holding, GEICO, and warns that "such market variations will not always be on the pleasant side."
