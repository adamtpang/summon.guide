# 1984: Wonderful Businesses, Not Bargain Bins

> **Key principle:** Berkshire's best returns come not from finding statistically cheap, mediocre companies but from owning businesses like Nebraska Furniture Mart and See's Candies, run by people who "think like owners," that can compound capital internally at high rates for decades; Buffett pairs this with an equally firm rule that when no such opportunity exists, cash should go toward buying back undervalued stock rather than sitting idle or chasing a weaker deal.

*Synthesized from Berkshire Hathaway's 1984 Chairman's Letter to shareholders, by Warren E. Buffett.*

## Key lessons

- 1984's $152.6 million net worth gain (13.6%) is explicitly framed by Buffett as mediocre against Berkshire's 22.1% twenty-year compounded record; he warns plainly that sustaining even 15% annual growth going forward "will require a few big ideas — small ones just won't do," and that he and Charlie Munger "do not have any such ideas at present."
- Buffett devotes an extended section to Nebraska Furniture Mart and 91-year-old Rose "Mrs. B" Blumkin, whom he calls a manager who "probably makes more decisions in a day than most CEOs do in a year." NFM's single Omaha store did $115 million in sales on a gross margin barely half that of the leading national furniture chain, Levitz (about 22% vs. 44.4%), and operating expenses of only 16.5% of sales versus Levitz's 35.6% — a structural cost advantage, not a temporary bargain, built from a $500 investment by Mrs. B in 1937.
- The Berkshire-NFM deal itself illustrates what "honest and competent management" means in practice to Buffett: Berkshire paid $55 million for 90% of NFM without an audit, inventory verification, or title check, on the strength of Mrs. B's word alone — "that made for an even exchange."
- See's Candies gets its usual multi-year performance recap: revenues grew from $31.3 million in 1972 (the year of purchase) to $135.9 million in 1984, while after-tax operating profit grew from $2.1 million to $13.4 million, achieved by an "exceptional product and an exceptional manager," Chuck Huggins, in an industry where many well-known competitors were losing money or barely profitable.
- Buffett states outright how hard it had become to find new opportunities meeting Berkshire's standards: "it's been over ten years since it has been as difficult as now to find equity investments that meet both our qualitative standards and our quantitative standards of value versus price," adding that "doing nothing" is often the hardest discipline of all to maintain.
- Share repurchases get a fuller treatment than in earlier letters: when a company with a sound business sells well below intrinsic value, buying back its own stock is, in Buffett's view, the single most certain way to benefit remaining shareholders, since companies "often find it easy to get $2 of present value for $1," a ratio corporate acquisitions almost never achieve. He explicitly excludes "greenmail" repurchases — paying off a hostile "shareholder extortionist" at other owners' expense — calling the practice "odious and repugnant."
- He argues repurchases carry a second, subtler benefit beyond the arithmetic: consistently buying back undervalued stock signals a management genuinely oriented toward owners rather than empire-building, which over time causes the market to assign a higher, more rational valuation to the business — "the key word is demonstrated." GEICO, Washington Post, and General Foods, Berkshire's three largest holdings at the time, had all engaged in exactly this kind of repurchase.
- In the "Dividend Policy" section, Buffett draws a sharp line between "restricted" earnings (capital a business must reinvest just to hold its competitive position, however poor the resulting return — illustrated by Consolidated Edison reinvesting at 25 cents of market value per dollar retained under punitive regulation) and "unrestricted" earnings, which should be retained only when the company can turn each retained dollar into at least a dollar of market value, and distributed otherwise; he treats this test, not a fixed payout ratio, as the only sound basis for dividend policy.
