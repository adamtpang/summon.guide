# 2010-2011: Estimating Intrinsic Value and the Big Four

> **Key principle:** Because intrinsic value can't be pinned down precisely, Buffett and Munger track per-share book value as a conservative proxy, one that never captures the full worth of Berkshire's businesses but that tracks it closely enough, year to year, to serve as an honest yardstick. The same "buy only below value" logic that governs their stock picks governs Berkshire's own share repurchases.

*Synthesized from Berkshire Hathaway's 2010 and 2011 Chairman's Letters to shareholders, by Warren E. Buffett.*

## Key lessons

- **2010:** Buffett describes intrinsic value as resting on three pillars, only two of which can actually be measured: the market value of Berkshire's investments (stocks, bonds, cash, totaling $158 billion at yearend 2010, funded partly by $66 billion of "free" insurance float), and the earnings power of its 68 non-insurance operating businesses. The third pillar, how well future retained earnings get reinvested, is inherently subjective; he illustrates the stakes by noting a dollar entrusted to Sam Walton had a very different destiny than a dollar left with the 1960s CEOs of Sears or Montgomery Ward.
- **2010:** He explains why book value, though "understated," is used as the tracking proxy: precision on intrinsic value is impossible ("present that task to Charlie and me separately, and you will get two different answers"), but the premium of true value over book value doesn't swing wildly year to year, so book value's percentage change is a reasonable stand-in for how Berkshire is actually doing.
- **2010:** The GEICO story is offered as a case study of intrinsic value exceeding book value: Berkshire bought roughly a third of GEICO in the 1970s for a total of $46 million as it later grew to a 50% stake, then purchased the remaining half outright in 1996, a business Buffett had followed since discovering it as a 20-year-old Columbia student and going to its Washington headquarters on a Saturday to meet Lorimer "Davy" Davidson.
- **2010:** Buffett estimates Berkshire's "normal" earning power (excluding capital gains) at about $17 billion pre-tax and $12 billion after-tax, based on assumptions of a year without a mega-catastrophe and a business climate between 2010's weak conditions and the stronger years of 2005-2006; he flags the BNSF railroad purchase (using $22 billion of cash, raising share count 6%) as adding nearly 40% to that pre-tax power.
- **2011:** Buffett names the "Big Four" equity holdings directly: 13.0% of American Express, 8.8% of Coca-Cola, 5.5% of IBM (a new position that year, 63.9 million shares costing $10.9 billion), and 7.6% of Wells Fargo. He notes that if Berkshire had held all four positions for the full year, reported dividends would have been $862 million, while Berkshire's actual share of the four companies' combined earnings was far larger, about $3.3 billion, meaning roughly $2.4 billion of value went unreported on Berkshire's books simply because undistributed profits aren't booked as income.
- **2011:** He restates the book-value-as-proxy logic almost verbatim from the prior year, calling it "considerably understated" but useful because Berkshire's excess of intrinsic value over book value doesn't swing wildly from year to year, even as it grows in absolute dollar terms over time.
- **2011:** On capital allocation, Buffett gives a specific repurchase rule: in September 2011 Berkshire announced it would buy back its own shares at prices up to 110% of book value, though in practice it was only actually in the market a few days, buying $67 million of stock before the price rose past that ceiling. He sets two explicit conditions for favoring repurchases at all: the company must have ample funds beyond its operating and liquidity needs, and the stock must be selling at a material discount to a conservatively calculated intrinsic value; he adds that Berkshire won't repurchase if its cash-equivalent holdings fall below $20 billion, since unquestionable financial strength takes precedence over everything else.
- **2011:** Using IBM's own repurchase program as a worked example, Buffett shows why long-term owners should root for a stock price to stay low, not rise, while a company is actively buying back shares: modeling IBM spending roughly $50 billion on repurchases over five years, he calculates that an average price of $200 (versus $300) leaves Berkshire owning about 7% of IBM instead of 6.5%, worth an estimated $1.5 billion more to Berkshire if IBM's fifth-year earnings reach $20 billion, because cheaper repurchases retire more shares per dollar spent.
