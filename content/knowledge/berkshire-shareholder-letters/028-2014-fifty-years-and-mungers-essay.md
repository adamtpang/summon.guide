# The 50-Year Letter: Buffett's Retrospective and Munger's Essay

> **Key principle:** Berkshire's first 50 years were built less on picking cheap stocks than on a deliberate structural bet: extreme autonomy for subsidiary managers, a nearly empty headquarters, and centralized capital allocation concentrated in one person for decades. Buffett's half owns the mistakes and the mechanics of how that structure came to be; Munger's half explains, point by point, why it worked and why it is hard to copy.

*Synthesized from Berkshire Hathaway's 2014 Chairman's Letter to shareholders (50th-anniversary edition, including Charlie Munger's appended essay), by Warren E. Buffett and Charlie Munger.*

## Key lessons

**Buffett's fifty-year retrospective**

- Buffett calls taking control of Berkshire in 1965 "a monumentally stupid decision": he only bought more shares out of irritation after Seabury Stanton reneged on a verbal $11.50 tender-offer price and mailed a formal offer of $11.375 instead, leaving him controlling a dying New England textile mill he knew little about.
- He calls his 1967 purchase of National Indemnity for $8.6 million an even more serious blunder, not because the deal was bad but because he made it through Berkshire rather than through Buffett Partnership Ltd., which he estimates diverted roughly $100 billion over time from his own partners to Berkshire's pre-existing textile shareholders.
- His "cigar-butt" style, learned from Ben Graham, worked on small sums in the 1950s but didn't scale; he credits Charlie Munger with redirecting him toward Munger's blueprint of "buy wonderful businesses at fair prices," first proven on See's Candy in 1972 (bought for $25 million, has since earned $1.9 billion pretax on only $40 million of additional investment).
- His "most gruesome" acknowledged mistake was Dexter Shoe (1993, $433 million), whose value went to zero once foreign competition hit, made worse because he paid in Berkshire stock that would otherwise be worth about $5.7 billion.
- He traces insurance's role as Berkshire's engine directly to the 1967 National Indemnity deal, noting float grew from $39 million in 1970 to $83.9 billion by 2014, with 12 consecutive years of underwriting profit totaling $24 billion by 2014.
- He explains why Berkshire keeps its conglomerate form rather than spinning businesses off: it can move capital between subsidiaries without tax or frictional cost, and it has become a preferred "permanent home" for owners who want to sell without their people being fired for "synergies" or loaded up with private-equity debt.
- On succession, he proposes his son Howard as a non-paid, non-executive chairman purely as a safety valve to remove a CEO if needed, and lists the traits a Berkshire CEO must have: rational, calm, decisive, and able to fight off what he calls "the ABCs of business decay" — arrogance, bureaucracy, complacency.
- He notes headquarters employs only 25 people, with no committees, no required budgets, and no legal, HR, PR, or strategy departments, calling that "extraordinary delegation of authority" the antidote to bureaucracy as Berkshire grows.

**Munger on why the structure worked**

- Munger lays out what he calls "the Berkshire system" as roughly 15 fixed policies: a diffuse conglomerate; subsidiaries run with extreme autonomy; a tiny headquarters of a chairman, a CFO, and a few assistants; casualty insurers kept as a permanent core for float; no company-wide personnel or incentive systems; acquisitions paid in cash, not stock; and a practice of almost never selling a subsidiary or forcing a CEO out over age.
- He compares Buffett's fifty years of concentrating almost all his attention on a few kinds of decisions to basketball coach John Wooden giving nearly all playing time to his seven best players, and to Roger Federer's tennis — except, Munger notes, Buffett's skill kept improving with age rather than deteriorating.
- He describes a "virtuous circle": autonomy attracted better subsidiaries and CEOs, and those better subsidiaries and CEOs then required even less oversight from headquarters, reinforcing the whole system.
- Reviewing mistakes honestly, Munger says nearly all of Berkshire's big errors were errors of omission rather than commission — not buying, for instance, when Walmart stock was "sure to work out enormously well" — and estimates such missed opportunities cost Berkshire's net worth at least $50 billion.
- He predicts the results would continue even if Buffett left immediately, citing Ajit Jain and Greg Abel by name as "world-class," even "world-leading" successors unlikely to want to change the system, and closes by arguing bureaucracy should be treated "like the cancers they so much resemble," citing George Marshall's move to let Congress override seniority in choosing WWII generals as a model of fixing it.
