# 1996: The Owner's Manual and the Circle of Competence

> **Key principle:** Berkshire reprints, in full, an "Owner's Manual" of its owner-related business principles, and in the same letter Buffett distills his investing method to a single sentence: "You only have to be able to evaluate companies within your circle of competence. The size of that circle is not very important; knowing its boundaries, however, is vital."

*Synthesized from Berkshire Hathaway's 1996 Chairman's Letter to shareholders, by Warren E. Buffett.*

## Key lessons

- **The Owner's Manual, formally issued.** Following the creation of Class B shares earlier in 1996, Berkshire reprints its full set of owner-related principles in the annual report for the first time, rather than the brief listing used in prior years. Buffett refers back to it repeatedly in the letter for definitions like "intrinsic value," treating it as the standing reference document for how he wants shareholders to think about the company.
- **The circle of competence, stated directly.** In a passage aimed explicitly at individual investors, Buffett writes that intelligent investing "is not complex, though that is far from saying that it is easy," and that what it requires is not broad expertise but honest self-knowledge of where one's understanding actually ends. He adds that investors don't need to understand beta, efficient markets, or option pricing — in fact they may be better off not knowing these — and that the only two courses worth teaching are "How to Value a Business" and "How to Think About Market Prices."
- **The index-fund recommendation.** Before laying out his own approach, Buffett tells most investors, institutional and individual alike, that a low-cost index fund will beat the net results delivered by the majority of investment professionals after fees — advice he frames as more useful to most readers than anything else in the letter.
- **Serendipitous, low-friction acquisitions.** Kansas Bankers Surety was bought for $75 million after Buffett happened to sit next to a director at a birthday party and, weeks later, received a one-paragraph letter offering the numbers. FlightSafety International, at roughly $1.5 billion, came together after a shareholder-couple's tip to CEO Al Ueltschi led to a deal struck within about sixty seconds of meeting him — both examples Buffett uses to illustrate that Berkshire's acquisition "strategy" is really just patience plus a willingness to move fast once the fit is obvious.
- **GEICO's widening moat.** Buffett credits GEICO's 1996 push of underwriting and loss-adjustment expenses down to 23.6% of premiums as evidence of a "virtuous circle": low costs enable low prices, low prices retain policyholders, and satisfied policyholders generate over a million annual referrals that lower acquisition costs further still — a self-reinforcing advantage he says widened again that year.
- **"The Inevitables" and the discipline of focus.** Buffett names a small class of companies — Coca-Cola and Gillette among them — whose durable competitive position makes their long-run dominance close to certain, but warns that even Inevitables can be ruined by management that loses focus, citing Coke's old foray into shrimp farming and Gillette's into oil exploration as cautionary, half-forgotten history.
- **Float keeps compounding.** The insurance-float table is updated to show growth from $17.3 million in 1967 to $6.7 billion by year-end 1996, a 22.3% compounded annual rate, again mostly financed at negative cost — Buffett states plainly that understanding float is essential to understanding Berkshire's intrinsic value, since it is a major value component that book value does not capture.
- **Ten-year, not ten-minute, ownership.** Buffett tells shareholders that if you aren't willing to own a stock for ten years, you shouldn't own it for ten minutes, and that a portfolio of a few companies whose earnings march upward over decades is the entire method behind Berkshire's own "look-through" earnings growth.
