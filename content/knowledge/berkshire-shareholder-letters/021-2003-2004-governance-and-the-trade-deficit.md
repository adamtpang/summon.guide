# 2003-2004: Governance and the Trade Deficit

> **Key principle:** Buffett argues that "independent" directors as legally defined routinely fail shareholders because boardroom atmosphere sedates their fiduciary instincts, and offers Berkshire's own board — where every director's family owns a real, uninsured stake — as the alternative model. In a separate, unrelated argument, he warns that persistent U.S. trade deficits are steadily transferring American wealth abroad in a way that ordinary government budget deficits never do.

*Synthesized from Berkshire Hathaway's 2003 and 2004 Chairman's Letters to shareholders, by Warren E. Buffett.*

## Key lessons

### Governance: CEO pay as the acid test

- **The "acid test."** Buffett states directly that CEO pay remains the clearest measure of whether corporate reform is genuine, and finds most of corporate America failing it, singling out Jeff Immelt of GE as a rare example whose pay reforms were "more admired than followed" elsewhere.
- **Why comp-committee negotiations break down.** Unlike an arm's-length vendor negotiation, Buffett argues that a CEO cares intensely about the difference between an option grant of 100,000 versus 500,000 shares, while a compensation committee, especially before options counted against reported earnings, often treated the difference as "play-money" — a mismatch in stakes that let executive pay run away through the 1990s, spread company to company by consultants who, in his account, know "who buttered their bread."
- **The mutual-fund director case study.** Buffett examines "independent" mutual fund directors as a long-running natural experiment (mandated since the Investment Company Act of 1940) and finds them routinely rehiring underperforming managers and approving excessive fees, only to switch loyalties instantly and profitably whenever a fund's management company itself was sold to a new owner — behavior he calls not malicious but the product of a boardroom atmosphere that "almost invariably sedates" fiduciary duty.
- **What "independence" actually requires.** Buffett argues that formal rules mislabel independence: a director drawing 100% of his income from board fees across several companies can be "independent" by statute while someone like Berkshire's Ron Olson, who receives a small fraction of his income from Berkshire-related legal work, technically isn't — proposing instead that the real test is how much of a person's income depends on staying in management's good graces.
- **Berkshire's own governance as the counter-model.** All eleven Berkshire directors, Buffett notes, own more than $4 million of stock bought in the open market (never granted via options), receive only nominal director fees, and — unlike at most companies — sit on a board that carries no directors-and-officers liability insurance, meaning their personal downside is worse than an ordinary shareholder's, not better. His summary: "You win, they win big; you lose, they lose big."
- **Two post-bubble reforms Buffett wishes he'd adopted sooner (2004).** He credits regular director sessions held without the CEO present, and a whistleblower line letting any of Berkshire's roughly 180,000 employees report problems directly to him and the audit committee, as improvements that should have been standard practice for decades before Berkshire actually implemented them.
- **The Coca-Cola "independence" episode.** Buffett recounts institutional investors challenging his own board "independence" as a Coca-Cola director because Berkshire subsidiaries purchase Coke products, a claim he rebuts by pointing out that Berkshire's roughly $8 billion Coca-Cola stock stake dwarfs any incentive from routine purchasing — quoting Matthew 6:21 ("For where your treasure is, there will your heart be also") as, in his view, a sharper definition of independence than the formal rules use.

### The trade deficit and currency hedging

- **A new position, driven by conviction.** Buffett writes that Berkshire entered the foreign-currency market for the first time in his life in 2002 and expanded the position through 2003 and 2004, becoming "increasingly bearish on the dollar" even while acknowledging that "the cemetery for seers has a huge section set aside for macro forecasters."
- **The mechanics of the deficit.** He explains that a persistent U.S. trade deficit — $618 billion in 2003, up from $263 billion in 1999 — is "force-feeding" foreign claims on American assets: either IOUs from U.S. institutions or direct foreign ownership of U.S. stocks and real estate. By the 2004 letter, he estimates this transfer was proceeding at $1.8 billion a day and had left foreigners with roughly $3 trillion of net U.S. ownership, versus "negligible" a decade earlier.
- **Trade deficit versus budget deficit — not "twins."** Buffett draws a sharp distinction often conflated in commentary: a government budget deficit only redistributes the national pie among Americans (through taxes and spending), leaving 100% of output in American hands, whereas a persistent trade deficit permanently sends a growing "royalty" on American output abroad — likening the country to "a family that consistently overspends its income" and increasingly works for outside creditors.
- **A hedge, not a bet against America.** Buffett is explicit that the currency position reflects no doubt about the U.S. economy itself, which he calls "far and away the strongest in the world." The hedge exists because Berkshire holds many billions in dollar-denominated cash equivalents, and owning foreign-exchange contracts offsets that dollar exposure given his trade-policy concerns; by year-end 2004 those contracts totaled about $21.4 billion spread across twelve currencies.
- **A largely ignored warning.** Buffett notes that a 318-page Congressional study on the consequences of sustained trade deficits, commissioned in 2000, had "been gathering dust" ever since — evidence, in his account, that policymakers were inclined toward what he calls "not-so-benign neglect" of the issue.
