# 1982: The Dollar-for-a-Dollar Test

> **Key principle:** Berkshire's job, in Buffett's words, is to select businesses "with economic characteristics allowing each dollar of retained earnings to be translated eventually into at least a dollar of market value," and that same yardstick condemns the ordinary practice of using undervalued company stock to buy fully-valued businesses in mergers.

*Synthesized from Berkshire Hathaway's 1982 Chairman's Letter to shareholders, by Warren E. Buffett.*

## Key lessons

- Operating earnings fell to just 9.8% of beginning equity (down from 15.2% in 1981 and a 19.4% high in 1978), driven by weaker insurance underwriting, a growing equity base without matching growth in directly-operated businesses, and an ever-larger share of earnings locked inside partly-owned companies that accounting rules exclude from Berkshire's reported results. Buffett jokes that struggling managers "shoot the arrow of business performance into a blank canvas and then carefully draw the bullseye around" it, but says Berkshire's own abandonment of the operating-earnings yardstick this year is genuinely warranted.
- The GEICO example makes the "non-reported ownership earnings" problem concrete: Berkshire's roughly 35% stake generated only $3.5 million in reported dividends, while GEICO's actual undistributed earnings attributable to Berkshire were about $23 million — and Buffett notes the perverse accounting result that if GEICO earned $100 million more and retained all of it, Berkshire's reported earnings would be completely unchanged.
- He states the retained-earnings test explicitly: Berkshire's job is to select businesses where "each dollar of retained earnings" gets "translated eventually into at least a dollar of market value." He credits "St. Offset" (a nod to economist Arthur Okun) for the fact that Berkshire's over-performing holdings have so far more than made up for its laggards.
- The four major non-controlled holdings (GEICO, General Foods, The Washington Post, and a newly enlarged stake in R.J. Reynolds) together generated over $40 million in undistributed 1982 earnings attributable to Berkshire — more than Berkshire's entire reported earnings, which included only $14 million in actual dividends from that group.
- In the "Issuance of Equity" section, Buffett lays out the mechanics of why stock-financed mergers so often destroy value: a seller negotiating a full-company sale can demand full intrinsic value, but a buyer whose own stock trades below intrinsic value has no such leverage when issuing shares — the buyer ends up "using an undervalued (market value) currency to pay for a fully valued (negotiated value) property," in effect giving up $2 of value to receive $1.
- He illustrates the same dilution with a sharper hypothetical: if Investment Company X, trading at 50% of asset value, issues shares worth 100% of Investment Company Y's asset value to acquire Y, X's shareholders would be trading $2 of their own intrinsic value for $1 of Y's — a transaction the SEC would block outright in the regulated investment-company world, even though economically similar trades happen routinely in ordinary corporate mergers.
- Buffett criticizes how "dilution" is conventionally measured — focused narrowly on near-term earnings-per-share impact rather than intrinsic business value — and notes that some earnings-per-share-dilutive mergers have actually been value-enhancing, while plenty of "non-dilutive" mergers by that narrow measure have destroyed value. He also reframes merger language itself: "Company A to Acquire Company B" is more honestly described as "Part of A sold to acquire B," since in a stock deal, what the acquirer gives up matters as much as what it receives.
- Miscellaneously, this year's letter lays out the formal acquisition-criteria list Berkshire uses to screen inbound opportunities: (1) large purchases of at least $5 million of after-tax earnings, (2) demonstrated consistent earning power (not projections or turnarounds), (3) good returns on equity with little or no debt, (4) existing management in place, (5) simple businesses ("if there's lots of technology, we won't understand it"), and (6) an offering price, since Berkshire won't negotiate on an unstated price. It promises confidentiality and a decision "customarily within five minutes," and states it will not participate in unfriendly transactions.
