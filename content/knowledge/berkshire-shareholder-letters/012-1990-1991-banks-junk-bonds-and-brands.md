# 1990-1991: Banks, Junk Bonds, and Brands

> **Key principle:** Across these two letters Buffett explains why Berkshire bought into Wells Fargo despite a general aversion to banking, why the junk-bond boom of the 1980s was built on a flawed statistical assumption, and how to tell a true "economic franchise" (which can survive bad management) from an ordinary business (which cannot).

*Synthesized from Berkshire Hathaway's 1990 and 1991 Chairman's Letters to shareholders, by Warren E. Buffett.*

## Key lessons

### 1990: banking leverage and the junk-bond mania

- Buffett states banking is "no favorite" of Berkshire's because a common industry ratio of assets to equity of about twenty-to-one means a mistake touching only a small share of assets can wipe out a large share of equity; he attributes many banks' 1990-era losses to the "institutional imperative" driving executives to "follow-the-leader" lending with "lemming-like zeal."
- Despite that aversion, Berkshire raised its Wells Fargo stake to just under 10% (the maximum allowed without Federal Reserve approval), buying roughly $290 million worth at under five times after-tax earnings after a bank-stock panic driven by fears of California real estate losses; he credits managers Carl Reichardt and Paul Hazen specifically, comparing them to Tom Murphy and Dan Burke at Capital Cities.
- He runs the downside math explicitly: even if 10% of Wells Fargo's entire $48 billion loan book, not just real estate loans, took losses averaging 30% of principal in a single year, the bank would roughly break even, a scenario he calls a low-level possibility Berkshire could tolerate, not a likelihood.
- On falling prices generally, he argues investors should welcome declines the way they welcome cheaper food, since they intend to be perpetual buyers of businesses; "it's optimism that is the enemy of the rational buyer," though he cautions that unpopularity alone doesn't make a purchase intelligent.
- On junk bonds, Buffett describes new-issue junk of the 1980s as a "bastardized fallen angel," distinct from old-fashioned fallen angels (investment-grade bonds later downgraded); the "dagger on the steering wheel" theory that heavy debt disciplines management ignores that business has potholes, and a plan requiring every pothole to be dodged is "a plan for disaster."
- He identifies the specific statistical error behind the junk-bond sales pitch: promoters relied on the historical default record of fallen angels to justify projected defaults on newly issued junk bonds, even though the two universes of issuer were fundamentally different in motivation and quality, an error he compares to judging Kool-Aid's safety from its ordinary death rate before drinking the version served at Jonestown.
- He restates Ben Graham's three-word distillation of sound investing, "margin of safety," as the correct rebuttal to the whole debt-driven capital-structure mania, alongside a real example: an LBO-financed Tampa TV station where interest expense alone exceeded the station's gross revenue.

### 1991: the anatomy of an economic franchise

- Buffett defines an economic franchise precisely: a product or service that is needed or desired, believed by customers to have no close substitute, and not subject to price regulation; a franchise "can tolerate mis-management," since inept managers can dent its profits but not kill it, while an ordinary "business" without those traits can be killed by poor management.
- He applies the distinction to his own media holdings, arguing that newspapers, television, and magazines had begun shifting from franchise-like economics toward ordinary-business economics as new sources of entertainment competed for a fixed 24-hour day of attention, materially reducing intrinsic value even where reported earnings held up.
- A worked example shows why the shift matters so much: valuing $1 million of earnings as a perpetual 6%-growth annuity at a 10% discount rate justifies paying $25 million, but treating the same $1 million as merely "bobbing around" cyclically cuts the justified price to $10 million, a large valuation swing from a modest change in growth assumption.
- He credits Buffalo News, Capital Cities, and Washington Post with weathering the media franchise's erosion better than peers because their managements avoided the era's irrational acquisition prices and kept balance sheets nearly debt-free, unlike most publicly-traded media companies carrying debt of five times net income or more.
- The letter's second half turns to See's Candy on its twentieth anniversary under Berkshire: bought in 1972 for $25 million against $7 million of tangible net worth, See's grew sales from $29 million to $196 million and pre-tax profit from $4.2 million to $42.4 million by 1991, an outcome Buffett attributes to the pricing power of a true franchise rather than to heavy reinvestment.
- Coca-Cola and Gillette, held throughout this period, are singled out as "two of the best companies in the world," and the letter notes their combined stock-price re-rating accounted for nearly $1.6 billion of Berkshire's $2.1 billion net worth gain in 1991, a gain Buffett flags as unlikely to repeat annually.
