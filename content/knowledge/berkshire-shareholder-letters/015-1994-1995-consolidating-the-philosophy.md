# 1994-1995: Consolidating the Philosophy

> **Key principle:** By the mid-1990s Buffett is less interested in new doctrine than in re-explaining, with fresh examples, ideas he has held for decades: that book value is a poor proxy for intrinsic value, that a manager's job is to price rather than time purchases, and that a "fat wallet is the enemy of superior investment results."

*Synthesized from Berkshire Hathaway's 1994 and 1995 Chairman's Letters to shareholders, by Warren E. Buffett.*

## Key lessons

- **1994 — book value versus intrinsic value.** Buffett uses a college education as an analogy: its "book value" is its cost, but its intrinsic value is the discounted future earnings it produces minus what the graduate would have earned anyway. Applied to Berkshire, he shows that Scott Fetzer, bought in 1986 for $315.2 million against $172.6 million of book value, had by 1994 seen its book value fall (through accounting purchase-premium write-offs) even as its actual earnings roughly doubled, meaning intrinsic value and carrying value diverged sharply in Berkshire's favor.
- **The capital-allocation warning.** Buffett illustrates why using near-term earnings-per-share impact to judge a merger is "silly" with a deliberately absurd example: a 25-year-old MBA student merging his future economic interest with a day laborer's would boost his own near-term "earnings" enormously, yet the deal would obviously be terrible for him. Acquirers who chase this kind of short-term accretion, he argues, usually give up more intrinsic value than they receive — "a chain letter in reverse," in the words of banker John Medlin.
- **Compensation as symmetry.** Ralph Schey, CEO of Scott Fetzer, is paid on Scott Fetzer's results alone, not Berkshire's, with a bonus formula that charges him a real cost for incremental capital and credits him equally for capital released — a deal worked out "in about five minutes" and never renegotiated. Buffett contrasts this with typical stock-option plans that don't adjust for retained earnings, letting a manager profit merely by "treading water."
- **The USAir mistake, admitted in full.** Buffett calls his 1989 purchase of $358 million in USAir preferred stock his worst mistake of the year, writing the position down to 25 cents on the dollar. He traces the error to failing to see that airline deregulation would eventually force high-cost carriers to compete on price with no way to control their embedded labor and capital costs — "sloppy analysis," in his own words, compounded by the false comfort of holding a senior security.
- **GEICO — the 45-year relationship completed.** Buffett recounts discovering GEICO as a 20-year-old student of Ben Graham's in 1951, buying and then foolishly selling his stake, watching Berkshire acquire roughly a third of the company in 1976 when it was near insolvency, and finally, right after year-end 1995, paying $2.3 billion for the roughly half of GEICO Berkshire didn't yet own — a "steep price" justified by full ownership of a business whose direct-marketing cost advantage was, in his words, unchanged since 1951.
- **Insurance float as a compounding engine.** Both letters return to Berkshire's insurance float — premiums held before claims are paid — showing it grew from $17 million in 1967 to $3.6 billion by 1995 at a compounded rate above 20%, usually at a cost below zero (an underwriting profit), which Buffett treats as effectively free leverage unavailable to insurers whose float costs more than it's worth.
- **Super-cat discipline.** Ajit Jain's catastrophe-reinsurance unit is presented as a deliberate embrace of "lumpy" results: Berkshire will write single policies (a $400 million California earthquake cover in 1994, for instance) that no other insurer will touch, accepting large potential losses in exchange for pricing power, because Berkshire's balance-sheet strength lets it be paid and stay solvent when a true catastrophe hits.
- **Acquisitions by walking around.** The 1995 letter tells the origin stories of Helzberg's Diamond Shops (met on a New York street corner) and R.C. Willey Home Furnishings (introduced by Nebraska Furniture Mart's Irv Blumkin), both closed with minimal negotiation because Buffett's stated preference is to compare every deal against the alternative of simply buying small pieces of great businesses in the stock market — a discipline he says most expansion-minded managers skip.
- **Investments stay few and simple.** Buffett repeats a recurring theme: "The truly big investment idea can usually be explained in a short paragraph," and an investor's scorecard doesn't reward complexity for its own sake — being right about a simple, easily understood business pays exactly the same as being right about something complicated.
