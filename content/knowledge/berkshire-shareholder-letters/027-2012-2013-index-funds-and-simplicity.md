# 2012-2013: Index Funds and the Case for Simplicity

> **Key principle:** Buffett argues that picking individual winners is a skill neither the non-professional investor nor his paid "helpers" actually possess, so the sounder goal is to own a low-cost cross-section of American business (a low-cost S&P 500 index fund) and hold it cheaply, patiently, and without trying to time entries and exits. He backs this with his own will, which instructs a simple 90/10 index-fund-and-bonds split for his wife's trust.

*Synthesized from Berkshire Hathaway's 2012 and 2013 Chairman's Letters to shareholders, by Warren E. Buffett.*

## Key lessons

- **2012:** Buffett frames Berkshire's own performance against this same standard: he tells shareholders that if he and Charlie Munger ever fail to grow intrinsic value faster than the S&P 500 over time, "our management will bring no value to our investors, who themselves can earn S&P returns by buying a low-cost index fund." The index fund is treated as the honest baseline every active manager, including Berkshire itself, must beat to justify its fees.
- **2013:** In "Some Thoughts About Investing," Buffett illustrates the case for simplicity with two small, non-stock investments he made personally: a 400-acre Nebraska farm bought from the FDIC in 1986 for $280,000, and a New York retail property near NYU bought in 1993 with Larry Silverstein and Fred Rose. Both were bought on a simple estimate of normalized yield (about 10% in each case), held for decades without ever checking a daily quote, and both multiplied several times in value.
- **2013:** He draws the direct lesson from those two deals: the ordinary investor "doesn't need to be an expert" but must recognize the limits of that non-expertise and follow a course built to work anyway, keeping things simple, ignoring quick-profit pitches, and focusing on an asset's future productivity rather than its day-to-day price.
- **2013:** He states the core claim plainly: the goal of the non-professional "should not be to pick winners — neither he nor his 'helpers' can do that — but should rather be to own a cross-section of businesses that in aggregate are bound to do well," and that a low-cost S&P 500 index fund achieves exactly that.
- **2013:** He warns that market chatter is a cost, not a benefit: both individuals and institutions are constantly urged toward activity by people who profit from advice-giving or trading, and the resulting frictional costs are, for investors as a group, "devoid of benefit." His prescription: "ignore the chatter, keep your costs minimal, and invest in stocks as you would in a farm."
- **2013:** He extends the argument to timing as well as stock selection: the "know-nothing" investor who both diversifies broadly and keeps costs minimal is "virtually certain to get satisfactory results," accumulating shares steadily and never selling into bad news, while the sophisticated professional who is blind to even one weakness can do worse.
- **2013:** Buffett makes the point personal and concrete by quoting his own will: cash left in trust for his wife's benefit carries a specific instruction to the trustee, "Put 10% of the cash in short-term government bonds and 90% in a very low-cost S&P 500 index fund," naming Vanguard as his suggested provider. He states his belief that this simple policy will outperform the long-term results achieved by most pension funds, institutions, or individuals who instead pay high-fee managers.
- **2013:** He credits Benjamin Graham's *The Intelligent Investor* (bought in 1949) as the source of this discipline, contrasting Graham's plain, formula-free reasoning with his own earlier years spent chart-reading and watching the ticker tape, activity he says left him "wasn't getting anywhere."
