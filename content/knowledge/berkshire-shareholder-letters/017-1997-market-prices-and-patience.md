# 1997: Market Prices and Patience

> **Key principle:** Buffett poses a "final exam" question: if you expect to be a net saver of stocks for the next five years, should you want the market higher or lower during that time? Most investors answer wrong, celebrating price rises on the very "hamburgers" — shares — they are about to buy more of.

*Synthesized from Berkshire Hathaway's 1997 Chairman's Letter to shareholders, by Warren E. Buffett.*

## Key lessons

- **A "quack" of a year.** Despite a 34.1% gain in per-share book value, Buffett rates 1997's performance as unremarkable, comparing Berkshire to "the preening duck that quacks boastfully after a torrential rainstorm, thinking that its paddling skills have caused it to rise in the world." Passive S&P index investors, unburdened by Berkshire's corporate taxes (Berkshire paid roughly $4.2 billion, about 18% of beginning net worth, in federal income tax that year), rose almost as fast.
- **The hamburger analogy.** Buffett asks whether someone who eats hamburgers but doesn't raise cattle should want beef prices higher or lower — obviously lower — then applies the same logic to stocks: an investor who will be a net buyer for years should prefer a falling market, since it makes future purchases cheaper. He notes that Berkshire itself is functionally always a net buyer, since it retains all its earnings and redeploys them into more businesses and securities.
- **Why people get it backwards.** Buffett observes that investors "rejoice because prices have risen for the 'hamburgers' they will soon be buying," a reaction he calls nonsensical for anyone who isn't about to sell. He reframes the standard headline "Investors lose as market falls" as "Disinvestors lose as market falls — but investors gain," since every transaction has both a buyer and a seller and what hurts one necessarily helps the other.
- **Repurchases benefit more when prices are low.** Because Berkshire owns large stakes in companies (Coca-Cola, The Washington Post, Wells Fargo) that buy back their own shares, Buffett notes that share repurchases made at low prices increase Berkshire's percentage ownership by more than the same dollar amount spent at high prices — another reason a Berkshire shareholder, as a permanent holder, should want prices depressed rather than elevated.
- **The Ted Williams strike zone.** Buffett revisits Ted Williams's account of dividing the batting strike zone into 77 cells and swinging only at pitches in his best cell to maximize his average. With business and stock prices "high" in 1997, Buffett describes the available opportunities as pitches "just catching the lower outside corner" — tempting to swing at, but likely to produce only low returns if he does.
- **Unconventional, non-traditional positions.** With few attractive stock or business purchases available, Berkshire held three "unconventional commitments" at year-end: oil futures contracts (a leftover 1994-95 position), 111.2 million ounces of silver bought in 1997, and $4.6 billion of long-term zero-coupon U.S. Treasury bonds — all framed as opportunistic bets rather than the "wonderful business at a fair price" purchases Buffett prefers, and all carrying real risk of being wrong.
- **Float discipline continues.** The float table shows growth to $7.1 billion by year-end 1997 at a 21.7% compounded annual rate since 1967, still financed at negative cost in most years; Buffett stresses that because loss reserves require estimation, investors must be wary of insurers whose reported underwriting results may mask reserving errors, honest or otherwise.
- **The lesson scaled down for size.** Buffett notes that an investment idea producing a $10,000 gain moved his personal results by a full percentage point in 1951; by 1997, an idea would need to produce roughly $500 million pre-tax to move Berkshire's results by the same one percentage point — a concrete illustration of why he expects future returns to fall short of the past.
