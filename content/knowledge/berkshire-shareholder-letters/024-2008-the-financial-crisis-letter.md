# The 2008 Letter: Buying Into the Panic

> **Key principle:** Berkshire's Gibraltar-like balance sheet, huge cash reserves, and refusal to depend on short-term borrowing were built for exactly this moment: when the rest of the financial system froze, Berkshire had both the nerve and the liquidity to keep buying.

*Synthesized from Berkshire Hathaway's 2008 Chairman's Letter to shareholders, by Warren E. Buffett.*

## Key lessons

- 2008 was the worst year on record for both measures Buffett tracks: Berkshire's per-share book value fell 9.6% and the S&P 500 (with dividends) fell 37.0%, the two worst results in the 44-year table since present management took over.
- Buffett describes the mechanism of the crisis directly: a dysfunctional, then "non-functional" credit market combined with tumbling home and stock prices produced "a paralyzing fear that engulfed the country," which he calls a vicious negative-feedback cycle: fear caused business contraction, which caused more fear.
- He states plainly that in good years and bad he and Charlie Munger focus on four fixed goals, the first of which is maintaining Berkshire's "Gibraltar-like financial position": huge excess liquidity, modest near-term obligations, and dozens of independent sources of earnings and cash. The other three are widening competitive moats, acquiring new earnings streams, and developing outstanding managers.
- His investing rule for the year, stated in his own words: "Berkshire is always a buyer of both businesses and securities, and the disarray in markets gave us a tailwind in our purchases. When investing, pessimism is your friend, euphoria the enemy." He pairs this with a Ben Graham line he says shaped his thinking: "Price is what you pay; value is what you get."
- The signature crisis-era deals: Berkshire put $14.5 billion into fixed-income securities issued by Wrigley, Goldman Sachs, and General Electric. He values these primarily for their high current yields, which alone made them "more than satisfactory," but each purchase also came with a bonus equity participation attached. To fund them, he sold down other holdings he'd rather have kept, including Johnson & Johnson, Procter & Gamble, and ConocoPhillips.
- He frames the discipline behind those purchases as a standing pledge: "I have pledged - to you, the rating agencies and myself - to always run Berkshire with more than ample cash. We never want to count on the kindness of strangers... When forced to choose, I will not trade even a night's sleep for the chance of extra profits."
- He documents the perverse credit-market distortion of the crisis directly: entities with any government guarantee (FDIC-insured banks, Fed-backed commercial paper issuers) had access to nearly free money, while Berkshire, one of only seven AAA-rated corporations in the country, faced borrowing costs at record levels relative to Treasuries. His line: "it is much better to be a financial cripple with a government guarantee than a Gibraltar without one."
- Buffett endorses the government's response even while warning of its costs: he calls the Treasury and Fed's intervention going "all in" in poker terms, says the dosage will "almost certainly bring on unwelcome aftereffects" including likely inflation, but insists the action was essential because a total breakdown of the financial system would have been "cataclysmic" for every part of the economy.
- His diagnosis of the housing crisis runs through Clayton Homes' manufactured-housing lending. He traces a 1997-2000 fiasco in manufactured housing, where "borrowers who shouldn't have borrowed" were financed by "lenders who shouldn't have lent" and the loans were securitized and sold off, as a canary-in-the-coal-mine that investors, government, and rating agencies then ignored when the identical pattern repeated in conventional housing from 2004-07, with both sides counting on ever-rising home prices to bail out loans that incomes alone couldn't support.
- He contrasts this with Clayton's own underwriting: its borrowers had a median FICO score of 644 against a 723 national median, yet delinquencies stayed at 3.6% because Clayton verified real income against real monthly payments rather than relying on refinancing or price appreciation. His stated policy lesson: home purchases should require a genuine down payment of at least 10% and monthly payments the borrower's verified income can actually support.
- On the bond-insurance business, he describes offering early in 2008 to take over roughly $822 billion of tax-exempt bond guarantees from the three largest distressed monoline insurers at a 1.5% rate; when they rejected the offer (with insults), it turned out to be good news, since Berkshire later wrote about $15.6 billion of "second-to-pay" insurance on the same bonds at an average 3.3% rate, in one case being paid three times the going rate to stand fourth in line to pay.
- He gives an unusually direct verdict on derivatives: "Derivatives are dangerous. They have dramatically increased the leverage and risks in our financial system." He cites unwinding General Re's 23,218 derivatives contracts with 884 counterparties after the 1998 purchase, a process that took five years and cost more than $400 million, and argues that opacity, not just risk-taking, was central to the crisis, noting that Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac's regulator (OFHEO), with over 100 employees dedicated solely to overseeing them, still completely missed years of earnings manipulation.
- He explains the systemic danger of derivatives through counterparty interdependence, quoting Fed official Tim Geithner's account of the Bear Stearns rescue and endorsing the Fed's intervention as correct, then names what he calls "The First Law of Corporate Survival" for leveraged financial CEOs: being merely modestly incompetent isn't enough to guarantee a bailout, only "mindboggling screw-ups" reliably are.
- He explicitly contrasts Berkshire's own approach to derivatives with the industry's failures: Berkshire's contracts are structured so counterparties pay Berkshire money upfront at inception, meaning Berkshire holds the cash and carries "no meaningful counterparty risk," the opposite of the exposure that sank other institutions.
- He owns his mistakes rather than only touting successes: he calls a large, poorly timed purchase of ConocoPhillips stock near peak oil prices his biggest error of commission, and separately admits a $244 million bet on two Irish banks that had already been written down to $27 million, an 89% loss, by yearend.
- He closes the crisis narrative on a historical note, cataloguing the 20th century's two world wars, roughly a dozen panics and recessions, a 21.5% prime rate, and the Great Depression's 15-25% unemployment, arguing that living standards still rose nearly sevenfold over the century and concluding plainly: "America's best days lie ahead."
