# 1988: Coca-Cola and Permanent Holdings

> **Key principle:** In 1988 Berkshire's insurance companies made major purchases of Coca-Cola and Freddie Mac preferred stock, and Buffett uses the occasion to state plainly that "our favorite holding period is forever," rejecting the Wall Street habit of selling winners early while nursing losers indefinitely.

*Synthesized from Berkshire Hathaway's 1988 Chairman's Letter to shareholders, by Warren E. Buffett.*

## Key lessons

- Buffett discloses Berkshire's new stakes for the year: 14,172,500 shares of Coca-Cola (cost $592.5 million) and a large preferred position in Federal Home Loan Mortgage Corp. ("Freddie Mac"), alongside the unchanged permanent holdings of Capital Cities/ABC, GEICO, and The Washington Post.
- He states the principle directly: "when we own portions of outstanding businesses with outstanding managements, our favorite holding period is forever," and contrasts this with investors who rush to sell winners and book profits while clinging to disappointing holdings, quoting Peter Lynch's line about "cutting the flowers and watering the weeds."
- Buffett explains the logic of concentration: Berkshire deliberately holds only a handful of businesses it has strong long-term convictions about, and when such a business is found, the goal is to participate "in a meaningful way" rather than spread capital thinly, backed by a Mae West line that "too much of a good thing can be wonderful."
- A long section on CEO accountability explains why Buffett trusts these particular managements so completely: unlike a secretary or salesperson whose performance is measured against a clear standard, a mediocre CEO is rarely removed because performance standards are vague, boards are reluctant to criticize, and directors who make a bad hiring call are seldom held to account themselves.
- Against that backdrop he names the specific people behind Berkshire's permanent holdings, Tom Murphy and Dan Burke at Cap Cities, Bill Snyder and Lou Simpson at GEICO, and Kay Graham and Dick Simmons at The Washington Post, as the reason those stakes are exceptions to his broader skepticism of corporate management.
- On the insurance side of the letter, Buffett describes California's Proposition 103, an initiative to force auto insurance prices down despite rising costs, as a serious threat to GEICO's economics precisely because GEICO's low-cost structure gives it real "economic goodwill" to lose, whereas most mediocre insurers have little goodwill at risk from price controls.
- He also recounts unusually large arbitrage profits in 1988 (about $78 million pre-tax on roughly $147 million average invested), framed as opportunistic use of cash between long-term commitments rather than a shift in overall philosophy.
