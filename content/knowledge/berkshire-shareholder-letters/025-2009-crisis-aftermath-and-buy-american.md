# 2009: Crisis Aftermath and the BNSF Railroad Bet

> **Key principle:** Buffett frames 2009 as the year Berkshire converted crisis-era liquidity into permanent, capital-intensive infrastructure. Berkshire didn't just survive the meltdown, it wrote billion-dollar checks in the worst of it, then bought a railroad outright on the reasoning that the country cannot approach its economic potential without first-class rail and electricity systems.

*Synthesized from Berkshire Hathaway's 2009 Chairman's Letter to shareholders, by Warren E. Buffett.*

## Key lessons

- Berkshire's book value per share grew 19.8% in 2009, a $21.8 billion gain in net worth, even as the letter concedes the company's margin of outperformance over the S&P 500 keeps shrinking as its size grows ("huge sums forge their own anchor").
- Buffett recounts that when the financial system went into "cardiac arrest" in September 2008, Berkshire was "a supplier of liquidity and capital to the system, not a supplicant," pouring $15.5 billion into the market at the peak of the panic: $9 billion to shore up capital at three well-regarded American businesses and $6.5 billion to help fund the Wrigley acquisition, both completed without pause while panic reigned elsewhere.
- He contrasts Berkshire's posture with "too-big-to-fail": the company deliberately keeps liquidity needs dwarfed by its own cash reserves (over $20 billion held in cash-equivalents even while earning "a pittance") so it never depends on the kindness of strangers.
- The BNSF acquisition added roughly 65,000 new shareholders to Berkshire's rolls (bringing the total past 500,000) and about 257,000 employees company-wide; Buffett frames much of the letter as an orientation for these new owners.
- His stated rationale for BNSF centers on infrastructure economics, not railroading nostalgia: he argues the railroad's characteristics mirror Berkshire's electric utilities, both provide services "essential to the economic well-being of our customers, the communities we serve, and indeed the nation," both require investment far exceeding depreciation for decades, and both depend on a "social compact" with regulators to earn a fair return on huge committed capital.
- He states plainly that "it is inconceivable that our country will realize anything close to its full economic potential without its possessing first-class electricity and railroad systems," and commits Berkshire to helping ensure they exist, the closest the letter comes to an explicit long-run bet on the U.S. economy.
- On the deal's structure, Buffett explains the selling BNSF shareholders were paid $100 per share, about 40% of it in Berkshire stock, which he and Munger believed was worth more than its market price; because Berkshire already owned a large chunk of BNSF bought for cash, only about 30% of the total cost ended up funded with Berkshire shares, deploying $22 billion of cash into a business "we understood and liked for the long term," run by a manager (Matt Rose) they trusted.
- He is candid that the stock portion was a real trade-off, calling the decision "a close one" and warning that had more stock been required, the deal would have made no sense, since issuing undervalued shares in a merger effectively gives away more intrinsic value than it receives.
- Elsewhere he ties the same "buy American" instinct to specific numbers: Berkshire's insurance float grew to $62 billion by year-end 2009 (from $16 million in 1967), funding investments including large new stakes bought during the crisis in American Express, Wells Fargo, Wal-Mart, U.S. Bancorp, and non-traded positions in Dow Chemical, GE, Goldman Sachs, Swiss Re, and Wrigley, all purchased in the eighteen months before the letter.
