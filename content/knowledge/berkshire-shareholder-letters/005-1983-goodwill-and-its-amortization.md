# 1983: Goodwill and Its Amortization

> **Key principle:** When Blue Chip bought See's Candies in 1972 for $17 million over its net tangible assets, accounting rules required that premium be amortized down to zero over 40 years as if it were a wasting asset; Buffett's appendix argues the opposite happened economically, See's real "economic Goodwill," its durable consumer franchise, kept growing precisely because it required so little reinvested capital to keep earning outsized returns.

*Synthesized from Berkshire Hathaway's 1983 Chairman's Letter to shareholders, by Warren E. Buffett.*

## Key lessons

- In the letter proper, Buffett explains that Berkshire's intrinsic business value now considerably exceeds its book value, for two reasons: unrealized gains on securities not required to be marked to market, and, "more important," several owned businesses possess economic Goodwill "far larger than the accounting Goodwill... reflected in book value." He admits his own thinking has "changed drastically" from 35 years earlier, when he was trained to favor tangible assets and distrust businesses whose value rested on goodwill, quoting Keynes: "the difficulty lies not in the new ideas but in escaping from the old ones."
- The appendix, "Goodwill and its Amortization: The Rules and The Realities," opens by defining accounting Goodwill precisely: when a purchase price exceeds the fair value of a business's identifiable net assets, the excess is booked as an intangible asset and, for any purchase made after November 1970, must be amortized in equal annual charges over up to 40 years, charges that are not tax-deductible and therefore hit after-tax income roughly twice as hard as ordinary expenses.
- See's is the worked example: bought in 1972 for $25 million against about $8 million of net tangible assets and roughly $2 million of after-tax earnings, its 25% after-tax return on tangible assets, achieved with no financial leverage, is what Buffett calls the source of its economic Goodwill — "businesses logically are worth far more than net tangible assets when they can be expected to produce earnings on such assets considerably in excess of market rates of return."
- The 1983 merger that folded the remaining 40% of Blue Chip into Berkshire created new accounting Goodwill of $28.4 million for See's and $23.3 million for the Buffalo Evening News on top of the roughly $7.5 million of Goodwill left over from the original 1971 purchase, leaving Berkshire with two different amortization schedules for pieces of the same underlying asset, a result Buffett calls arbitrary but unavoidable under current rules.
- His central argument: by 1983 See's was earning $13 million after tax on about $20 million of net tangible assets, "a performance indicating the existence of economic Goodwill far larger than the total original cost of our accounting Goodwill" — while accounting Goodwill steadily shrank toward zero, economic Goodwill grew "in irregular but very substantial fashion."
- He explains why inflation specifically favors goodwill-rich, asset-light businesses: to keep pace with a doubling price level, both a goodwill-rich business (like See's) and an asset-heavy "mundane" business must roughly double nominal earnings and nominal tangible investment, but the asset-light business needs far less new capital to get there. In his illustration, See's would gain "over $3 of nominal value for each $1 invested" versus the mundane business's dollar-for-dollar gain, "the same result they would have achieved if they had added money to a savings account." He calls this effect "the gift that keeps giving" during inflation, and notes it explains why so many great business fortunes came from intangible-heavy, capital-light franchises like the communications industry, contradicting the old "In Goods We Trust" belief that hard assets are the best inflation hedge.
- Buffett distinguishes true economic Goodwill from "spurious accounting Goodwill," which he suggests renaming "No-Will": when an "overexcited management" overpays for a business, the excess still lands in the Goodwill account and gets amortized on the same 40-year schedule as if the purchase had been sound, disguising a bad deal as a normal asset.
- He closes with a pointed thought experiment: a business earning 25% on $20 of tangible net worth per share might sell for $100, meaning an investor effectively pays $80 per share for Goodwill; should that investor impute a $2-per-share annual amortization charge to "true" earnings the way accounting would for a corporate acquirer? His answer is that amortization should be ignored both in evaluating a business's ongoing economics and in evaluating the wisdom of the price paid to acquire it — "a good business is not always a good purchase — although it's a good place to look for one." At yearend 1983, Berkshire's accounting Goodwill totaled $62 million, which Buffett states plainly is far below the real economic figure.
