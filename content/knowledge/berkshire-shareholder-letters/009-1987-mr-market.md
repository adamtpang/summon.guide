# 1987: Mr. Market

> **Key principle:** Buffett retells Ben Graham's allegory of "Mr. Market," an emotionally unstable business partner who shows up every day offering to buy your stake or sell you his at whatever price his mood dictates. His usefulness lies entirely in his pocketbook, never in his judgment, and the investor who cannot value a business independently of his daily quotes doesn't belong in the game.

*Synthesized from Berkshire Hathaway's 1987 Chairman's Letter to shareholders, by Warren E. Buffett.*

## Key lessons

- Buffett describes Mr. Market as a partner in a private business who appears daily with a price at which he'll buy your interest or sell you his; the more manic-depressive his swings between euphoria and despair, the better for the investor, because transactions are always at your option, never obligatory.
- The line he treats as the whole point of the allegory: Mr. Market "is there to serve you, not to guide you." An investor who isn't confident he can value a business better than Mr. Market's daily quote, he warns, using a poker analogy, is "the patsy" at the table.
- He contrasts Graham's allegory with the era's academic vocabulary of efficient markets, dynamic hedging, and betas, arguing that investment success comes from coupling sound business judgment with insulation from the market's "super-contagious emotions," not from arcane formulas.
- He restates Graham's summary line that in the short run the market is "a voting machine" but in the long run "a weighing machine," and adds that Berkshire lets its holdings' operating results, not their daily or even yearly price quotes, tell it whether an investment is succeeding.
- Buffett names three holdings, Capital Cities/ABC, GEICO, and The Washington Post, that Berkshire would not sell even if the market priced them far above what he believes they're worth, treating them as permanent parts of the business rather than merchandise for Mr. Market to eventually bid away.
- He recounts the 1987 market itself as a live demonstration: the Dow moved only 2.3% for the year despite wild swings, with Mr. Market "on a manic rampage until October" before a sudden crash, driven substantially by "portfolio insurance" strategies that mechanically dumped stock as prices fell, which he compares to a farm owner ordering his land sold off every time a neighbor's plot changes price.
- He rejects the popular conclusion that big institutional money has left small investors with no chance, arguing the opposite: markets whipsawed by irrational professional money give any investor who sticks to his "investment knitting" more chances to act intelligently, not fewer.
- He points to two of Berkshire's own permanent holdings as proof the approach pays off over time: Washington Post stock bought in 1973 at $5.63 a share was earning $10.30 a share after tax by 1987, and GEICO stock bought at an average of $6.67 a share was earning $9.01 a share, cases where he says "Mr. Market has proven to be a mighty good friend."
