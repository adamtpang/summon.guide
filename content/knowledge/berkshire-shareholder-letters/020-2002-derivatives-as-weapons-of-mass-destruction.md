# 2002: Derivatives as Weapons of Mass Destruction

> **Key principle:** In a passage widely cited after the 2008 financial crisis, Buffett writes that he and Charlie Munger view derivatives and the trading activity around them "as time bombs, both for the parties that deal in them and the economic system," because the risk they create is difficult to value, easy to hide, and concentrated among a small number of interlinked dealers.

*Synthesized from Berkshire Hathaway's 2002 Chairman's Letter to shareholders, by Warren E. Buffett.*

## Key lessons

- **What a derivative actually is.** Buffett defines the category broadly: any contract where money changes hands at a future date based on a reference item — interest rates, stock prices, currency values, even, he jokes, the number of twins born in Nebraska in 2020. Contracts can run twenty years or more, and unless collateralized, their ultimate value depends entirely on the counterparty's creditworthiness decades out.
- **Inherited, unwanted, and hard to exit.** General Re Securities, a derivatives dealer Berkshire acquired along with General Re in 1998, is a business Buffett says he and Munger never wanted and tried to sell, but couldn't. He compares winding down a derivatives book to reinsurance: "like Hell, both are easy to enter and almost impossible to exit," since once a long-dated contract is written, you're typically stuck with the resulting liability. Even after ten months of active liquidation, the unit still held 14,384 contracts with 672 counterparties worldwide.
- **Mark-to-model becomes "mark-to-myth."** Because many derivatives have no real market to price against, dealers use internal models instead, and Buffett argues this creates "enormous incentives to cheat," since traders and CEOs are often paid on reported mark-to-market profits before a single dollar has actually changed hands. He cites the energy and electric-utility sectors, where companies booked large derivatives "earnings" that evaporated the moment they tried to convert the underlying receivables into cash.
- **The downgrade spiral.** Buffett describes how derivatives contracts often require a company to post collateral immediately if its credit rating is cut — meaning a downgrade caused by unrelated troubles can trigger a sudden, large cash demand that itself causes further downgrades, a self-reinforcing "spiral that can lead to a corporate meltdown."
- **Daisy-chain, systemic risk.** He draws a direct parallel between derivatives dealers and reinsurers who lay off risk onto others: large uncollateralized receivables build up across many counterparties, and in a crisis, problems that looked diversified can "correlate in a manner undreamed of in more tranquil times" — with no central bank, unlike in banking, assigned to stop the resulting chain reaction.
- **Long-Term Capital Management as proof of concept.** Buffett points to the 1998 near-collapse of LTCM, a firm employing only a few hundred people, whose leveraged derivatives positions were severe enough that the Federal Reserve intervened to organize a rescue for fear of the broader market damage a default would cause — evidence, in his view, that derivative-driven risk concentration is not theoretical.
- **Even sophisticated readers can't assess the risk.** Buffett writes that when he and Munger finish reading the long derivatives footnotes in major banks' financial statements, "the only thing we understand is that we don't understand how much risk the institution is running" — a statement he offers not as false modesty but as a warning about how opaque the instruments had become even to experienced investors.
- **The closing line.** Buffett's own phrase, offered as a summary judgment rather than a hedge: "derivatives are financial weapons of mass destruction, carrying dangers that, while now latent, are potentially lethal." Berkshire's own response was to exit the derivatives-dealing business entirely, even though doing so would take years.
