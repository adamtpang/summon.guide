# COVID and the Four Categories of Value

> **Key principle:** Writing amid a pandemic year of falling operating earnings and a $11 billion write-down, Buffett reframes Berkshire's worth around four flagship holdings, its insurance operation, BNSF, its Apple stake, and BHE, and argues that share buybacks, not big new deals, are the surest way to hand shareholders a larger claim on all of them.

*Synthesized from Berkshire Hathaway's 2020 Chairman's Letter to shareholders, by Warren E. Buffett.*

## Key lessons

- Berkshire earned $42.5 billion under GAAP in 2020, built from four pieces Buffett says pull in different directions: $21.9 billion of operating earnings (down 9% for the year), $4.9 billion of realized capital gains, a $26.7 billion gain from rising unrealized gains in the stock portfolio, and an $11 billion write-down. He tells shareholders to watch operating earnings as the real signal and treat the capital-gains swings as noise that "fluctuate capriciously from year to year."
- Buffett names the write-down his own mistake: Berkshire's 2016 purchase of Precision Castparts (PCC). He says no one misled him, he was "simply too optimistic" about PCC's normalized profit potential, and the miscalculation was exposed when the aerospace industry, PCC's main customer base, weakened. He credits CEO Mark Donegan's management while owning the pricing error as his, calling PCC "far from my first error of that sort" but "a big one."
- Buffett draws Berkshire's distinguishing "family jewels" framework: he says most of the company's value sits in four businesses, three controlled and one a minority stake, all described as jewels. In order: the property/casualty insurance operation run by Ajit Jain (Berkshire's core for 53 years); a near-toss-up second and third place between 100%-owned BNSF and the 5.4% stake in Apple; and fourth, the 91%-owned Berkshire Hathaway Energy (BHE), whose annual earnings grew from $122 million to $3.4 billion over 21 years of ownership. He labels this group the "Big Four."
- Berkshire's insurance float reached $138 billion, described as funds that "do not belong to us, but are nevertheless ours to deploy" in bonds, stocks, or Treasury bills, and which he says has been costless to the company on a cumulative basis. He contrasts this equity-heavy flexibility with typical insurers, who are pushed toward low-yielding bonds by regulatory and credit-rating pressure, noting the 10-year Treasury yield had fallen to 0.93% at yearend, down 94% from 1981's 15.8%.
- Berkshire repurchased about 5% of its own shares in 2020, the equivalent of 80,998 "A" shares for $24.7 billion, which Buffett says increased shareholders' claim on every Berkshire business by 5.2% "without requiring you to so much as touch your wallet." He frames buybacks as conditional: only when the price is right and only when ample funds remain for opportunities or problems, contrasting Berkshire's approach with CEOs who buy back more when prices are high than when they have fallen.
- He illustrates the compounding effect of dual buybacks through Apple: Berkshire's $36 billion cost basis grew to a 5.4% stake (from 5.2% at the 2018 purchase's completion) purely because Apple kept shrinking its own share count through repurchases, even after Berkshire sold a portion of its position in 2020 and collected roughly $11 billion. Layering Berkshire's own buybacks on top, shareholders' indirect claim on Apple's assets and future earnings rose a full 10% between mid-2018 and the letter's writing.
- The equity portfolio table lists fifteen largest holdings by market value at yearend, led by Apple ($120.4 billion market value on a $31.1 billion cost basis), Bank of America ($31.3 billion), Coca-Cola ($21.9 billion), American Express ($18.3 billion), and Moody's ($7.2 billion on a $248 million cost basis), with total equity investments carried at $281.2 billion market value against $108.6 billion of cost. Kraft Heinz is excluded from the ranked list because Berkshire must use equity-method accounting for that control-group stake.
- COVID-19 surfaces directly in the letter's account of Nebraska Furniture Mart, which closed its stores for more than six weeks because of the pandemic yet still set a sales record in 2020, part of a broader "Tale of Two Cities" section tracing See's Candy, GEICO, National Indemnity, NFM, Clayton Homes, and Pilot Travel Centers back to their founders. BNSF also posted a 7% decline in freight volume for the year, offset by CEO Carl Ice and incoming CEO Katie Farmer raising the railroad's profit margin by 2.9 percentage points despite the downturn.
- Buffett notes Berkshire holds more U.S. property, plant and equipment, $154 billion of depreciated cost, than any other American company, ahead of AT&T's $127 billion, and argues asset-heavy businesses like BNSF and BHE can still be excellent investments: the two together earned $4.2 billion in 2011 (Berkshire's first full year owning BNSF) versus $8.3 billion in 2020. He also details BHE's $18 billion, multi-decade project (started 2006, expected complete by 2030) to rebuild Western U.S. transmission lines for renewable power.
- Closing the letter, Buffett describes Berkshire's ownership as sitting in five "buckets": his own founder shares (destined for philanthropy), two institutional buckets (index funds versus active managers), individual shareholders who trade opportunistically, and a fifth bucket of long-term individual holders he says he and Munger feel special kinship with, illustrated by 100-year-old original partnership investor Stan Truhlsen. He restates Berkshire's 1983 principle, "our attitude is partnership," as unchanged.
