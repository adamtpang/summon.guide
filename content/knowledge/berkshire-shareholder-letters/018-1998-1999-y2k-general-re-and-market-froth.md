# 1998-1999: General Re and the Market's Growing Froth

> **Key principle:** Buffett explains why Berkshire, not General Re itself, was the right owner for a reinsurance business — because publicly traded reinsurers get graded on the smoothness of earnings they can't actually control — and closes 1999 warning that equity investors' return expectations had become "wildly optimistic" relative to what GDP growth could plausibly deliver.

*Synthesized from Berkshire Hathaway's 1998 and 1999 Chairman's Letters to shareholders, by Warren E. Buffett.*

## Key lessons

### 1998: General Re and the accounting fights

- **Why General Re needed Berkshire.** Buffett explains that reinsurers exist to absorb the earnings volatility primary insurers want to shed, but a publicly held reinsurer's own stock gets punished by markets and credit-raters for that same volatility — pushing reinsurers toward costly "retrocessions" or rejecting good business simply to smooth results. Berkshire, in contrast, "happily accepts volatility" and has the balance-sheet strength (a "Fort Knox of capital") to retain risk that other reinsurers must lay off, which he frames as the entire economic logic of the $22 billion deal.
- **The stock-option accounting fight.** Buffett attacks the era's accounting rules for letting companies pay employees in options while recording no compensation expense at all, illustrating the absurdity by imagining Berkshire paying its ad agency in ten-year stock options instead of cash and asking whether anyone would then argue no advertising cost had been incurred. He notes Berkshire's own earnings-revision practice: when evaluating an option-issuing company, Charlie Munger and he subtract an estimate of what the options could have been sold for publicly, a downward adjustment that has sometimes changed their portfolio decisions outright.
- **Restructuring charges as earnings manipulation.** Buffett describes the "distortion du jour" of dumping years of legitimate costs into a single disappointing quarter so that future quarters can consistently "beat" lowered expectations — comparing it to a golfer who cards a deliberate 140 in his first round of the season so his real scores can later look consistently better than they are. A survey he cites found 1,369 such special charges announced in 1998 alone, totaling $72.1 billion.
- **GEICO's deliberate front-loading of costs.** GEICO's marketing budget nearly quadrupled between 1995 and 1998 (from $33 million to $143 million), and Buffett explains that GEICO's bonus formula excludes first-year policy costs entirely so that associates aren't penalized for signing profitable long-term customers — a compensation design built to avoid the same short-termism he criticizes elsewhere in corporate America.

### 1999: tech, circle of competence, and market froth

- **Why Berkshire owns no tech stocks.** Buffett states plainly that Berkshire avoids technology companies not because he doubts they'll transform society, but because he and Munger have no reliable way to identify which specific participants hold a truly durable competitive advantage — a direct application of staying "well within our circle of competence" rather than at its "perimeter."
- **The GDP-growth ceiling on stock returns.** Buffett argues that if corporate profits grow roughly with GDP (which he estimates at about 3% real growth plus 2% inflation), valuations placed on American business as a whole can't rationally climb much faster than that pace — meaning the return expectations investors were expressing (a contemporary survey averaged 19% annually) were, in his words, simply unrealistic given the actual number of "birds" available in the economic "bush."
- **A blunt worst year.** Buffett grades his own 1999 capital allocation a "D," citing the underperformance of several major equity holdings, and repeats his standing conviction that relative performance against the S&P 500, not absolute results in any one year, is the correct yardstick for judging Berkshire.
- **Repurchase discipline as a live example.** Buffett lays out the only condition under which a company should buy back its own stock — spare cash plus a price below conservatively estimated intrinsic value — and criticizes the era's fashion of repurchasing merely to "pump or support the stock price" or to offset shares issued through option exercises, warning that overpaying departing shareholders at the expense of those who stay is "not good business for those who stick around."
