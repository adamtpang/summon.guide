# 1979-1980: The Earnings Iceberg

> **Key principle:** Because Berkshire's insurance subsidiaries hold large stakes (often under 20%) in companies like GEICO that retain most of what they earn, accounting rules let Berkshire report only the dividends it receives while ignoring its true share of underlying profit; Buffett argues the value of retained earnings depends on how well they are reinvested, not on the size of the ownership stake or on accounting convention.

*Synthesized from Berkshire Hathaway's 1979 and 1980 Chairman's Letters to shareholders, by Warren E. Buffett.*

## Key lessons

**1979**

- A new accounting rule now requires insurers to carry equity holdings at market value rather than the lower of cost or market; Buffett notes the quirk that Blue Chip Stamps, consolidated into Berkshire but still bound by the old rule, could end up carrying identical securities at a different value than Berkshire's own insurance subsidiaries do.
- He restates his preferred yardstick: operating earnings against beginning shareholders' equity valued at cost (18.6% in 1979, down from 1978) rather than earnings-per-share growth, which he compares to a "dormant savings account" or "stopped clock" that looks like a growth stock purely from compounding.
- Buffett introduces an "investor's misery index": the sum of the inflation rate plus the tax rate an owner pays to convert nominal gains into spendable cash. At a 14% inflation rate, even a 20%-compounding business could leave shareholders with close to zero real, after-tax purchasing-power gain.
- Book value per share had grown from $19.46 in 1964 to $335.85 by yearend 1979, a 20.5% compounded annual rate, but Buffett immediately undercuts the triumphalism: a commentator had pointed out that in gold terms, the 1964 book value bought about the same half-ounce of gold that the 1979 book value buys, "the rub" being that governments can print money but not gold.
- The "Sources of Earnings" table is expanded to include newly-acquired Precision Steel, and Buffett notes textiles and Associated Retail Stores continue producing only modest cash relative to capital employed, contrasted with businesses like network TV stations where extraordinary returns on tangible capital are "almost unavoidable."

**1980**

- Buffett lays out, step by step, how consolidation accounting treats ownership stakes above 50%, between 20% and 50%, and below 20% differently, and explains that Berkshire's concentration in the insurance business puts most of its holdings in that under-20% bucket, where GAAP records only dividends received and ignores retained earnings entirely.
- He states the core thesis plainly: "our economic well-being is determined by their earnings, not their dividends." In 1980 the earnings retained on Berkshire's behalf by partly-owned companies exceeded Berkshire's own total reported operating earnings, meaning "conventional accounting only allows less than half of our earnings iceberg to appear above the surface."
- The value of retained earnings, Buffett argues, has nothing to do with ownership percentage and everything to do with how well the money is reinvested: "if a tree grows in a forest partially owned by us, but we don't record the growth in our financial statements, we still own part of the tree." He states he would rather have earnings retained by a 10%-owned company under a management he didn't hire than earnings he gets full accounting credit for but that get poorly reinvested, even by his own hand.
- Share repurchases get an early, enthusiastic endorsement as one of the best uses of a company's retained earnings: buying back undervalued stock can let "finely-run companies... purchase portions of their own businesses at a price under 50% of that needed to acquire the same earning power through... negotiated acquisition."
- GEICO is the letter's worked example of the iceberg concept: Berkshire's roughly 33% stake (accounted for as under-20% because voting rights were assigned to a third party per regulatory order) generated about $3 million in reported dividend income but Buffett estimated Berkshire's true share of GEICO's earning power at about $20 million annually, meaning "undistributed earnings applicable to this holding alone may amount to 40% of total reported operating earnings of Berkshire."
- Buffett draws an explicit parallel between GEICO's 1976 near-bankruptcy recovery under Jack Byrne and American Express's 1964 salad-oil scandal: both were "one-of-a-kind companies" with a "localized excisable cancer" rather than true turnarounds, distinguishing them from the many failed "turnaround" stories he says almost always end with "the reputation of the business" surviving intact and the manager's reputation not.
- He quantifies his confidence in the strategy concretely: if Berkshire sold its equity stakes and replaced them with tax-free bonds, reported operating earnings would immediately rise by over $30 million a year — a trade he says "tempts us not at all," because real economic value matters more than the number that appears on the income statement.
