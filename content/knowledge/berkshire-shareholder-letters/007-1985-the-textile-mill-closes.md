# 1985: The Textile Mill Closes

> **Key principle:** Berkshire's founding textile business, run by capable and cooperative people for two decades, still could not overcome a commodity industry's structural economics; Buffett concludes that "a good managerial record... is far more a function of what business boat you get into than it is of how effectively you row," and that a chronically losing business deserves energy spent finding the exit, not patching leaks.

*Synthesized from Berkshire Hathaway's 1985 Chairman's Letter to shareholders, by Warren E. Buffett.*

## Key lessons

- In July 1985 Buffett closed Berkshire's textile operations for good. He traces the business back to Buffett Partnership's original 1964 purchase of Berkshire Hathaway, which had a $22 million accounting net worth entirely tied up in textiles, even though nine prior years of $530 million in aggregate sales had produced a net $10 million aggregate loss — "one step forward, two steps back."
- He credits managers Ken Chace and his successor Garry Morrison as fully the equal of managers running Berkshire's more profitable businesses, and credits the New Bedford and Manchester labor force and union leadership with real cooperation: they "did not push for unrealistic wage increases," and even performed well through the liquidation itself. Buffett notes the irony that a more combative union, years earlier, might have forced an earlier, less costly closure.
- He restates and then retracts his own 1978-letter reasoning for staying in textiles (important local employer, honest management, cooperative labor, expectation of "modest cash returns relative to investment"), admitting plainly: "It turned out that I was very wrong about (4)." A moderately profitable 1979 was followed by years of major cash consumption, and by mid-1985 even Buffett could see the trend would not reverse. He states his operating rule going forward: he won't shut a merely sub-normal business just to nudge up the corporate return, but he also won't keep funding a business "once it appears to have unending losses in prospect."
- He acknowledges the delay was his own failure to act on evidence he had: "A recent Business Week article stated that 250 textile mills have closed since 1980. Their owners were not privy to any information that was unknown to me; they simply processed it more objectively." He blames himself for believing what he preferred to believe rather than following Comte's advice that intellect should serve the heart "but not its slave."
- The Burlington Industries comparison is his sharpest illustration of the underlying structural problem: Burlington, 21 years earlier the dominant U.S. textile company with $1.2 billion in sales against Berkshire's $50 million, invested about $3 billion in capital expenditures from 1964-1985 (over $200 per original share) while pursuing a rational strategy of staying in textiles — yet by 1985 its stock, adjusted for a 1965 split, traded barely above its 1964 level even as the CPI more than tripled, meaning shareholders lost roughly two-thirds of their real purchasing power despite disciplined, well-run capital investment.
- He generalizes the lesson with two images: Samuel Johnson's remark that "a horse that can count to ten is a remarkable horse — not a remarkable mathematician" (a textile company that allocates capital brilliantly within its industry is "a remarkable textile company — but not a remarkable business"), and his own advice that "should you find yourself in a chronically-leaking boat, energy devoted to changing vessels is likely to be more productive than energy devoted to patching leaks." He also describes the collective-action trap that made textile capital spending self-defeating: each company's efficiency investment looked rational in isolation, but once competitors matched it, industrywide price cuts absorbed the gains, "just as happens when each person watching a parade decides he can see a little better if he stands on tiptoes."
- The liquidation auction delivers a final, concrete lesson about book value versus real value: textile equipment that originally cost about $13 million and could have been replaced new for $30-50 million sold at auction for gross proceeds of just $163,122 — with net proceeds below zero after sale costs, and looms bought for $5,000 apiece in 1981 selling for scrap at $26 each. Buffett draws the moral directly: "the economic goodwill attributable to two paper routes in Buffalo — or a single See's candy store — considerably exceeds the proceeds we received from this massive collection of tangible assets" that had once employed over 1,000 people.
