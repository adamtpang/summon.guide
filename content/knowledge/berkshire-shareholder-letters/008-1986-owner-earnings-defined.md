# 1986: Owner Earnings Defined

> **Key principle:** Buffett works through a real Berkshire acquisition, Scott Fetzer, to show that two sets of "true" GAAP earnings for the identical business can differ by millions of dollars depending on purchase-price accounting alone. He concludes that the only number worth trusting is what he calls "owner earnings": reported earnings, plus depreciation, depletion, amortization and other non-cash charges, minus the average annual capital expenditure a business needs just to maintain its competitive position and unit volume.

*Synthesized from Berkshire Hathaway's 1986 Chairman's Letter to shareholders, by Warren E. Buffett.*

## Key lessons

- Buffett opens the appendix with a quiz: two abbreviated 1986 income statements, Company "O" and Company "N," with materially different net income ($40.2 million versus $28.6 million). He then reveals both are Scott Fetzer, the only difference being that "N" reflects the purchase-price accounting adjustments required because Berkshire paid $315 million for net assets carried on Scott Fetzer's own books at $172.4 million.
- That $142.6 million premium had to be allocated under GAAP: $37.3 million to write inventory up to current value, $81 million to write up fixed assets and adjust deferred taxes, and a $24.3 million residual booked as Goodwill, each subsequently generating its own non-cash depreciation or amortization charge against reported earnings.
- The two companies, he stresses, are economically identical: same sales, same wages, same taxes, same cash generated for owners. Only the accounting differs, which is why relying on GAAP net income alone to value or compare businesses can be badly misleading.
- From this Buffett derives "owner earnings": (a) reported earnings, plus (b) depreciation, depletion, amortization and other non-cash charges, minus (c) the average annual amount of capitalized expenditure on plant, equipment and working capital that the business requires to fully maintain its long-term competitive position and unit volume.
- He is explicit that (c) is a necessary estimate, not a precise figure, and defends the imprecision by quoting Keynes: "I would rather be vaguely right than precisely wrong." Owner earnings for Company O and Company N work out identical once (c) is applied correctly, which is the sanity check that the concept is sound.
- Buffett singles out "cash flow" (earnings plus depreciation, with no subtraction for necessary capital spending) as a number popularized by Wall Street sales brochures and often used to justify debt loads or stock prices that GAAP earnings alone could not support; he argues it is meaningful only for businesses like a bridge or a long-lived gas field that need almost no reinvestment, and is otherwise a way to "justify the unjustifiable."
- He notes that when a business's true required capital spending (c) exceeds its non-cash charges (b), GAAP earnings systematically overstate the cash actually available to owners, citing the oil industry of the era as a case where companies that spent only at the level of (b) were guaranteeing their own real shrinkage.
- The closing point is a reminder that accounting is an aid to business thinking, never a substitute for it: the accountant's job is to record, and the evaluation of what a business is really worth falls to investors and managers themselves.
