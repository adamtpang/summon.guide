# Tax Reform and a New Accounting Rule

> **Key principle:** Berkshire's headline 2017 net-worth gain was inflated by a one-time tax windfall, and a new GAAP rule is about to make reported earnings noisy and misleading, so Buffett tells shareholders to watch operating earnings, not the bottom line.

*Synthesized from Berkshire Hathaway's 2017 Chairman's Letter to shareholders, by Warren E. Buffett.*

## Key lessons

- Berkshire's net worth grew $65.3 billion in 2017, lifting per-share book value 23%, but Buffett is explicit that this was "far from standard": only $36 billion came from actual Berkshire operations, while $29 billion was a one-time windfall from Congress rewriting the U.S. Tax Code in December 2017.
- He warns shareholders in advance about a new GAAP rule requiring the net change in unrealized gains and losses on Berkshire's stock holdings to flow through reported net income every quarter. With $170 billion in marketable stocks (excluding Kraft Heinz) that can swing $10 billion or more in a quarter, he calls the rule's effect "truly wild and capricious" and says it will make GAAP bottom-line results "useless" for analysis.
- This compounds a problem Buffett says he has long flagged: realized gains and losses, which Berkshire generates based on when selling seems intelligent rather than to manage earnings, already fluctuate randomly and sometimes mask how the underlying portfolio actually performed in a given period.
- His stated countermeasure is to keep publishing financial reports late Friday or early Saturday, giving investors and analysts time to parse the numbers before markets reopen Monday, while still expecting "considerable confusion" among shareholders unfamiliar with the accounting. He directs attention instead to normalized per-share earning power, the metric he and Charlie Munger actually track.
- On the insurance side that funds Berkshire, float grew from $19.4 million when National Indemnity was bought for $8.6 million in 1967 to $114.5 billion by 2017, helped in 2017 by a record $10.2 billion reinsurance premium from AIG covering up to $20 billion of long-tail losses.
- 2017 broke Berkshire's streak of 14 consecutive years of underwriting profit (totaling $28.3 billion pre-tax): three hurricanes (Texas, Florida, Puerto Rico) drove an industry-wide insured loss Buffett estimated near $100 billion, of which Berkshire's share was about $3 billion pre-tax ($2 billion after tax), producing a $3.2 billion pre-tax underwriting loss for the year even though it cut Berkshire's GAAP net worth by less than 1%.
- Major 2017 acquisitions included a 38.6% partnership stake in Pilot Flying J, the Haslam family's roughly $20 billion-revenue travel-center chain (set to rise to 80% by 2023); Clayton Homes' purchase of Oakwood Homes and Harris Doyle, more than doubling its site-built home business; Shaw Industries' acquisition of U.S. Floors; and HomeServices' absorption of Long and Foster, Houlihan Lawrence, and Gloria Nilson, adding 12,300 agents.
- Non-insurance businesses earned $20 billion pre-tax in 2017, with BNSF and Berkshire Hathaway Energy contributing about 44% of that; yearend cash and Treasury Bills stood at $116.0 billion, which Buffett calls "far beyond" the level he and Munger want to hold idle, awaiting a large acquisition.
- Buffett closes out his ten-year, $1 million charity bet against Protégé Partners: an unmanaged S&P 500 index fund returned 125.8% over the decade versus 21.7% to 87.7% for five hedge-fund-of-funds portfolios, which he attributes largely to the roughly 2.5% annual fees layered onto fund-of-funds investors regardless of results ("Performance comes, performance goes. Fees never falter.").
- He recounts that in 2012, seeing the bond's yield fall to 0.88% against much richer equity return prospects, he and Protégé sold the zero-coupon Treasury bonds funding the bet and bought Berkshire shares instead, ultimately delivering Girls Inc. of Omaha $2,222,279 rather than the original $1 million target.
