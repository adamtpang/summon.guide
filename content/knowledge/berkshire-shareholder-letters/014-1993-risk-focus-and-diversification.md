# 1993: Risk, Focus, and Diversification

> **Key principle:** Buffett argues that academic finance's definition of risk, a stock's historical volatility ("beta"), produces absurd conclusions and misses what actually threatens an investor: losing purchasing power. For an investor who truly understands a business, concentrating capital in a handful of the best-understood, highest-conviction opportunities is the lower-risk strategy, not conventional broad diversification.

*Synthesized from Berkshire Hathaway's 1993 Chairman's Letter to shareholders, by Warren E. Buffett.*

## Key lessons

- Buffett states Berkshire's strategy plainly: because the universe of investments large enough to matter shrinks as capital grows, Berkshire settled for needing to be right "only a very few times," even down to "one good idea a year," which precludes following "standard diversification dogma."
- He argues, against conventional wisdom, that concentrating a portfolio "may well decrease risk" because it forces greater intensity of thought and a higher comfort bar before buying, defining risk in plain dictionary terms as "the possibility of loss or injury" rather than as a statistical measure.
- He singles out beta, a stock's calculated historical volatility relative to the market, as the academic proxy for risk, and shows why it produces an absurdity: under beta-based theory, Washington Post stock became "riskier" after it fell sharply in the early 1970s, precisely when Buffett was buying, even though a lower price for an unchanged business is obviously safer, not riskier.
- He describes a "beta purist" as someone willing to assess a company's risk from its price history alone, without knowing what it produces, who its competitors are, or how much debt it carries, whereas his own approach forgoes price history entirely in favor of understanding the underlying business; he says he wouldn't be bothered if markets closed for a year or two after a purchase.
- Buffett's own definition of real investment risk: whether an investment's after-tax proceeds over the intended holding period will preserve at least as much purchasing power as was originally committed, plus a modest return, assessed through five factors: the certainty of the business's long-term economics, the certainty of management's ability and honesty, the certainty management will channel rewards to shareholders, the purchase price, and expected levels of taxation and inflation.
- He argues Coca-Cola and Gillette carry far less long-term business risk than a typical computer company or retailer, citing Coke's roughly 44% global share of soft drinks and Gillette's over 60% share of the blade market by value, brand and distribution strength he describes as "a protective moat around their economic castles," even though their stocks' betas look no different from run-of-the-mill companies with no such advantage.
- He explicitly distinguishes when wide diversification does make sense: for strategies like arbitrage that need many independent bets to smooth outcomes, or for a "know-nothing" investor who doesn't understand individual business economics and should own a broad index and space out purchases over time ("when 'dumb' money acknowledges its limitations, it ceases to be dumb").
- For a "know-something" investor able to find five to ten sensibly priced businesses with real long-term advantages, he says conventional diversification "is apt simply to hurt your results and increase your risk," questioning why anyone would put money into their 20th-favorite idea instead of adding to their best ones, again reaching for Mae West: "Too much of a good thing can be wonderful."
