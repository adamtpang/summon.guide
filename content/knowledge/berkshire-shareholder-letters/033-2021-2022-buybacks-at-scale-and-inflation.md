# Buybacks at Scale and Inflation

> **Key principle:** With attractive whole-company acquisitions scarce and low interest rates pushing up the price of every productive asset, Buffett leans harder on share repurchases as Berkshire's default way to compound shareholder value, while candidly admitting that even Berkshire's vast, diversified collection of businesses offers only imperfect protection against runaway inflation.

*Synthesized from Berkshire Hathaway's 2021 and 2022 Chairman's Letters to shareholders, by Warren E. Buffett.*

## Key lessons

- The 2021 letter lays out Berkshire's three paths to increasing shareholder value, in order of preference: grow the earning power of controlled businesses, buy non-controlling stakes in good public companies, or repurchase Berkshire shares. Buffett says internal growth opportunities are too small relative to Berkshire's size, and that low long-term interest rates push up the price of "all productive investments... stocks, apartments, farms, oil wells, whatever," leaving him finding "little that excites us" in marketable stocks. That backdrop makes repurchases the most reliable remaining lever.
- Berkshire repurchased 9% of the shares outstanding at yearend 2019 over 2020-2021, for a total cost of $51.7 billion, leaving continuing shareholders owning about 10% more of every controlled and part-owned business, from BNSF and GEICO to Coca-Cola and Moody's. An additional $1.2 billion was bought back between yearend 2021 and February 23, 2022. Buffett stresses the appetite is "always price-dependent" and would be value-destroying if Berkshire overpaid for its own stock.
- A quirk specific to Berkshire: because repurchases shrink the share count, they mechanically raise insurance float per share. Buffett reports that figure rose 25% over the two years, from $79,387 to $99,497 per "A" share, calling it a value gain "easily overlooked" but real. He also notes Berkshire's buy-and-hold shareholder base limits repurchase opportunities compared to a stock dominated by short-term speculators, a tradeoff he says he and Munger far prefer anyway.
- In the 2022 letter, Buffett defends repurchases more combatively after buying back 1.2% of outstanding shares that year (with Berkshire's stakes in Apple and American Express also rising passively as those companies bought back their own stock). Using an analogy of three shareholders in a local auto dealership, one of whom sells back to the company at a fair price, he argues value-accretive buybacks harm no one and benefit continuing owners, while overpaying only benefits the seller and "the friendly, but expensive, investment banker." He calls critics of all buybacks "either an economic illiterate or a silver-tongued demagogue."
- Inflation gets comparatively thin but pointed treatment in these two letters. In 2021, Buffett frames insurance as "made to order for Berkshire" partly because its product "will never be obsolete, and sales volume will generally increase along with both economic growth and inflation." In 2022, discussing the decade's $32.3 trillion in federal tax receipts against $43.9 trillion in federal spending, he states plainly that Berkshire's broad collection of businesses "offers some modest protection from runaway inflation, but this attribute is far from perfect."
- Insurance float, the investable cash Berkshire holds but doesn't own, grew from $19 million when Berkshire entered insurance to $147 billion by yearend 2021, then to $164 billion by yearend 2022 after the acquisition of Alleghany Corporation (led by Joe Brandon), a property-casualty insurer Buffett says benefits from Berkshire's financial strength. He credits much of the float's value creation to hiring Ajit Jain in 1986, whose only qualification, by his own account, was "None" — Buffett hired him anyway.
- The 2021 letter's "Big Four" — the insurance cluster, Apple, BNSF, and Berkshire Hathaway Energy — anchor Berkshire's value. Apple's stake rose to 5.55% (from 5.39%) purely through Apple's own buybacks, with Berkshire's "share" of Apple's earnings reaching $5.6 billion against only $785 million in actual dividends received. BNSF posted record earnings of $6 billion on 143 million miles traveled and 535 million tons of cargo; BHE earned a record $4 billion, up more than thirtyfold from $122 million in 2000.
- The 2022 letter's "Secret Sauce" section makes the long-holding-period case with numbers: Berkshire's roughly $1.3 billion purchases each of Coca-Cola (completed 1994) and American Express (completed ~1995) now pay annual dividends of $704 million and $302 million respectively (up from $75 million and $41 million), with the stakes valued at $25 billion and $22 billion each, versus a hypothetical flat $1.3 billion bond investment that would now be an insignificant 0.3% of net worth. Buffett's summary: "The weeds wither away in significance as the flowers bloom."
- Other 2022 specifics: operating earnings hit a record $30.8 billion, while quarterly GAAP earnings swung wildly ($7.0B, -$43.8B, -$2.7B, $18.2B under the mark-to-market rule) — Buffett again insists shareholders judge Berkshire by operating earnings, not the "100% misleading" quarterly GAAP figure. He also details Berkshire's outsized federal tax footprint: $3.3 billion paid in 2021 alone, and $32 billion over the 2012-2021 decade, versus $402 billion and $32.3 trillion collected by the Treasury in those respective periods.
- The 2021 letter closes with the story of Paul Andrews, founder of TTI, who chose to sell his company to Berkshire in 2006 specifically to avoid a competitor's "synergies" layoffs or a leveraged-buyout firm's "exit strategy," telling Buffett Berkshire was "the only guy left." That relationship indirectly led to Berkshire's 2009 acquisition of BNSF, arranged in eleven days after Buffett met BNSF's CEO Matt Rose while visiting Fort Worth for a TTI board presentation.
