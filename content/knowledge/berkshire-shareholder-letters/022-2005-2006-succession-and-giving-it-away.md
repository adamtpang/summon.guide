# Succession Plans and Giving It Away: 2005-2006

> **Key principle:** Berkshire's management succession is treated as already solved, not merely planned, and Buffett's wealth is treated as belonging to society on a fixed clock, not as a dynasty-building tool or a permanent foundation to be run forever by people not yet born.

*Synthesized from Berkshire Hathaway's 2005-2006 Chairman's Letters to shareholders, by Warren E. Buffett.*

## Key lessons

**2005 letter — naming the successors, absorbing a record catastrophe year**
- In a section titled "Management Succession," Buffett tells shareholders the board has already, unanimously, picked which of three reasonably young internal candidates would become CEO if he died that night, and that the directors revisit the choice as circumstances change. He frames the harder open question as what happens if he merely declines rather than dies — and commits that the board would be doing him a favor by telling him to step aside, because every Berkshire share he owns is destined for philanthropy and any delay in a needed transition would shrink what society ultimately receives.
- The investment side of succession is admitted to be less settled: Charlie Munger was once the informal backup for managing the portfolio, and more recently Lou Simpson (GEICO's investment chief) filled that role, but Simpson is only six years younger than Buffett, making him a short-term fallback rather than a long-term answer.
- 2005 was a brutal insurance year: Buffett estimates Berkshire's own Katrina losses at $2.5 billion, with Rita and Wilma adding another $0.9 billion, yet the float across all insurance operations still ended up costless for the year because of strong results elsewhere, especially GEICO. He credits GEICO CEO Tony Nicely by name and cites a specific productivity gain: over two years, GEICO's productivity rose 32% as policy count grew 26% while employment actually fell 4%.
- Five acquisitions were struck in 2005, including Forest River (recreational vehicles), closed within days of a two-page fax from owner Pete Liegl that Buffett says immediately matched Berkshire's stated acquisition criteria, and Business Wire, which came to Berkshire after its president read a Wall Street Journal piece about Berkshire's acquisition style and wrote in unsolicited.

**2006 letter — the pledge to give it away**
- Buffett reports Berkshire's $16.9 billion gain in net worth for 2006, which he calls a record one-year dollar gain for any American business, and notes book value per share grew 18.4% to $70,281, a 21.4% compounded annual rate since 1964.
- He confirms that in the prior year he "arranged for the bulk of my Berkshire holdings to go to five charitable foundations," describing it as carrying out a lifelong plan to eventually direct all his shares to philanthropic purposes, with full details posted separately on Berkshire's website. He states plainly that taxes had nothing to do with the decision or its timing, since his 2006 federal and state income taxes were unchanged from what they would have been without the gifts.
- His will directs that proceeds from any shares he still owns at death be spent within ten years of his estate closing, which he expects to take about three years, putting the full distribution window at roughly 25 years out. His stated reason for not funding a perpetual foundation instead: he wants the money spent by people he already knows to be capable and motivated, arguing that living funders can apply a real-time "market test" to which causes still deserve support better than instructions a decedent wrote decades earlier and can no longer revise.
- He also addresses shareholders worried that foundation stock sales would depress Berkshire's price, estimating the added sales could raise annual turnover by at most three percentage points above its existing 15% rate, still the lowest around, and states that foundation ownership will not influence Berkshire's dividend, repurchase, or share-issuance decisions.
- On the investment side of succession, Buffett describes an actual hiring plan agreed at the October board meeting: bring in one or more younger investment managers with the potential to become chief investment officer, selected less for a strong recent record than for temperament — someone, in his words, "genetically programmed to recognize and avoid serious risks." The same letter recounts the year's headline deal, an 80% purchase of Israeli cutting-tool maker ISCAR for $4 billion, which began with an unsolicited letter from chairman Eitan Wertheimer citing Berkshire as the right home for a family business facing its own generational-transfer question.
