## Key lessons

- He writes about force almost as a living thing: an unseen power lodged in a body by outside pressure, which is "born in violence and dies in liberty" and spends itself fastest when greatest. These passages are descriptive and poetic, not measured physics.
- He poses problems as questions for himself, for example how deep a weight falling forty braccia will bury itself compared with one falling two. He notes cases where simple proportion fails: ten blows of a one-pound hammer will not drive a nail that one blow of a ten-pound hammer drives.
- He draws physics from everyday sights: jumpers swing their fists or carry stones to leap farther, the first chip from an axe blow flies farthest, and an arrow shot forward from a moving ship at the ship's speed seems not to leave it.
- He reasons about the sky: the moon has no light of its own, the stars are hidden by day because sunlit moisture in the air outshines them, and seen from the moon the earth would shine as the moon does for us. He also writes that the earth is not at the center of the sun's orbit or of the universe.
- He calls mechanical science the noblest and most useful, since all moving bodies act through it, and his notes on gears set out which way meshed wheels turn and how tooth spacing should differ between a driving pinion and a driven wheel.
- The Inventions chapter includes his numbered letter offering services: portable bridges, siege methods, cannon, tunnels, armored cars, catapults and warships take up nine points. Architecture, water works, sculpture and painting come last, as what he can do in peacetime. The weapons are described by their intended effect of terror and loss to an enemy.
- He argues flight is possible because air resists an object as much as the object presses on air, pointing to eagles held up by wingbeats and ships driven by sails. He answers the objection that birds are far stronger than men by saying birds keep a large reserve for pursuit and carrying prey and need little force just to stay up.
- He studies how birds turn and rise in circles on the wind, and says the model for a machine should be the bat, whose membrane binds the wing into one airtight surface.
- His test plans are concrete and cautious: build a trial wing of cane, net and pasteboard on a two hundred pound plank and stop if it does not lift; try the machine over water and wear a wineskin as a float; keep the center of gravity low so the craft cannot flip. He also notes a linen tent twelve braccia wide and deep for descending safely from a height, and a starched linen screw that should rise if spun fast. The notebooks do not record any successful flight.
