## Key lessons

- He defines his terms before using them: darkness is the absence of light, and "Shadow is the absence of light" caused by an opaque body blocking the rays. He adds that shadow is the stronger of the two, since it can remove light from a body entirely while light never fully drives shadow from an opaque one.
- He classifies shadows. A primary shadow clings to the unlit side of a body; a derived shadow leaves the body and travels through the air to fall elsewhere. He classifies light as well: direct (sun, window, flame), diffused (cloudy sky), reflected, and light passing through thin materials such as linen or paper.
- He reasons that a derived shadow takes one of three shapes depending on whether the light source is smaller than, equal to, or larger than the object, and names the moon's eclipse as an instance of the converging case.
- He ties brightness to angle: the part of a surface struck most squarely by the rays is brightest, and the parts struck at unequal angles are darker. Much of the chapter consists of lettered diagrams of a window and a ball working this out.
- He advises painters to make a shadow darkest next to the object casting it and let it fade toward its end, and notes that objects seen between light and shadow show the most relief.
- He notes effects of contrast: a dark object looks smaller against a bright ground and a bright object larger against a dark one. His example is buildings that seem to lose height when lightning flashes behind them.
- He explains why a shaded place looks darker than it is from outdoors: the pupil contracts in bright air and needs time to open again. He says a mistake of his own in painting an eye taught him this.
- He observes that the sun's reflection is brighter on small, criss-crossing waves than on large ones, because many small reflections blend and hide the little shadows between them.
- Applied today: his habit of naming, sorting and diagramming a vague phenomenon before trying to use it is a method a reader can borrow for any fuzzy problem.
