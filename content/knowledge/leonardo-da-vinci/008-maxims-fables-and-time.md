## Key lessons

- He states that all knowledge starts from what the senses perceive, and that study forced without appetite harms the memory in the way eating against one's inclination harms health.
- He holds that gaining any knowledge is useful, because the intellect can then discard what is worthless and keep what is good, and nothing can be loved or hated before it is known.
- He counts it the worst misfortune when a person's opinion of his work runs ahead of the work itself, and writes that obstacles give way to firm resolve.
- His fable of the razor tells of a blade that, proud of its shine, hides from the barber's shop to live at ease and comes out months later rusted. He draws the moral himself: minds given to sloth lose their edge.
- His fable of the flint and steel has the flint complain at being struck, then bear it patiently and see fire come of it. He says this is meant for those dismayed at the start of their studies.
- Other fables warn against restlessness and vanity: a flea leaves the dog for a sheep's fleece and starves, a moth flies into a candle and burns, a flame leaves the furnace for a candle and dies with it. Several end in death or ruin, and he tells them plainly.
- He writes a bestiary of virtues and vices drawn from received lore, not observation: the phoenix for constancy, the caterpillar for virtue, the crocodile for hypocrisy, the mole for a lie that dies once exposed. He also records a few jokes at the expense of priests, friars and a merchant.
- He compares patience to clothing against cold: as insults grow, add more of it. He also advises a speaker to watch listeners for yawns and to shorten or change the talk.
- On time he writes that people wrongly complain it passes too fast, when it is sufficient. He likens the present moment to river water that is the last of what has gone and the first of what is coming, and concludes "Life, if well spent, is long." He adds that he never tires of being useful.
