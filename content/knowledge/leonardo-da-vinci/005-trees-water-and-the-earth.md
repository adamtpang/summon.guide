## Key lessons

- He states a rule for trees: at each level, the branches taken together equal the thickness of the trunk below. He then applies the same rule to water, saying the branches of a stream moving at equal speed equal the main channel.
- He describes how leaves are arranged so that each covers the others as little as possible, letting air and sun through and passing dew from leaf to leaf. He lists the patterns in which one leaf sits above another.
- He reports an experiment: he left a gourd one small root, kept it watered, and it still ripened about sixty fruits. He concluded that night dew taken in through the leaves was feeding it.
- For painters he notes how trees change with viewpoint: those between the viewer and the sun show translucent leaf tips and dark shadows, and wind turns up the paler undersides. He tells the painter who does not know these laws to draw everything from nature and not scorn such study as those who work only for money do.
- He develops the old idea of man as a lesser world, calling soil the earth's flesh, rock its bones, springs its blood and the tides its breathing. He uses the analogy to explain how water reaches mountain tops, an account that is not correct by later science.
- He reads river beds and banks as records: stones wear smaller downstream, sediment settles in layers with the heaviest lowest, and mud hardens into stone that preserves leaves and shells in place.
- On sea shells found in high mountains he argues against the explanation by a single deluge: rain would wash things toward the sea, not uphill, and oysters fixed to rock with worm-bored corals could not have been carried in. He concludes the plains were once under salt water, and remarks that "things are much more ancient than letters".
- He lists the properties of water in short statements (it takes on any smell, color or taste, it never rests until it reaches the sea) and writes at length about the ruin floods bring to farms, families and cities, calling them the worst of destructive forces and beyond human defense.
- The Geography chapter also contains drafts of letters to a Devatdar of Syria describing Mount Taurus and a disaster in Armenia. The editor presents them without comment, and the measurements in them are not reliable geography.
