## Key lessons

- He tells the student to draw slowly and attend to which lights are brightest, which shadows darkest and how they blend. Speed, he says, arrives unnoticed once hand and judgment have been trained by care.
- He gives a method for learning by heart: draw the thing from memory, lay a tracing of the model on glass over the attempt, note exactly where they differ, and redraw the wrong parts until they hold. Thin oiled parchment can stand in for glass.
- He says he found it useful to lie in bed in the dark, before sleep or on waking, and go over in imagination the forms he had studied or ideas he had worked out, as a way of fixing them in memory.
- He advises collecting quick notes of real actions in streets, squares and fields using the barest marks (a circle for a head, a line for a limb) and finishing them at home. For faces he suggests learning the types of each feature, noses of ten kinds in profile for instance, and keeping them in a small book.
- On light he prefers the moderate kind: overcast weather or evening makes faces graceful, while strong sun or firelight makes work harsh. His summary is "The medium is best."
- He gives both sides on company. A painter needs solitude to keep the mind clear and should deal with one hard subject fully before turning to the next. Yet he also insists drawing among others is better than alone, because shame, rivalry and the example of better work push a student on.
- He urges the painter to hear everyone's opinion while working, since even non-painters can judge whether a figure looks right. Weigh the criticism, correct the fault if it is fair, and ignore it or argue the point if it is not.
- To judge one's own picture he recommends looking at it reversed in a flat mirror so it seems another's work, taking breaks, and viewing it from a distance, because sitting too close for too long misleads.
- He expects to be dismissed as unlettered and replies that his subjects are settled by experience, which he calls the mistress of the authors his critics quote. He calls those critics reciters of other men's work. He also remarks that small rooms discipline the mind and large ones weaken it.
