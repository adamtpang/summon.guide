## Key lessons

- He records the proportions he attributes to Vitruvius (four fingers to a palm, six palms to a cubit, four cubits to a man's height) and adds his own ratios, for example that outspread arms equal a man's height and that the face divides into thirds. The Human Figures chapter is largely long lists of such measurements keyed to his drawings.
- He analyses balance as a matter of weight around a center of gravity: a man climbing throws weight forward onto the upper foot, a runner leans further the faster he goes, and a seated man cannot rise unless more weight sits in front of his axis than behind it.
- He says a figure's attitude should make its state of mind readable, in the way a deaf person can follow a conversation from the speakers' gestures. He also says a turn of the body should pass by degrees through foot, knee, hip and neck.
- He warns that painters tend to reproduce their own bodily faults in their figures, so each should find his own worst feature and take special care to correct it in his studies.
- He insists the painter know the sinews, muscles and tendons well enough to show only those that work in a given movement. He mocks nudes drawn with every muscle bulging, and separately cautions the anatomically minded painter against making figures look wooden.
- He contrasts ages: small children are slender at the joints and plump between them, and "wherever children have hollows men have prominences". Old men are to be shown slow and bent, and he assigns women modest, closed postures, a convention of his time.
- He explains why drawings can teach anatomy better than one dissection: in a real body the tissues are confused, stained with blood and destroyed as one works. He therefore plans several drawings of each part from different sides, as if the limb were turned in the hand.
- He states that he dissected more than ten human bodies to understand the veins, repeated the work to learn the differences, and lists what the task demands: a strong stomach for nights among corpses, drawing skill, perspective, geometry and patience. He says only lack of time held him back.
- He compares man with other animals (the crosswise gait shared with four-legged animals, the keener senses he found in the lion tribe) and with the earth itself, likening bones to rocks and blood to the waters. He also holds that no human invention is more economical than nature's, where nothing is missing and nothing is superfluous. Some of his physiology is wrong by later knowledge, such as the idea that a mother's wishes mark the unborn child.
