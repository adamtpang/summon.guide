## Key lessons

- He says the painter's mind should work like a mirror that takes on whatever stands before it, and that a walk through the fields should be spent turning attention from one object to the next, picking out the facts worth keeping. He faults painters who walk for relaxation yet register nothing they pass, not even friends who greet them.
- He ranks the eye as the chief means by which the mind takes in nature, with the ear second. In his comparison of the arts he answers the charge that painting is mute poetry by calling poetry "blind painting".
- He tells the painter to carry a small book of tinted paper at all times and note, in a few light strokes, how people behave when they talk, quarrel, laugh or fight, and how bystanders react. The sketches are to be kept, not rubbed out, because the variety of forms is more than memory can hold.
- He argues that sight is fast but takes in only one thing properly at a time, as a page is read word by word and a building climbed step by step. A learner should therefore fix each detail in memory and practice before moving on, and should value diligence above speed.
- He warns that painting declines when painters take other paintings as their standard, pointing to the painters after the Romans who copied one another. Study from natural objects is what he says bears fruit.
- As a check on a finished picture he recommends a flat mirror: compare the reflection of the real scene with the painting, since both show relief on a flat surface.
- He observes that the boundary of a body is a surface with no thickness of its own, so the painter should not ring figures with drawn outlines, least of all small ones.
- He suggests looking at a stained wall or a mix of stones when a scene has to be invented: landscapes, battles, faces and costumes can be found in the confusion and then worked into finished forms, as any word can be heard in the jangle of bells.
- He holds that a painter who can do only one thing well, such as a head or a nude, has achieved little, because painting takes in everything the eye can see. The same chapter gives long, unsparing instructions for depicting a battle (dust, blood, the dead and the dying) and a storm or flood, written as descriptions of what such scenes actually look like.
