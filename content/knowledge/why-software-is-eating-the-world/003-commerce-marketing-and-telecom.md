# Commerce, Marketing, and Telecom Get Rewired

> **Key principle:** Andreessen extends the pattern from entertainment into services businesses built on relationships and reach: Google's dominance of direct marketing, Groupon's software-driven local-deals model, LinkedIn's real-time approach to recruiting, and Skype's software-based calling all represent a software company taking over a large chunk of a market that used to run on people, print, and physical phone networks.

*Synthesized from the commerce, marketing, recruiting, and telecommunications sections of "Why Software Is Eating the World" by Marc Andreessen, originally published in The Wall Street Journal, August 2011, republished at a16z.com.*

## Key lessons

- Andreessen names Google as already the dominant force in direct marketing, a software platform that had displaced large parts of an industry that used to run on print, television buys, and human ad-sales relationships.
- Groupon, only two years old at the time of writing and already generating roughly $700 million a year, is presented alongside Living Social and Foursquare as evidence that local retail marketing, historically one of the hardest categories to digitize, had become software's next target.
- LinkedIn gets singled out for recruiting specifically: by making resumes searchable in real time, it positioned itself to capture a meaningful slice of what Andreessen sizes as a $400 billion global recruiting industry, an industry that had previously run on agencies, referrals, and classified ads.
- On telecom, he frames Skype (acquired by Microsoft for $8.5 billion around the time of writing) as the fastest-growing telecom company in the world despite owning none of the infrastructure a traditional carrier depends on, purely because it replaced the phone call with a piece of software.
- The incumbents' numbers make the threat concrete: CenturyLink, then the third-largest U.S. telecom company with a $20 billion market cap, was seeing access lines shrink by about 7 percent a year and legacy service revenue drop over 11 percent annually, evidence the decline wasn't hypothetical.
- He credits AT&T and Verizon's relative survival specifically to partnering with smartphone makers, above all Apple, rather than resisting the shift, the lesson being that the carriers who survived did so by becoming distribution partners for software platforms, not by defending the old model.
- The common mechanism across marketing, recruiting, and telecom is the same one from the entertainment section: a real-time, searchable, software-native product beats a slower, relationship- or infrastructure-based incumbent even when the incumbent has scale, because software's marginal cost of reaching one more customer keeps falling toward zero.
