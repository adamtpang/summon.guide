# Healthcare, Education, and National Security Are Next

> **Key principle:** Having built the case that software had already eaten media, marketing, telecom, and the physical industries, Andreessen turns forward-looking: healthcare and education are named as the next major targets specifically because they have resisted entrepreneurial change the longest, and national defense is offered as proof the pattern already reaches into the most institutionally conservative sector of all.

*Synthesized from the healthcare, education, defense, and incumbent-technology sections of "Why Software Is Eating the World" by Marc Andreessen, originally published in The Wall Street Journal, August 2011, republished at a16z.com.*

## Key lessons

- Andreessen predicts healthcare and education are due for "fundamental software-based transformation," and frames their resistance to entrepreneurial disruption up to that point not as evidence they're immune, but as evidence of pent-up disruption still to come, and discloses his own firm was already backing aggressive startups in both sectors.
- On national defense, he describes the modern soldier as operating inside a software-integrated intelligence and communications network, points to drones executing airstrikes without putting a pilot at risk, and to large-scale data mining used to detect terrorist plots, presenting the military as already deep into the same shift civilian industries were only beginning.
- Even inside the technology industry itself, he argues no company is safe purely for being a software company already: he names Oracle and Microsoft as incumbents facing real threat from newer software-native challengers, Salesforce in enterprise software and Google's Android in mobile handsets, the same disruption dynamic recurring one layer up.
- He invokes Joseph Schumpeter's idea of creative destruction explicitly to frame all of this as a healthy, not alarming, economic process, and argues incumbents in heavy industries like oil and gas could still benefit by adopting software themselves rather than only being disrupted by outside entrants.
- He ties the whole pattern to a specific claim about American advantage: Google, Amazon, and eBay are offered as proof that the U.S. combination of research universities, a culture that tolerates risk, deep innovation capital, and reliable legal protections for new companies was producing the entrepreneurs positioned to lead this transition globally.
- The section functions as the essay's pivot from description to prediction, everything before this point documents what had already happened by 2011, this section is Andreessen's explicit bet on where the same forces go next, healthcare, education, and further up the technology stack itself.
- Naming national security alongside healthcare and education is a deliberate move to preempt the objection that some sectors are too regulated, too safety-critical, or too institutionally resistant for software to actually take over, if it already reached the military, he argues, no sector gets to claim exemption on those grounds.
