# Software Eats the Physical Industries

> **Key principle:** Andreessen's argument reaches its widest scope here: even sectors that look purely physical, cars, freight, airplanes, oil fields, farms, and payment terminals, are shown to already be run by software underneath, with the physical asset increasingly functioning as the delivery shell for a software-driven advantage rather than the advantage itself.

*Synthesized from the automotive, logistics, energy, agriculture, and financial-services sections of "Why Software Is Eating the World" by Marc Andreessen, originally published in The Wall Street Journal, August 2011, republished at a16z.com.*

## Key lessons

- Automotive: modern cars are described as software systems wrapped in a chassis, engine management, safety systems, entertainment, navigation, and connectivity are all software-controlled already, and Andreessen points to Google's and major manufacturers' push toward electric and autonomous vehicles as the logical end state where a car becomes almost entirely a software product.
- Logistics: Walmart's dominance is attributed to software-driven logistics and distribution rather than retail merchandising alone, and FedEx is described bluntly as a software network with trucks and planes attached to it, the physical fleet exists to execute what the routing software decides.
- Airlines: Andreessen credits software-powered pricing and route optimization, not aircraft or service quality, as the determining factor in airline profitability, a category most people assume runs on hardware and fuel costs.
- Energy and agriculture: he notes that oil and gas companies were early, heavy adopters of supercomputing and data visualization for exploration, and that agriculture increasingly depends on satellite soil analysis and software-driven seed-selection algorithms, industries with no obvious connection to Silicon Valley already running on the same underlying capability.
- Finance: he frames every transaction, from a coffee purchase to a credit-derivative trade, as already running on software, and highlights Square and PayPal as software-first challengers inside payments specifically, noting PayPal alone was generating over $1 billion in quarterly revenue and growing around 31 percent year over year.
- The essay's rhetorical move in this section is to collapse the distinction between "tech company" and "everyone else": if oil exploration, farming, and freight routing already depend on software this heavily, the question for any industry stops being whether software will enter it and becomes only when, and who inside the industry builds the software layer first.
- Taken together with the entertainment and marketing sections, this is the essay's broadest claim: no industry is naturally exempt from software competition purely because its product is physical, the physical product is just the last visible layer on top of a software-run operation.
