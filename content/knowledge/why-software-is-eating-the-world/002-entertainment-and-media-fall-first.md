# Entertainment and Media Fall First

> **Key principle:** Andreessen walks through media category after media category, video, music, gaming, film production, photography, and finds the same pattern each time: a software-first company (Netflix, Apple/Spotify/Pandora, Zynga, Pixar, Shutterfly) captured the growth while the physical-distribution incumbent (Blockbuster, the traditional record labels, Electronic Arts and Nintendo's disc-based model, Kodak) either collapsed or was forced to become a software company itself to survive.

*Synthesized from the entertainment-industry section of "Why Software Is Eating the World" by Marc Andreessen, originally published in The Wall Street Journal, August 2011, republished at a16z.com.*

## Key lessons

- Andreessen opens the industry-by-industry case with retail bookselling as the template for everything that follows: Borders handed its entire online business to Amazon in 2001 because it considered e-commerce "non-strategic," while Amazon used that decade to become a software company that happened to sell books, then pushed the Kindle to further replace the physical product itself.
- Video: he credits Netflix, by subscriber count already the largest video service, with destroying Blockbuster, and notes that legacy cable and satellite providers like Comcast and Time Warner were only staying relevant by building their own "TV Everywhere" software layers on top of their pipes.
- Music: he traces digital revenue climbing from roughly 2 percent of the industry's total in 2004 to around 29 percent by 2010, worth about $4.6 billion, crediting Apple's iTunes alongside newer streaming entrants Spotify and Pandora with the shift.
- Gaming: the sector nearly doubled in five years, from about $30 billion to $60 billion, driven by software-native companies, Zynga (FarmVille) posting roughly $235 million in quarterly revenue with year-over-year growth, and Rovio going from near bankruptcy to an expected $100 million in annual revenue off Angry Birds, while disc-based incumbents Electronic Arts and Nintendo stagnated.
- Film: Andreessen singles out Pixar as, functionally, a software company that became the best movie studio of the era, strong enough that Disney had to acquire it to stay competitive in animation, a direct inversion of who was supposed to be disrupting whom.
- Photography: digital cameras plus cloud services like Shutterfly, Snapfish, and Flickr replaced the film-and-processing business Kodak had built its entire company around, one of the essay's starkest examples because Kodak had no software-company escape route the way Comcast or EA did.
- The through-line he draws across all five categories is that the winning company in each case was not necessarily the biggest incumbent adapting fastest, it was very often a company that started as software-native and only later realized it was in the entertainment business, meaning defenders had to out-build attackers on the attackers' own turf.
