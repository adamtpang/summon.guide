# on-the-shortness-of-life

Original Summon syntheses, written 2026-10-01.

- Source: On the Shortness of Life (De Brevitate Vitae) by Seneca, translated by John W. Basore (Loeb Classical Library, Moral Essays vol. 2, 1932), read on English Wikisource at https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/On_the_shortness_of_life (20 subpages, /Chapter_I to /Chapter_XX, downloaded as raw wikitext with action=raw into _src.txt)
- Read in full: yes
- Parts read: All 20 chapters in full, including the translator's footnotes
- Parts not read: Nothing skipped. The Aubrey Stewart 1889 translation was not used: the suggested Wikisource title 'Of the Shortness of Life' returned Not Found and a Wikisource search did not locate it.

## Caveats

- Translation is Basore (1932), not Stewart (1889); wording of the short quotations follows Basore.
- Basore renders 'occupati' as 'the engrossed'; the notes use that term alongside 'busy'.
- Translator's footnotes say the complaint Seneca attributes to Aristotle in ch. 1 actually belongs to Theophrastus, and that the 'greatest of poets' line in ch. 2 is from an unknown poet. The notes say 'a philosopher' and 'a poet' for that reason.
- Translator's footnotes flag an uncertain text in ch. 7 (loose structure) and at the end of the Scipio passage in ch. 17; note 006 keeps that passage general.
- The Cicero letter to Atticus quoted in ch. 5 is not extant, per the translator's footnote.
- Ch. 20 names the old official Sextus Turannius; the footnote says Tacitus gives the praenomen as Gaius. Note 006 uses only 'Turannius'.
- Seneca's moral judgments (for example calling Drusus's laws evil, or his scorn for wrestling as un-Roman) are reported as his views, not as established fact.
- The final bullet of note 006 is marked 'Applied today' and is the note-writer's application, not Seneca's text.
- _src.txt in this folder is public-domain text (raw wikitext of all 20 chapters with headers and footnote markup).

No source text is stored here. Every lesson is a paraphrase.
