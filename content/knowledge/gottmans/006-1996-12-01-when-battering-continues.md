## Key lessons

- Situation: Jacobson, Gottman, Gortner, Berns and Shortt followed 45 men who batter and their wives for two years, using self-report, physiological and interaction measures, to see when couples split up and when the abuse decreases.
- What they found: at two years 62% of couples were still together and 38% had separated or divorced. Among those still together, 46% of the batterers did not reduce severe violence and 54% did. Only 7% stopped completely, and emotional abuse did not decrease even when physical abuse did. In the authors' words, "only 7% achieved complete desistance".
- Separation was predicted by a combination of six variables (husband's emotional abuse, wife's dissatisfaction, husband's physiological arousal, and the wife defending herself assertively); the abstract reports 90.2% accuracy, an in-sample figure for a small group.
- Related work from the same group found that no wife behavior suppressed a husband's violence once it started (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7806730/).
- Transferable lesson: the guide must not frame violence or coercion as a communication problem the victim can fix. Every advice pattern in Gottman's marriage research assumes both partners are safe. If there is fear, threats or violence, the step is safety planning with an advocate, not repair skills.
